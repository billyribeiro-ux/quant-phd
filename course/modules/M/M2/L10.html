The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

  • Uses: the definite integral and its Darboux sums of Lesson 9, the derivative, the quotient and power rules of Lesson 5, the power rule for positive integer exponents of Lesson 7, Example 1.3, and the mean value theorem of Lesson 6

Lesson 9 defined the integral as the common value of the suprema of lower sums and infima of upper sums, and computing it from sums needs a limit of sums. The fundamental theorem removes the sums. Given Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (assumed there and not proved here), if \(F\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\) with \(F' = f\), and \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then the integral of \(f\) over \([a,b]\) is \(F(b) - F(a)\). Lessons 11 and 12 evaluate their closed-form integrals this way. Two hypotheses are easy to forget, the continuity of \(F\) on the closed interval and the integrability of \(f\), and this lesson states both and checks them on two examples.

1Antiderivatives and the mean value theorem

Definition 1.1 (antiderivative)

Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\). An antiderivative of \(f\) on \(I\) is a function \(F\) that is differentiable on \(I\) with \(F' = f\) at every point of \(I\).

Theorem 1.2 (mean value theorem, Theorem 2.3 of Lesson 6)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(p < q\). If \(\varphi\) is continuous on \([p, q]\) and differentiable on \((p, q)\), then there is a point \(c\) with \(p < c < q\) such that \(\varphi(q) - \varphi(p) = \varphi'(c)\,(q - p)\).

Remark 1.3

Theorem 1.2 is used here in the form stated above. It is the mean value theorem of Lesson 6, whose proof there takes the extreme value theorem as an assumed fact; this lesson relies on that statement and does not reprove it.

2The evaluation form

For a function \(F\) and an interval \([a, b]\), the increment of \(F\) over \([a, b]\) is \(F(b) - F(a)\).

Theorem 2.1 (fundamental theorem of calculus, evaluation form)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(a < b\). Let \(F\) be continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\), with \(F' = f\) on \((a, b)\). Suppose that \(f\) is integrable on \([a, b]\) in the sense of Definition 1.3 of Lesson 9. Then \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a). \] The function \(f\) need not be continuous. The integrability of \(f\) is stated as a hypothesis, and this lesson does not derive it from the differentiability of \(F\).

Proof

Let \(P\) be any partition \(a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b\). On each subinterval, \(F\) is continuous on \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\) and differentiable on \((x_{i-1}, x_i)\), so Theorem 1.2 gives a point \(c_i\) with \(x_{i-1} < c_i < x_i\) and \[ F(x_i) - F(x_{i-1}) = F'(c_i)\,\Delta x_i = f(c_i)\,\Delta x_i. \] Summing over \(i\), the left sides telescope to \(F(b) - F(a)\), so \(F(b) - F(a) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(c_i)\,\Delta x_i\). Since \(c_i\) lies in \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\), the number \(f(c_i)\) lies between the infimum \(m_i\) and the supremum \(M_i\) of \(f\) on that subinterval. Multiplying by \(\Delta x_i\) and summing, \[ L(f, P) \le F(b) - F(a) \le U(f, P). \] This holds for every partition \(P\). Taking the supremum of the left inequality over \(P\) gives \(I_* \le F(b) - F(a)\), and taking the infimum of the right inequality gives \(F(b) - F(a) \le I^*\). Since \(f\) is integrable, \(I_* = I^* = \int_a^b f\), so \(\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)\).

Example 2.2 (a rational antiderivative)

Let \(F(x) = x/(1 + x)\). By the quotient rule, \(F'(x) = \bigl((1+x) - x\bigr)/(1+x)^2 = 1/(1+x)^2\) for \(x > -1\). The function \(f(x) = 1/(1+x)^2\) is nonincreasing on \([0, 1]\), so it is integrable there by Theorem 2.2 of Lesson 9. Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 2.1 gives \[ \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{(1+x)^2} = F(1) - F(0) = \frac{1}{2} - 0 = \frac{1}{2}. \] The quotient \(F\) is continuous on \([0, 1]\), since its denominator does not vanish there.

3Polynomials

Corollary 3.1 (powers)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), for every integer \(k \ge 0\) and every \(0 \le a < b\) (for \(a = b\), both sides are 0 by the convention \(\int_a^a f = 0\) of Lesson 9, Definition 1.3), \(\displaystyle \int_a^b x^k\,dx = \frac{b^{k+1} - a^{k+1}}{k+1}\).

Proof

Let \(F(x) = x^{k+1}/(k+1)\). Then \(F\) is a polynomial, hence continuous, and \(F'(x) = x^k\) by the power rule for positive integer exponents (Lesson 7, Example 1.3, which uses the product rule of Lesson 5 by induction), and for \(k = 0\) directly, since \(F(x) = x\) and \(F' = 1\). For \(k = 0\) the integrand is constant, and for \(k \ge 1\) the function \(x^k\) is nondecreasing on \([0, \infty)\), because for \(0 \le s \le t\) we have \(t^k - s^k = (t - s)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} t^{k-1-j}s^j \ge 0\). So \(x^k\) is integrable on \([a, b]\) by Theorem 2.2 of Lesson 9, and Theorem 2.1 gives the formula.

Example 3.2 (the cubic of Lab 1)

Let \(F(x) = x^3 - 2x\), so \(F' = f = 3x^2 - 2\). The function \(f\) is nondecreasing on \([0, 1]\), because \(f'(x) = 6x \ge 0\) there (Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 6, under Assumption 2.1), so it is integrable by Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2. Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 2.1 gives \(\int_0^1 (3x^2 - 2)\,dx = F(1) - F(0) = (1 - 2) - 0 = -1\), which is the value that the sums of Lab 1, part A bracket.

Lab 1 · Darboux sums bracket the increment, and a central difference checks the derivative

Stated before running: for \(F(x) = x^3 - 2x\) on \([0, 1]\), the upper and lower sums of \(f = F'\) for the uniform partitions with \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256\) parts bracket \(F(1) - F(0) = -1\), and \(U_n - L_n = 3/n\) exactly (part A). For \(F(x) = x/(1+x)\) on \([0, 1]\), the sums bracket \(1/2\) and \(U_n - L_n = 3/(4n)\) for \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128\) (part B). A central difference of \(F\), the quotient \([F(x + h) - F(x - h)]/(2h)\), at \(x = 0.3\) with step \(h = 10^{-6}\) reproduces \(f(0.3)\) to within \(10^{-7}\) (part C).

"""M2 L10 lab 1: the integral of a derivative recovers the increment, checked by Darboux sums.

Part A takes F(x) = x^3 - 2x on [0, 1], so f = F' = 3x^2 - 2, which is nondecreasing on [0, 1]. For uniform
partitions into n parts, the upper and lower sums of f bracket F(1) - F(0) = -1, and U_n - L_n = 3/n exactly. Part B
takes F(x) = x/(1 + x) on [0, 1], so f = F' = 1/(1 + x)^2, which is nonincreasing; its increment is
F(1) - F(0) = 1/2 and U_n - L_n = 3/(4n) exactly. Part C checks by a central difference, in floating point, that
F'(0.3) = f(0.3) for the function of Part B. Exact fractions are used in Parts A and B; standard library only; no
randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

N_VALUES = (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256)
N_VALUES_B = (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128)


def fa(x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """f = F' for F(x) = x^3 - 2x, namely 3x^2 - 2."""
    return 3 * x * x - 2


def antider_a(x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """F(x) = x^3 - 2x."""
    return x**3 - 2 * x


def fb(x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """f = F' for F(x) = x/(1 + x), namely 1/(1 + x)^2."""
    return 1 / (1 + x) ** 2


def antider_b(x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """F(x) = x/(1 + x)."""
    return x / (1 + x)


def part_a() -> None:
    target = antider_a(Fraction(1)) - antider_a(Fraction(0))
    print(f"part A: F(x) = x^3 - 2x, F(1) - F(0) = {target}; f = 3x^2 - 2 is nondecreasing on [0, 1]")
    print("part A:    n         L_n          U_n        U_n - L_n")
    for n in N_VALUES:
        upper = sum((fa(Fraction(i, n)) / n for i in range(1, n + 1)), Fraction(0))
        lower = sum((fa(Fraction(i - 1, n)) / n for i in range(1, n + 1)), Fraction(0))
        if not lower <= target <= upper:
            raise AssertionError(f"F(1) - F(0) is not between L and U at n = {n}")
        if upper - lower != Fraction(3, n):
            raise AssertionError(f"U - L is not 3/n at n = {n}")
        print(f"part A: {n:4d}  {float(lower):12.8f}  {float(upper):12.8f}  {float(upper - lower):12.8f}")
    print("part A: F(1) - F(0) = -1 lies between L_n and U_n at every n, and U_n - L_n = 3/n exactly")


def part_b() -> None:
    target = antider_b(Fraction(1)) - antider_b(Fraction(0))
    print(f"part B: F(x) = x/(1 + x), F(1) - F(0) = {target}; f = 1/(1 + x)^2 is nonincreasing on [0, 1]")
    print("part B:    n         L_n          U_n        U_n - L_n")
    for n in N_VALUES_B:
        upper = sum((fb(Fraction(i - 1, n)) / n for i in range(1, n + 1)), Fraction(0))
        lower = sum((fb(Fraction(i, n)) / n for i in range(1, n + 1)), Fraction(0))
        if not lower <= target <= upper:
            raise AssertionError(f"F(1) - F(0) is not between L and U at n = {n}")
        if upper - lower != Fraction(3, 4 * n):
            raise AssertionError(f"U - L is not 3/(4n) at n = {n}")
        print(f"part B: {n:4d}  {float(lower):12.8f}  {float(upper):12.8f}  {float(upper - lower):12.8f}")
    print("part B: F(1) - F(0) = 1/2 lies between L_n and U_n at every n, and U_n - L_n = 3/(4n) exactly")


def part_c() -> None:
    x = 0.3
    h = 1e-6
    central = ((x + h) / (1 + x + h) - (x - h) / (1 + x - h)) / (2 * h)
    exact = 1 / (1 + x) ** 2
    error = abs(central - exact)
    if error > 1e-7:
        raise AssertionError(f"central difference of F disagrees with f at x = {x}: error {error}")
    print(f"part C: central difference of F at x = 0.3 with h = 1e-6 is {central:.8f}")
    print(f"part C: f(0.3) = 1/(1.3)^2 = {exact:.8f}")
    print(f"part C: absolute difference below 1e-7: {error < 1e-7}")


def main() -> None:
    part_a()
    part_b()
    part_c()

4Two antiderivatives differ by a constant

Proposition 4.1 (constant difference)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(F\) and \(G\) be antiderivatives of the same \(f\) on an open interval \(I\). Then \(F - G\) is constant on \(I\).

Proof

Let \(H = F - G\). Then \(H' = F' - G' = f - f = 0\) on \(I\). For \(p < q\) in \(I\), Theorem 1.2 applied to \(H\) on \([p, q]\) gives \(H(q) - H(p) = H'(c)(q - p) = 0\). So \(H\) takes the same value at any two points of \(I\).

5Where the canon breaks

  • Integrability is a hypothesis. The proof uses it in the last step, where \(I_* = I^*\) turns two inequalities into an equality. Without it, Theorem 2.1 gives only the bracket \(L(f,P) \le F(b) - F(a) \le U(f,P)\) for each partition, and no value for the integral. The integrands this lesson uses are integrable by monotonicity (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2), and it proves integrability for no other class.
  • Continuity of \(F\) is needed on the closed interval. The mean value theorem is applied on each closed subinterval \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\), whose endpoints include the interior partition points \(x_1\) to \(x_{n-1}\), so \(F\) must be continuous at every point of \([a, b]\). A condition only on the open interval would not give \(F(b) - F(a)\): \(F(x) = x\) on \((0, 1)\) with \(F(0) = 5\) and \(F(1) = 1\) is differentiable on \((0,1)\) with \(F' = 1\), and \(\int_0^1 F' = 1\), but \(F(1) - F(0) = -4\).
  • The formula uses differences, not values. Adding a constant to \(F\) changes \(F(b)\) and \(F(a)\) equally, so the integral is unchanged. Example 3.2 and the lab use \(F(1) - F(0)\), not \(F(1)\).

6Misconceptions

  1. “Differentiability of \(F\) on \((a, b)\) is enough; nothing is needed about \(F'\).”

    Theorem 2.1 lists the integrability of \(f = F'\) as a hypothesis, and the proof uses it at the last step, where the sums \(L(f,P)\) and \(U(f,P)\) must have the common value \(I_* = I^*\). The proof therefore needs that step, and the value of the integral is not established without it. The correction is to check integrability, which Lesson 9 does for monotone derivatives.

  2. “The integral \(\int_a^x f\) equals \(F(x)\), with no constant to subtract.”

    The correct value is \(F(x) - F(a)\). Take \(G(x) = x/(1+x) + 5\), which has the same derivative as \(F(x) = x/(1+x)\). Then \(G(1) - G(0) = 1/2\), the same as \(F(1) - F(0)\), while \(G(1) = 11/2\) differs from \(F(1) = 1/2\). The integral depends on \(F\) through the difference \(F(b) - F(a)\), so the added constant 5 cancels, and Proposition 4.1 states that two antiderivatives of the same \(f\) differ by a constant.

  3. “The fundamental theorem applies only to polynomials.”

    Example 2.2 applies Theorem 2.1 to \(F(x) = x/(1+x)\), which is not a polynomial, and Lab 1, part B checks the bracket for it at \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128\). Each hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 holds for it: \(F\) is continuous on \([0, 1]\) and differentiable on \((0, 1)\), and \(f = 1/(1+x)^2\) is nonincreasing on \([0, 1]\), hence integrable (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2).

Mechanics

M2-L10 · A cubic over \([0, 2]\)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, evaluate \(\int_0^2 (3x^2 - 2)\,dx\) by Theorem 2.1, and check that the derivative is nondecreasing on \([0, 2]\).

Solution

An antiderivative is \(F(x) = x^3 - 2x\). Then \(F(2) - F(0) = (8 - 4) - 0 = 4\). The integrand \(3x^2 - 2\) has derivative \(6x \ge 0\) on \([0, 2]\), so it is nondecreasing there (Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 6, under Assumption 2.1) and integrable by Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2. So, under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 2.1 gives the integral 4.

M2-L10 · Powers over \([1, 3]\)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, evaluate \(\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\) by Corollary 3.1.

Solution

Corollary 3.1 with \(k = 2\), \(a = 1\), \(b = 3\) gives \((3^3 - 1^3)/3 = (27 - 1)/3 = 26/3\). The interval lies in \([0, \infty)\), which is where the power is nondecreasing, so the corollary applies.

M2-L10 · A decreasing integrand on \([1, 2]\)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, evaluate \(\int_1^2 dx/(1+x)^2\) using the antiderivative \(F(x) = -1/(1+x)\).

Solution

The derivative of \(-1/(1+x)\) is \(1/(1+x)^2\) by the quotient rule, since \(\frac{d}{dx}(-1) = 0\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}(1+x) = 1\). The function \(1/(1+x)^2\) is decreasing on \([1, 2]\), hence integrable. By Theorem 2.1 (under Assumption 2.1), the integral is \(F(2) - F(1) = -1/3 + 1/2 = 1/6\).

M2-L10 · The power rule from the product rule

Prove that \((x^{j})' = j\,x^{j-1}\) for every integer \(j \ge 1\), by induction, using only the product rule.

Solution

For \(j = 1\), the derivative of \(x\) is 1. Assume \((x^j)' = j x^{j-1}\). Then \((x^{j+1})' = (x \cdot x^j)' = x\,(x^j)' + x^j \cdot 1 = x \cdot j x^{j-1} + x^j = (j+1)x^j\). This is the claim for \(j + 1\).

M2-L10 · The sandwich for one partition

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, and under the hypotheses of Theorem 2.1, prove that \(F(b) - F(a)\) lies between \(L(f,P)\) and \(U(f,P)\) for every partition \(P\), without using the integral.

Solution

On each \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\), Theorem 1.2 gives \(c_i\) in \((x_{i-1}, x_i)\) with \(F(x_i) - F(x_{i-1}) = f(c_i)\Delta x_i\). Since \(m_i \le f(c_i) \le M_i\), summing and telescoping gives \(L(f,P) \le F(b) - F(a) \le U(f,P)\). No integral appears in this argument.

M2-L10 · Constant differences on an interval

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, let \(F\) and \(G\) be antiderivatives of \(f\) on \((a,b)\). Show that \(F(x) - G(x) = F(y) - G(y)\) for all \(x, y\) in \((a,b)\), and deduce that \(F(b) - F(a) = G(b) - G(a)\) when \(F\) and \(G\) are both continuous on \([a,b]\).

Solution

Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Proposition 4.1 gives that \(F - G\) is constant on \((a,b)\). So \(F(x) - G(x) = F(y) - G(y)\) for \(x, y\) in \((a,b)\). Then \(F(b) - G(b)\) and \(F(a) - G(a)\) are equal when \(F\) and \(G\) are continuous on \([a,b]\), because the constant on \((a,b)\) extends to the endpoints by continuity. Subtracting gives \(F(b) - F(a) = G(b) - G(a)\).

M2-L10 · Expected size of the central-difference error

Lab 1, part C uses a central difference with step \(h = 10^{-6}\) at \(x = 0.3\). Estimate its truncation error, using \(F''' (x) = 6/(1+x)^4\) for \(F(x) = x/(1+x)\).

Solution

The truncation error of a central difference is about \(h^2 F'''(x)/6\). At \(x = 0.3\), \(F'''(0.3) = 6/1.3^4 = 6/2.8561 \approx 2.1\), so the truncation error is about \(10^{-12} \cdot 2.1/6 \approx 3.5 \times 10^{-13}\). Each value \(F(0.3 \pm h)\) is near \(0.3/1.3 \approx 0.23\), and the rounding of each value is counted as follows, each error at most half an ulp. Forming \(x \pm h\) (half an ulp \(2.8 \times 10^{-17}\) at 0.3) moves \(F\) by \(1/(1+y)^2 \approx 0.59\) times that, about \(1.6 \times 10^{-17}\). Forming \(1 + x \pm h\) (half an ulp \(1.1 \times 10^{-16}\) at 1.3) moves \(F\) by \(y/(1+y)^2 \approx 0.18\) times that, about \(2.0 \times 10^{-17}\). The quotient (half an ulp \(1.4 \times 10^{-17}\) at 0.23) adds \(1.4 \times 10^{-17}\). Each value therefore carries at most about \(5.0 \times 10^{-17}\), and the difference of two values, divided by \(2h = 2 \times 10^{-6}\), carries rounding error at most about \(5 \times 10^{-11}\) by this count. Both are far below the \(10^{-7}\) tolerance of part C.

M2-L10 · A two-sided bound from the mean value theorem

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, let \(F\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), with \(F' = f\) on \((a,b)\), where \(f\) is defined and nondecreasing on \([a,b]\). Prove that \((b-a)f(a) \le F(b) - F(a) \le (b-a)f(b)\).

Solution

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 1.2 gives a point \(c\) in \((a,b)\) with \(F(b) - F(a) = f(c)(b-a)\). Since \(f\) is nondecreasing and \(a < c < b\), \(f(a) \le f(c) \le f(b)\), and multiplying by \(b - a > 0\) gives the claim. The bound needs no integral.

M2-L10 · Monotone in the upper limit

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, for integer \(k \ge 1\) and \(0 \le a\), show that \(b \mapsto \int_a^b x^k\,dx\) is strictly increasing on \([a, \infty)\).

Solution

By Corollary 3.1 (under Assumption 2.1) the integral equals \((b^{k+1} - a^{k+1})/(k+1)\). For \(b_1 < b_2\) with both at least \(a \ge 0\), the map \(t \mapsto t^{k+1}\) is strictly increasing on \([0, \infty)\), so \(b_2^{k+1} > b_1^{k+1}\), and the difference of the integrals is positive.

M2-L10 · The gap bound for the cubic

For \(f = 3x^2 - 2\) on \([0,1]\) and the uniform partition into \(n\) parts, show from Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2 that \(U_n - L_n \le 3/n\), and that equality holds.

Solution

The mesh is \(1/n\), and \(f(1) - f(0) = 1 - (-2) = 3\). Theorem 2.2 gives \(U_n - L_n \le (1/n)\cdot 3 = 3/n\). For the uniform partition, every subinterval has width \(1/n\), and \(f\) is nondecreasing, so \(U_n - L_n = \sum_i (f(x_i) - f(x_{i-1}))/n = (f(1) - f(0))/n = 3/n\). Equality holds.
