Euclidean Space, Open Sets and Functions of Several Variables

  • Uses: the limit of a sequence (Lesson L01), and the limit of a function at a point (Lesson L03).
  • Introduces: no concept of its own; it prepares the partial derivatives of Lesson L16 and the gradient of Lesson L17
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A sensitivity of a price to two inputs, or a risk measure that depends on a vector of exposures, is a function of several variables. Its behaviour near a point depends on the direction of approach. This lesson fixes the distance on the plane and in higher dimensions, proves the two inequalities that make it behave well, defines open sets and limits of functions of several variables, and shows with a pair of functions that a sample of values at nearby points cannot settle whether a function is continuous.

1Euclidean space and the norm

A point of \(\R^n\) is a list \(x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)\) of \(n\) real numbers. Points are added and multiplied by numbers coordinate by coordinate. The dot product and the length of a point are defined from the coordinates.

Definition 1.1 (dot product and Euclidean norm)

For \(x, y\) in \(\R^n\), the dot product is \(x \cdot y = \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i y_i\), and the Euclidean norm is \(|x| = \sqrt{x \cdot x} = \bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i^2\bigr)^{1/2}\). The distance between \(x\) and \(y\) is \(|x - y|\). The norm is non-negative, and \(|x| = 0\) only for \(x = 0\), because a sum of squares is zero only when each square is zero.

In \(\R^2\) the norm of \((3, 4)\) is \(\sqrt{9 + 16} = 5\), and the distance between \((3, 4)\) and the origin is 5. In this lesson \(|\cdot|\) denotes the Euclidean norm.

2Two inequalities, and open sets

Proposition 2.1 (Cauchy–Schwarz)

For all \(x, y\) in \(\R^n\), \(|x \cdot y| \le |x|\,|y|\).

Proof

If \(y = 0\) both sides are zero. Otherwise \(|y|^2 > 0\). For each real \(t\), \(0 \le |x + t y|^2 = |x|^2 + 2t\,x \cdot y + t^2 |y|^2\). Put \(t = -x \cdot y/|y|^2\). The inequality becomes \(0 \le |x|^2 - 2 (x \cdot y)^2/|y|^2 + (x \cdot y)^2/|y|^2 = |x|^2 - (x \cdot y)^2/|y|^2\). Multiply by \(|y|^2\) and take square roots. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.2 (triangle inequality)

For all \(x, y\) in \(\R^n\), \(|x + y| \le |x| + |y|\).

Proof

Expand \(|x + y|^2 = |x|^2 + 2\,x \cdot y + |y|^2\). By Proposition 2.1, \(x \cdot y \le |x|\,|y|\), so \(|x + y|^2 \le |x|^2 + 2|x|\,|y| + |y|^2 = (|x| + |y|)^2\). Both sides are non-negative, so taking square roots gives the claim. \(\blacksquare\)

Definition 2.3 (open ball, open set)

The open ball of radius \(r > 0\) about \(a\) is \(B(a, r) = \{x \in \R^n : |x - a| < r\}\). A set \(U\) in \(\R^n\) is an open set when every point \(a\) of \(U\) has some radius \(r > 0\) with \(B(a, r) \subseteq U\). The point \(a\) is then an interior point of \(U\).

Example 2.4 (an open ball is open)

Let \(x \in B(a, r)\), so \(|x - a| < r\), and put \(s = r - |x - a| > 0\). If \(|y - x| < s\), then by Proposition 2.2 \(|y - a| \le |y - x| + |x - a| < s + |x - a| = r\). So \(B(x, s) \subseteq B(a, r)\). Each point of the ball has a ball about it inside the ball, which is the definition of an open set.

The closed disc \(\{x : |x| \le 1\}\) is not open: the point \((1, 0)\) lies in it, and every ball about \((1, 0)\) contains points \((1 + t, 0)\) with \(t > 0\), which have norm greater than 1. Problem M2 asks for the check.

3Limits and continuity of functions of several variables

Let \(U\) be a set in \(\R^n\) and \(f : U \to \R\). The limit of \(f\) at a point \(a\) is taken over points \(x\) of \(U\) with \(0 < |x - a|\), so the value of \(f\) at \(a\) itself does not enter. This is the limit of a function at a point as Lesson L03 uses it for one variable, with the distance \(|x - a|\) of this lesson in place of the distance on the line. The statement \(\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L\) means that for each \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(\delta > 0\) with \(|f(x) - L| < \varepsilon\) whenever \(x\) is in \(U\) and \(0 < |x - a| < \delta\).

Continuity at a point \(a\) in \(U\) is the statement that the limit as \(x\) tends to \(a\) exists and equals \(f(a)\). This lesson uses continuity in this sense, and does not study continuity beyond the examples below.

Proposition 3.1 (continuity passes to sequences)

Let \(f : U \to \R\) be continuous at \(a \in U\), and let \((x_k)\) be a sequence in \(U\) with \(x_k \to a\). Then \(f(x_k) \to f(a)\).

Proof

Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Continuity gives \(\delta > 0\) with \(|f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon\) whenever \(x\) is in \(U\) and \(0 < |x - a| < \delta\). The case \(x = a\) also satisfies \(|f(x) - f(a)| = 0 < \varepsilon\). So \(|f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon\) whenever \(x\) is in \(U\) and \(|x - a| < \delta\). Since \(x_k \to a\), there is \(N\) with \(|x_k - a| < \delta\) for \(k \ge N\), and then \(|f(x_k) - f(a)| < \varepsilon\) for \(k \ge N\). \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 3.2 (a failing sequence refutes continuity)

Let \(f : U \to \R\) and \(a \in U\). If some sequence \((x_k)\) in \(U\) with \(x_k \to a\) has \(f(x_k)\) not converging to \(f(a)\), then \(f\) is not continuous at \(a\).

Proof

This is the contrapositive of Proposition 3.1. If \(f\) were continuous at \(a\), every sequence \(x_k \to a\) in \(U\) would give \(f(x_k) \to f(a)\), which the hypothesis excludes. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.3 (a continuous function at the origin)

Let \(f(x, y) = x^2 y / (x^2 + y^2)\) for \((x, y) \ne (0, 0)\), and \(f(0, 0) = 0\). Since \(x^2/(x^2 + y^2) \le 1\), we have \(|f(x, y)| \le |y| \le |(x, y)|\) for every point other than the origin, and the bound also holds at the origin. Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), take \(\delta = \varepsilon\). Then \(|(x, y)| < \delta\) implies \(|f(x, y) - f(0, 0)| < \varepsilon\), so \(f\) is continuous at \((0, 0)\).

Example 3.4 (a discontinuous function at the origin)

Let \(g(x, y) = x y / (x^2 + y^2)\) for \((x, y) \ne (0, 0)\), and \(g(0, 0) = 0\). Along the sequence \(x_k = (1/k, 1/k)\), \(g(x_k) = (1/k^2)/(2/k^2) = 1/2\) for every \(k\). But \(x_k \to (0, 0)\) and \(g(0, 0) = 0\). By Corollary 3.2, \(g\) is not continuous at \((0, 0)\). Along the \(x\)-axis the values are \(g(t, 0) = 0\) for \(t \ne 0\), so the limit depends on the direction of approach.

Proposition 3.5 (the largest values on a circle)

Let \(r > 0\) and \(\theta\) be real. Then \(g(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) = \tfrac12 \sin 2\theta\) and \(f(r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta) = r\cos^2\theta\,\sin\theta\). Over all \(\theta\), the largest value of \(|g|\) is \(1/2\), attained at \(\theta = \pi/4\). The largest value of \(|f|\) is \(r \cdot 2/(3\sqrt3)\), attained where \(\sin\theta = 1/\sqrt3\), and \(|f|\) takes the same value where \(\sin\theta = -1/\sqrt3\).

Proof

Substituting \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\) gives \(x^2 + y^2 = r^2\), so \(g = r^2\cos\theta\sin\theta/r^2 = \cos\theta\sin\theta = \tfrac12\sin 2\theta\), and \(f = r^3\cos^2\theta\sin\theta/r^2 = r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\). Since \(|\sin 2\theta| \le 1\) with equality at \(2\theta = \pi/2\), the largest \(|g|\) is \(1/2\). For \(f\), put \(s = \sin\theta \in [-1, 1]\), so \(|\cos^2\theta\sin\theta| = |(1 - s^2)s|\). On \([0, 1]\) the function \((1 - s^2)s\) has derivative \(1 - 3s^2\), which vanishes at \(s = 1/\sqrt3\), and its values are \(0\) at \(s = 0\), \(2/(3\sqrt3)\) at \(s = 1/\sqrt3\), and \(0\) at \(s = 1\). Negative \(s\) gives the same absolute values. So the largest \(|f|\) is \(r \cdot 2/(3\sqrt3)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.6 (no value at the origin repairs \(g\))

For no real number \(c\) is the function \(g\) of Example 3.4, with \(g(0, 0)\) replaced by \(c\), continuous at the origin.

Proof

Suppose \(g\) with value \(c\) at the origin were continuous there. Along the sequence \((1/k, 1/k)\), Proposition 3.1 gives \(g(1/k, 1/k) \to c\). But \(g(1/k, 1/k) = 1/2\) for every \(k\), so \(c = 1/2\). Along the sequence \((1/k, 0)\), with \(g(1/k, 0) = 0\) for every \(k\), Proposition 3.1 gives \(c = 0\). Both cannot hold, so no \(c\) works. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Two functions sampled on circles and along a sequence

Predicted before running, from Examples 3.3 and 3.4 and Proposition 3.5. For \(g\) on the circle of radius \(r\), the point at angle \(\theta\) gives \(g = \cos\theta\sin\theta = \tfrac12\sin 2\theta\), which equals \(1/2\) at \(\theta = \pi/4\). So the largest sampled value is \(0.5\) for every radius, because the 1-degree grid contains \(\pi/4\). For \(f\) the value at radius \(r\) is \(r\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\), whose largest value over all angles is \(r \cdot 2/(3\sqrt{3}) = 0.3849 r\), so the sampled largest value divided by \(r\) should print about 0.3849 at every radius. Along \((1/k, 1/k)\) the values are \(1/2\) for \(g\) and \(1/(2k)\) for \(f\). The sample is finite, so the printed values describe the sampled points only.

"""M2 lab L15: two functions of two variables sampled near the origin, on circles and along a sequence.

Standard library only. The function g(x, y) = x y / (x^2 + y^2) and f(x, y) = x^2 y / (x^2 + y^2) are
set to 0 at the origin. For each radius r = 10^-k the lab samples 360 points on the circle of radius
r at one-degree steps. It prints the largest absolute value of g over those samples, and the largest
absolute value of f over those samples divided by r.
It also evaluates both functions on the sequence (1/n, 1/n). Every value is a sample, so the output
describes the sampled points only. It does not prove or disprove continuity. Nothing is random.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from collections.abc import Callable

Point = tuple[float, float]


def g(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """g(x, y) = x y / (x^2 + y^2) for (x, y) != (0, 0), and g(0, 0) = 0."""
    if x == 0.0 and y == 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return x * y / (x * x + y * y)


def f(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """f(x, y) = x^2 y / (x^2 + y^2) for (x, y) != (0, 0), and f(0, 0) = 0."""
    if x == 0.0 and y == 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return x * x * y / (x * x + y * y)


def circle_points(r: float, count: int = 360) -> list[Point]:
    """count points on the circle of radius r about the origin, at angles 0, 2 pi/count, 4 pi/count, ..."""
    return [(r * math.cos(2.0 * math.pi * k / count), r * math.sin(2.0 * math.pi * k / count)) for k in range(count)]


def circle_max(fn: Callable[[float, float], float], r: float) -> float:
    """The largest absolute value of fn over the sampled points of the circle of radius r."""
    return max(abs(fn(x, y)) for x, y in circle_points(r))


def main() -> None:
    print("radius r, sampled max |g| on circle, sampled max |f| on circle divided by r")
    for k in range(1, 7):
        r = 10.0 ** (-k)
        mg = circle_max(g, r)
        mf = circle_max(f, r)
        print(f"1e-{k:02d} {mg:.6f} {mf / r:.4f}")
    print("sequence (1/n, 1/n): g value, f value")
    for n in (10, 100, 1000, 10000):
        x = 1.0 / n
        print(f"n = {n}: {g(x, x):.6f} {f(x, x):.9f}")
    print(f"g(0, 0) by definition: {g(0.0, 0.0):.1f}")

The sampled maximum of \(|g|\) on each circle is \(0.500000\) for radii from \(10^{-1}\) to \(10^{-6}\). The sampled maximum of \(|f|\) divided by \(r\) is \(0.3849\) at each printed radius. The sampled angles do not change with \(r\), and \(f(rx, ry) = r\,f(x, y)\), so the same value holds at every radius. The largest sampled \(|f|\) on the circle is therefore proportional to \(r\) and tends to zero as \(r\) does. The exact maximum is \(2/(3\sqrt3) = 0.38490018\) (Proposition 3.5), and the sampled value \(0.3849\) agrees with it to four decimals and is not larger. The sequence values are \(0.500000\) for \(g\) at each printed \(n\), and \(f(1/n, 1/n) = 1/(2n)\), printed as \(0.05\) at \(n = 10\) and \(0.00005\) at \(n = 10000\). The lab checks points on a grid of directions, so it establishes these values at the sampled points. It does not show that \(f\) is continuous at the origin, which Example 3.3 proves, and it does not show that \(g\) is discontinuous at the origin, which Example 3.4 proves with the sequence and Corollary 3.2. The printed \(g(0, 0) = 0.0\) is the definition, not a computed limit.

4Where the canon breaks

Two facts about several variables differ from one variable, and a sampled check can mislead.

  • Direction matters. In one variable a limit has two sides. In \(\R^2\) the point can be approached along infinitely many paths. Example 3.4 has the value \(0\) along the \(x\)-axis and \(1/2\) along the diagonal, so no limit exists at the origin, and the distinction is not a matter of rounding.
  • A sample is a finite set. Proposition 3.5 gives the largest value of \(|g|\) and \(|f|\) on each circle exactly. The sampled values agree with it to the printed digits. For \(g\) the grid contains the maximizing angle. For \(f\) the maximizing angle is not on the grid, so the sampled value is a lower bound that agrees with the exact maximum only to four decimals. A sample can show that a value is large, but it cannot show that no larger value exists between the sample points.
  • The value at the point is separate from the limit. The definition in Section 3 excludes \(x = a\), so a function can have a limit at \(a\) that differs from \(f(a)\). Continuity requires the two to agree. Proposition 3.6 shows that no value at the origin makes \(g\) continuous there, because the diagonal and the axis give different limits.

5Misconceptions

  1. “If a function of two variables has the same limit along the \(x\)-axis and along the \(y\)-axis, it is continuous at the origin.”

    Example 3.4 has the value \(0\) along both axes, since \(g(t, 0) = g(0, t) = 0\) for \(t \ne 0\), and \(g\) is not continuous at the origin. The diagonal sequence in Corollary 3.2 is the test that fails. Two axes are two of infinitely many paths.

  2. “A grid of sampled values shows that a function is continuous.”

    Lab 1 samples \(f\) and \(g\) at 360 points on each circle. Its output is a fact about those points, and it cannot decide continuity, because a grid can miss a direction of approach. Example 3.3 proves the continuity of \(f\) with a bound, and Example 3.4 refutes the continuity of \(g\) with a sequence.

  3. “The closed disc \(\{x : |x| \le 1\}\) is open because it contains its interior.”

    The definition of an open set asks for a ball about every point of the set that lies inside it. The point \((1, 0)\) of the closed disc has no such ball, because every ball about it meets points of norm greater than 1. So the closed disc is not open.

Mechanics

M1 · Norm and distance in \(\R^3\)

Find \(|(1, 2, 2)|\) and the distance between \((1, 2, 2)\) and \((0, 0, 1)\).

Solution

\(|(1, 2, 2)| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3\). The difference is \((1, 2, 1)\), with norm \(\sqrt{1 + 4 + 1} = \sqrt6\), so the distance is \(\sqrt{6} \approx 2.449\).

M2 · The closed disc is not open

Show that the point \((1, 0)\) of \(\{x : |x| \le 1\}\) has no ball \(B((1, 0), r)\) contained in the disc, for any \(r > 0\).

Solution

For any \(r > 0\), the point \((1 + r/2, 0)\) lies in \(B((1, 0), r)\), since its distance to \((1, 0)\) is \(r/2 < r\). Its norm is \(1 + r/2 > 1\), so it is not in the closed disc. So \(B((1, 0), r)\) is not contained in the disc, and the disc is not open.

M3 · Triangle inequality for two axis vectors

Check Proposition 2.2 for \(x = (1, 0)\) and \(y = (0, 1)\).

Solution

\(|x + y| = |(1, 1)| = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142\), and \(|x| + |y| = 1 + 1 = 2\). The inequality \(1.4142 \le 2\) holds, and it is strict here because \(x\) and \(y\) are not positive multiples of each other.

Proof

P1 · Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz

Show that \(|x \cdot y| = |x|\,|y|\) when \(y\) is a multiple \(y = c x\) of \(x\), and that the proof of Proposition 2.1 gives this equality case.

Solution

If \(y = cx\), then \(x \cdot y = c|x|^2\), and \(|x|\,|y| = |x|\,|c|\,|x| = |c||x|^2\), so the two sides agree in absolute value. Conversely, suppose \(y \ne 0\) and \(|x \cdot y| = |x|\,|y|\). The proof of Proposition 2.1 then makes \(|x|^2 - (x \cdot y)^2/|y|^2 = 0\), so \(|x + t y|^2 = 0\) at \(t = -x \cdot y/|y|^2\). A squared norm of zero means \(x = -t y\), so \(x\) is a multiple of \(y\).

P2 · The complement of a point is open

Prove that \(\R^n \setminus \{a\}\) is open, for \(a\) in \(\R^n\).

Solution

Let \(x \ne a\), and put \(r = |x - a| > 0\). If \(y\) is in \(B(x, r)\), then \(|y - a| \ge |x - a| - |y - x| > r - r = 0\) by the triangle inequality, so \(y \ne a\). Hence \(B(x, r) \subseteq \R^n \setminus \{a\}\), and every point of the complement has a ball about it inside the complement.

P3 · Continuity of \(|x|^2\)

Let \(h(x) = |x|^2\) on \(\R^n\). Prove that \(h\) is continuous at \(0\) using the definition in Section 3.

Solution

\(h(0) = 0\), and \(|h(x) - h(0)| = |x|^2\). Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), take \(\delta = \sqrt{\varepsilon}\). If \(0 < |x| < \delta\), then \(|h(x) - h(0)| = |x|^2 < \delta^2 = \varepsilon\). So the limit of \(h\) at \(0\) is \(0 = h(0)\), and \(h\) is continuous at \(0\).

Empirical

E1 · The sampled maximum of \(|f|\) on a circle (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints \(0.3849\) for the largest sampled \(|f|\) divided by \(r\). Reproduce the maximum of \(|\cos^2\theta\,\sin\theta|\) over all angles from Proposition 3.5, and say why the sampled value is not larger than it.

Solution

Put \(s = \sin\theta\). Then \(\cos^2\theta\sin\theta = (1 - s^2)s\) for \(s \in [-1, 1]\). The derivative in \(s\) is \(1 - 3s^2\), zero at \(s = 1/\sqrt3\), where the value is \((2/3)(1/\sqrt3) = 2/(3\sqrt3) = 0.3849002\). The endpoints give \(0\), and negative \(s\) gives negative values, so the maximum of the absolute value is \(2/(3\sqrt3)\). A sample of angles takes values of the same function at some angles, so its largest value cannot exceed the exact maximum. The printed \(0.3849\) is the sampled value at 35, 145, 215 and 325 degrees. These four angles give the same absolute value, because \(\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\) is unchanged by \(\theta \mapsto 180^\circ - \theta\) and changes sign under \(\theta \mapsto \theta + 180^\circ\). That sampled value agrees with \(0.3849002\) to four decimals.

E2 · What the sampled values of \(g\) cannot show (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints \(0.500000\) for the largest sampled \(|g|\) on each circle. State what this output shows about \(g\) near the origin, and state what it does not show.

Solution

The output shows that \(g\) takes the value \(1/2\) at the sampled point of angle \(\pi/4\) on every circle, so the largest sampled value does not tend to zero as the radius does. By Corollary 3.2 and the sequence \((1/k, 1/k)\), this is consistent with the discontinuity of Example 3.4. The output does not show that the sampled maximum is the largest value on the circle, because the sample is finite. Proposition 3.5 gives the exact maximum, which is \(1/2\), so in this case the sample attains it. The output also does not establish discontinuity alone: the discontinuity follows from the sequence, not from the printed sample.

E3 · The diagonal sequence (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints \(f(1/n, 1/n)\) as \(0.05\) at \(n = 10\) and \(0.00005\) at \(n = 10000\). Derive both values from the definition of \(f\).

Solution

At \((t, t)\) with \(t = 1/n\), \(f = t^2\cdot t/(t^2 + t^2) = t^3/(2t^2) = t/2\). So \(f(1/n, 1/n) = 1/(2n)\). At \(n = 10\) this is \(0.05\), and at \(n = 10000\) it is \(0.00005\). The printed values agree, and they tend to zero, which is consistent with the continuity of \(f\) proved in Example 3.3.

Research

R1 · A parabola test

Let \(h(x, y) = x^2 y/(x^4 + y^2)\) for \((x, y) \ne (0, 0)\), and \(h(0, 0) = 0\). Show that the limit of \(h\) along every straight line through the origin is \(0\), and find the limit along the parabola \(y = x^2\). What does the answer say about the limit at the origin?

Solution

On the line \(y = mx\) with \(x \ne 0\), \(h = x^2 \cdot m x/(x^4 + m^2 x^2) = m x/(x^2 + m^2)\), which tends to \(0\) as \(x \to 0\) for every \(m\). Lines \(x = 0\) give \(h = 0\). On the parabola \(y = x^2\) with \(x \ne 0\), \(h = x^2 \cdot x^2/(x^4 + x^4) = 1/2\), so the limit along the parabola is \(1/2\). The limits along lines are all zero, but along the parabola the value is \(1/2\), so no limit exists at the origin, and \(h\) is not continuous there. Straight lines do not test every path.

R2 · Is a ball about the origin the only test?

The definition of an open set in Definition 2.3 uses balls. Explain why a set defined by a strict inequality \(\{x : \phi(x) < 0\}\) for a continuous \(\phi\) should be open, and state the hypothesis your argument needs.

Solution

Let \(\phi\) be a real function on \(\R^n\), and let \(a\) satisfy \(\phi(a) < 0\). Put \(\varepsilon = -\phi(a) > 0\). Continuity at \(a\) gives \(\delta > 0\) with \(|\phi(x) - \phi(a)| < \varepsilon\) for \(|x - a| < \delta\), which gives \(\phi(x) < \phi(a) + \varepsilon = 0\) on \(B(a, \delta)\). So \(B(a, \delta)\) lies in the set, and the set is open. The hypothesis the argument uses is continuity of \(\phi\) at each point \(a\) of the set, in the sense of Section 3. Only that point is used at each step, so the argument needs continuity at the points of the set and nowhere else.

R3 · Sampling as evidence

A study samples a continuous function at 10,000 random points near the origin and reports that every sampled value is within \(10^{-3}\) of the value at the origin. State what this proves and what it does not, using Corollary 3.2 and Example 3.4.

Solution

The result is a statement about 10,000 points, and it proves nothing about other points. For a continuous function, the values stay within any chosen tolerance of the value at the origin on some neighborhood, but a sample of 10,000 points does not prove that any particular neighborhood is free of values outside that tolerance. Example 3.4 shows a discontinuous function that is \(0\) along the axis and \(1/2\) along the diagonal, so a random sample that avoids the diagonal can miss the failure. Corollary 3.2 requires a sequence that converges to the point, and a random sample is not such a sequence. The sample therefore establishes the claim at the sampled points and nothing about other points.
