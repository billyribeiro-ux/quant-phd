Mean Value Theorem, Monotonicity and Extrema

  • Uses: the derivative (Lesson 4, Definition 1.1); differentiable implies continuous (Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1); the derivative of a constant (Lesson 4, Problem R3); the product and quotient rules (Lesson 5, §1); the chain rule (Lesson 5, §2)

If a differentiable function has a maximum or a minimum at a point inside an interval, such as a maximal payoff, its derivative vanishes there (§1 proves this and defines the terms). This lesson states what a zero derivative and the sign of a derivative establish, and what they do not. It derives the mean value theorem from a result about extremes that this lesson takes as Assumption 2.1, and from the mean value theorem it derives monotonicity from the sign of the derivative.

1Local extrema and the zero derivative

Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), and let \(c \in I\). The function has a local maximum at \(c\) when there is \(\delta > 0\) with \(f(x) \le f(c)\) for every \(x \in I\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\). A local minimum is defined the same way with \(f(x) \ge f(c)\). A point that is a local maximum or a local minimum is a local extremum. A critical point of a differentiable function is a point \(c\) with \(f'(c) = 0\).

Proposition 1.1 (a differentiable local extremum has zero derivative)

Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), let \(c \in I\), and suppose \(f\) is differentiable at \(c\) and has a local extremum at \(c\). Hypotheses: the derivative exists in the sense of Lesson 4, Definition 1.1, and the extremum is local in the sense above. Then \(f'(c) = 0\).

Proof

Suppose \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\), with \(\delta\) as in the definition. Let \(Q(h) = (f(c+h) - f(c))/h\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta\). For \(0 < h < \delta\), the numerator is at most 0, so \(Q(h) \le 0\). For \(-\delta < h < 0\), the numerator is at most 0 and the denominator is negative, so \(Q(h) \ge 0\). Since \(Q(h) \to f'(c)\) as \(h \to 0\), suppose \(f'(c) > 0\). Then for \(h\) small, \(Q(h) > f'(c)/2 > 0\) by the definition of the limit with tolerance \(f'(c)/2\), which contradicts \(Q(h) \le 0\) for \(0 < h < \delta\). So \(f'(c) \le 0\). The same argument from the other side gives \(f'(c) \ge 0\). Hence \(f'(c) = 0\). A local minimum is handled by the same argument with the inequalities reversed. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.1 gives a necessary condition. It does not say that a zero derivative produces an extremum, and Misconception 1 shows this.

2Rolle's theorem and the mean value theorem

Assumption 2.1 (extreme value theorem, assumed here and not proved)

For every function \(f\) that is continuous at each point of a closed interval \([a, b]\) with \(a < b\), \(f\) attains a maximum value and a minimum value on \([a, b]\). This is a stated assumption of this lesson. No proof is given here, and no grade is given to it. This page does not state which property of the real numbers a proof would use. Every result below that uses it begins its statement with "Assuming Assumption 2.1".

Theorem 2.2 (Rolle's theorem)

Assuming Assumption 2.1, let \(f\) be continuous at each point of \([a, b]\), with \(a < b\), and differentiable at each point of \((a, b)\), with \(f(a) = f(b)\). Hypotheses: the continuity and differentiability are as stated, and Assumption 2.1 applies to \(f\) on \([a, b]\). Then there is \(c\) with \(a < c < b\) and \(f'(c) = 0\).

Proof

If \(f\) is constant on \([a, b]\), every \(c\) in \((a, b)\) works, because the derivative of a constant is 0 (Lesson 4, Problem R3). Otherwise, \(f\) takes a value different from \(f(a)\). Assumption 2.1 gives a maximum value \(M\) and a minimum value \(m\) on \([a, b]\). Since \(f\) is not constant, \(M > m\), and at least one of \(M\) and \(m\) differs from \(f(a)\). Say \(M \ne f(a)\), so \(M\) is attained at some \(c\) with \(f(c) = M\). Because \(f(a) = f(b)\) and \(M \ne f(a)\), \(c\) is neither \(a\) nor \(b\), so \(c \in (a, b)\). Then \(c\) is a local maximum of \(f\) on \((a, b)\), where \(f\) is differentiable. By Proposition 1.1, \(f'(c) = 0\). If instead \(m \ne f(a)\), the same argument applies to the minimum. \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 2.3 (mean value theorem)

Assuming Assumption 2.1, let \(f\) be continuous at each point of \([a, b]\), with \(a < b\), and differentiable at each point of \((a, b)\). Hypotheses: the continuity and differentiability are as in Theorem 2.2 without the condition \(f(a) = f(b)\), and Assumption 2.1 applies to \(f\) on \([a, b]\). Then there is \(c\) with \(a < c < b\) and

\[f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.\]

Proof

Let \(s = (f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)\), and define \(g(x) = f(x) - f(a) - s(x - a)\) on \([a, b]\). The function \(x \mapsto s(x - a)\) is continuous, and it is differentiable with derivative \(s\), by the derivative of a linear function (Lesson 4, Definition 1.1 gives the quotient \(s h/h = s\)). So \(g\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), differentiable on \((a, b)\), with \(g'(x) = f'(x) - s\) there. Also \(g(a) = 0\) and \(g(b) = f(b) - f(a) - s(b - a) = 0\). Theorem 2.2 applied to \(g\) gives \(c\) in \((a, b)\) with \(g'(c) = 0\), that is, \(f'(c) = s\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Monotonicity from the sign of the derivative

A function \(f\) on an interval is nondecreasing when \(f(x) \le f(y)\) for \(x < y\), and increasing when \(f(x) < f(y)\) for \(x < y\). The words nonincreasing and decreasing are defined in the same way with the inequalities reversed.

Corollary 3.1 (sign of the derivative and monotonicity)

Assuming Assumption 2.1, let \(f\) be continuous at each point of \([a, b]\), with \(a < b\), and differentiable at each point of \((a, b)\). Hypotheses: as in Theorem 2.3. (i) If \(f'(x) \ge 0\) on \((a, b)\), then \(f\) is nondecreasing on \([a, b]\). (ii) If \(f'(x) > 0\) on \((a, b)\), then \(f\) is increasing on \([a, b]\). (iii) If \(f'(x) = 0\) on \((a, b)\), then \(f\) is constant on \([a, b]\).

Proof

Let \(x < y\) in \([a, b]\). The function \(f\) is continuous on \([x, y]\) and differentiable on \((x, y)\), which lies in \((a, b)\). Theorem 2.3 gives \(c\) in \((x, y)\) with \(f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x)\), and \(y - x > 0\). In (i), \(f'(c) \ge 0\), so \(f(y) - f(x) \ge 0\). In (ii), \(f'(c) > 0\), so \(f(y) - f(x) > 0\). In (iii), \(f'(c) = 0\), so \(f(y) = f(x)\) for all \(x, y\), and \(f\) is constant. \(\blacksquare\)

Assuming Assumption 2.1, Corollary 3.1 (ii) gives a sufficient condition for increase, not a necessary one. Misconception 2 shows that a strictly increasing function can have a zero derivative at a point.

Lab 1 · The sign of the derivative of x³ − 3x against its monotone intervals

Predicted before running. The derivative is \(3x^2 - 3 = 3(x-1)(x+1)\), which is positive for \(x < -1\) and for \(x > 1\), and negative between them. So \(f\) is increasing on \((-\infty, -1]\) and on \([1, \infty)\), and decreasing on \([-1, 1]\). On the grid from \(-2\) to \(2\) with step \(1/4\) there are 16 intervals. The 4 intervals in \([-2, -1]\) and the 4 in \([1, 2]\) should increase, and the other 8 should decrease. Also \(f(-1) = 2\) and \(f(1) = -2\).

"""M2 lab L06: the sign of the derivative of f(x) = x^3 - 3x against its monotone intervals, on an exact grid.

Standard library only. No randomness and no network. The derivative is f'(x) = 3x^2 - 3 = 3(x - 1)(x + 1), so f is
increasing on (-infinity, -1] and on [1, infinity), decreasing on [-1, 1], with a local maximum at -1 and a local
minimum at 1. The script uses exact rational numbers (fractions.Fraction) on the grid of x from -2 to 2 with step 1/4,
which has 16 intervals. It checks two things: the sign of f' at each grid point against the predicted sign, and the
direction of f on each grid interval against the sign of f' at its midpoint. It also prints the values f(-1) and f(1)
without a comparison. A grid is a finite check, and the monotone intervals are established on the page by the mean
value argument, under Assumption 2.1.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
from fractions import Fraction


def f(x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The cubic x^3 - 3x for a rational x."""
    return x**3 - 3 * x


def fprime(x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The derivative 3x^2 - 3 of the cubic."""
    return 3 * x * x - 3


def predicted_sign(x: Fraction) -> int:
    """The sign of 3(x - 1)(x + 1) from the factored form: +1, 0 or -1."""
    value = 3 * (x - 1) * (x + 1)
    return (value > 0) - (value < 0)


def grid(lo: Fraction, hi: Fraction, step: Fraction) -> list[Fraction]:
    """The points lo, lo + step, ..., hi, for a positive step that divides hi - lo exactly."""
    if step <= 0 or (hi - lo) % step != 0:
        raise ValueError("the grid needs a positive step that divides hi - lo")
    count = int((hi - lo) / step)
    return [lo + i * step for i in range(count + 1)]


def main() -> None:
    points = grid(Fraction(-2), Fraction(2), Fraction(1, 4))
    signs_agree = all((fprime(x) > 0) - (fprime(x) < 0) == predicted_sign(x) for x in points)
    print(f"sign of f' agrees with 3(x - 1)(x + 1) at all {len(points)} grid points: {signs_agree}")
    increasing = 0
    agreements = 0
    for lo, hi in itertools.pairwise(points):
        rising = f(hi) > f(lo)
        increasing += rising
        midpoint = (lo + hi) / 2
        agreements += rising == (fprime(midpoint) > 0)
    intervals = len(points) - 1
    print(f"grid intervals on which f increases: {increasing} of {intervals}")
    print(f"increase agrees with the sign of f' at the midpoint on {agreements} of {intervals} intervals")
    print(f"f(-1) = {f(Fraction(-1))}; f(1) = {f(Fraction(1))}")


main()
sign of f' agrees with 3(x - 1)(x + 1) at all 17 grid points: True
grid intervals on which f increases: 8 of 16
increase agrees with the sign of f' at the midpoint on 16 of 16 intervals
f(-1) = 2; f(1) = -2

The grid values are exact rational numbers, and the signs and comparisons are exact too. The first line checks the sign of \(f'\) at all 17 grid points. The second and third lines compare the direction of \(f\) on each of the 16 intervals with the sign of \(f'\) at its midpoint. The midpoints are odd multiples of \(1/8\), so none is \(\pm 1\), and the sign is nonzero at each midpoint. The count is a finite check on one grid. Assuming Assumption 2.1, Corollary 3.1 gives the monotone behaviour on each subinterval where \(f'\) has a fixed sign. C Basis: Lab 1.

4Where the canon breaks

Two dropped hypotheses break Rolle's conclusion, and a third case shows that the converse of Proposition 1.1 fails.

  • Continuity on the closed interval is needed for Rolle's theorem. Let \(f(x) = x\) on \([0, 1)\) and \(f(1) = 0\). Then \(f(0) = f(1) = 0\), but \(f\) is not continuous at 1, because its left-hand limit there is 1. Its derivative on \((0, 1)\) is 1 and never 0, so Rolle's conclusion fails. Problem R1 gives the full check.
  • Differentiability on the open interval is needed for Rolle's theorem. Let \(f(x) = |x|\) on \([-1, 1]\). Then \(f(-1) = f(1) = 1\), and \(f\) is continuous. Where \(f\) is differentiable, \(f'(c) = \pm 1\), and \(f\) is not differentiable at \(c = 0\). So no point of \((-1, 1)\) has \(f'(c) = 0\), and Rolle's conclusion fails.
  • A zero derivative at a point is not an extremum. For \(x^3\) at 0, \(f'(0) = 0\), yet \(f\) has no local extremum there. Misconception 1 gives the argument.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A zero derivative at a point produces a local extremum there.”

    Let \(f(x) = x^3\). Then \(f'(0) = 0\). But \(f(x) = x^3 > 0 = f(0)\) for every \(x > 0\), and \(f(x) < 0 = f(0)\) for \(x < 0\), so 0 is not a local extremum. Proposition 1.1 gives a necessary condition, and this example shows that it is not sufficient.

  2. “A strictly increasing function must have a positive derivative everywhere.”

    For \(f(x) = x^3\), \(f\) is strictly increasing on \(\R\). For \(x < y\), \(y^3 - x^3 = (y - x)(y^2 + xy + x^2)\), and \(y^2 + xy + x^2 = (x + y/2)^2 + 3y^2/4\) is positive, because \(x < y\) excludes \(x = y = 0\). So \(x^3 < y^3\). Its derivative at 0 is 0. Assuming Assumption 2.1, Corollary 3.1 (ii) asks for a positive derivative and gives a sufficient condition, and the cubic shows it is not necessary.

  3. “The point \(c\) of the mean value theorem is the midpoint of \([a, b]\).”

    Take \(f(x) = x^3\) on \([0, 1]\). The slope is \((1 - 0)/1 = 1\), so \(3c^2 = 1\) and \(c = 1/\sqrt{3} \approx 0.577\), which is not the midpoint \(0.5\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, Theorem 2.3 gives a point of the mean slope, and the point computed here need not be central.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · A quadratic and its minimum

Let \(f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 1\). Find the critical point, and, under Assumption 2.1, the intervals on which \(f\) is decreasing and increasing.

Solution

We have \(f'(x) = 2x - 4\), which is zero only at \(x = 2\). It is negative for \(x < 2\) and positive for \(x > 2\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, Corollary 3.1 (ii) applied to \(-f\) on each \([x, 2]\) with \(x < 2\), and Corollary 3.1 (ii) applied to \(f\) on each \([2, y]\) with \(y > 2\), give that \(f\) is decreasing on \((-\infty, 2]\) and increasing on \([2, \infty)\). The critical point 2 is the minimum, with \(f(2) = -3\).

M2 · The mean value point for a cube

Assuming Assumption 2.1, for \(f(x) = x^3\) on \([0, 2]\), find the point \(c\) of Theorem 2.3.

Solution

The slope is \((8 - 0)/2 = 4\). We need \(3c^2 = 4\), so \(c = 2/\sqrt{3} \approx 1.1547\), which lies in \((0, 2)\). This is the point the theorem promises.

M3 · Monotone on a grid interval

For \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\), decide from its derivative, under Assumption 2.1, whether \(f\) is increasing on \([-2, -1]\), and check with the values at the ends.

Solution

On \((-2, -1)\), \(f'(x) = 3(x-1)(x+1)\) has both factors negative, so \(f'(x) > 0\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, by Corollary 3.1 (ii), \(f\) is increasing on \([-2, -1]\). The end values are consistent with this: \(f(-2) = -2 < 2 = f(-1)\).

Proof

P1 · Constant functions

Assuming Assumption 2.1, prove Corollary 3.1 (iii) directly from Theorem 2.3, without citing (i) or (ii).

Solution

Let \(x < y\) in \([a, b]\). By Theorem 2.3 there is \(c\) in \((x, y)\) with \(f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x)\). Since \(f'(c) = 0\) by hypothesis, \(f(y) - f(x) = 0\). So \(f(x) = f(y)\) for every pair, and \(f\) is constant.

P2 · Injectivity from a nonzero derivative

Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\), with \(f'(x) \ne 0\) for every \(x\) in \((a, b)\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, prove that \(f\) is one-to-one on \([a, b]\), meaning that \(f(x) = f(y)\) forces \(x = y\).

Solution

Suppose \(f(x) = f(y)\) with \(a \le x < y \le b\) and \(x \ne y\). Then \(f\) is continuous on \([x, y]\) and differentiable on \((x, y)\), and \(f(x) = f(y)\). Rolle's theorem (Theorem 2.2) gives \(c\) in \((x, y) \subset (a, b)\) with \(f'(c) = 0\), which contradicts the hypothesis. So \(f\) is one-to-one.

P3 · A nonpositive derivative

Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\), with \(f'(x) \le 0\) there. Assuming Assumption 2.1, prove that \(f\) is nonincreasing on \([a, b]\).

Solution

Let \(g = -f\). Then \(g\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), differentiable on \((a, b)\), and \(g'(x) = -f'(x) \ge 0\). By Corollary 3.1 (i), \(g\) is nondecreasing. For \(x < y\), \(-f(x) \le -f(y)\), so \(f(y) \le f(x)\), which means \(f\) is nonincreasing.

Empirical

E1 · Counting the increasing intervals (from Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints 8 of 16 grid intervals on which \(f\) increases. Count them from the monotone intervals, and say which grid intervals they are.

Solution

The increasing intervals of the cubic are \((-\infty, -1]\) and \([1, \infty)\). On the grid from \(-2\) to \(2\) with step \(1/4\), the intervals inside \([-2, -1]\) are four, and those inside \([1, 2]\) are four. Together that is 8, which agrees with the printed count.

E2 · The end values (from Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints \(f(-1) = 2\) and \(f(1) = -2\). Check both from \(x^3 - 3x\), and say what they are in terms of the extrema.

Solution

We have \(f(-1) = -1 + 3 = 2\) and \(f(1) = 1 - 3 = -2\). The point \(-1\) is where \(f\) changes from increasing to decreasing, so it is a local maximum with value 2. The point 1 is where \(f\) changes from decreasing to increasing, so it is a local minimum with value \(-2\).

E3 · Why every midpoint has a nonzero sign (from Lab 1)

Lab 1 reports that the sign of \(f'\) at each interval midpoint agrees with the direction on all 16 intervals. Explain why no midpoint can be a critical point.

Solution

The grid endpoints are multiples of \(1/4\), so each interval's midpoint is an odd multiple of \(1/8\). The critical points are \(\pm 1\), which are even multiples of \(1/8\). So no midpoint equals \(\pm 1\), and \(f'\) is nonzero at each midpoint, which makes the comparison well defined.

Research

R1 · Rolle's conclusion fails without continuity

Let \(f(x) = x\) on \([0, 1)\) and \(f(1) = 0\). Show that \(f(0) = f(1)\), that \(f\) is differentiable on \((0, 1)\) with derivative 1 there, and that no \(c\) in \((0,1)\) has \(f'(c) = 0\). Name the hypothesis of Theorem 2.2 that fails.

Solution

We have \(f(0) = 0 = f(1)\). On \((0, 1)\), \(f(x) = x\), so \(f'(x) = 1\) there, and no \(c\) has derivative 0. The hypothesis that fails is continuity at 1 on \([0, 1]\): the left-hand limit at 1 is 1, while \(f(1) = 0\). So \(f\) is not continuous at the endpoint 1, and Theorem 2.2 does not apply.

R2 · The mean value theorem fails for a kink

Let \(f(x) = |x - 1|\) on \([0, 2]\). Show that the slope of the chord is 0, and that \(f'(c) \ne 0\) at every \(c\) in \((0, 2)\) where \(f\) is differentiable. What hypothesis of Theorem 2.3 fails?

Solution

The chord slope is \((f(2) - f(0))/2 = (1 - 1)/2 = 0\). For \(c\) in \((0, 1)\), \(f(x) = 1 - x\) near \(c\), so \(f'(c) = -1\). For \(c\) in \((1, 2)\), \(f(x) = x - 1\), so \(f'(c) = 1\). At \(c = 1\), \(f\) is not differentiable. So no point of \((0, 2)\) has \(f'(c) = 0\). The hypothesis that fails is differentiability at every point of \((0, 2)\).

R3 · A bounded derivative gives a Lipschitz bound

Let \(f\) be differentiable at every point of \(\R\), with \(|f'(x)| \le M\) for every \(x\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, prove that \(|f(x) - f(y)| \le M|x - y|\) for all \(x, y\). A function with this property is called Lipschitz with constant \(M\), and the name is given here in prose.

Solution

Let \(x < y\). Then \(f\) is continuous on \([x, y]\) (Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1) and differentiable on \((x, y)\). Theorem 2.3 gives \(c\) in \((x, y)\) with \(f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x)\). Then \(|f(y) - f(x)| = |f'(c)|\,(y - x) \le M(y - x)\). The case \(x = y\) is trivial, and the case \(x > y\) follows by exchanging the names.

  1. What does Proposition 1.1 say about a differentiable local extremum at \(c\)?

    Options

  2. Which hypothesis does Rolle's theorem need besides \(f(a) = f(b)\)?

    Options

    Theorem 2.2: continuity on \([a, b]\), differentiability on \((a, b)\), and \(f(a) = f(b)\).

  3. Assuming Assumption 2.1, for \(f(x) = x^3\) on \([0, 2]\), what is the mean value point \(c\), to four decimal places?

    \(3c^2 = 4\), so \(c = 2/\sqrt{3} \approx 1.1547\) (Problem M2).

  4. For \(f(x) = x^3\), is the derivative at 0 positive?

    Options

    \(f'(0) = 0\), and \(x^3\) is positive on one side of 0 and negative on the other, so 0 is not a local extremum (Misconception 1).

  5. Assuming Assumption 2.1, if \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(f'(x) > 0\) on \((a, b)\), what can be said on \([a, b]\)?

    Options

    Corollary 3.1 (ii): if \(f\) is continuous on \([a, b]\) and \(f' > 0\) on \((a, b)\), then \(f\) is increasing on \([a, b]\).

  6. For \(f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 1\), what is the minimum value \(f(2)\)?

    \(f(2) = 4 - 8 + 1 = -3\) (Problem M1).

  7. Is the midpoint of \([0, 1]\) always the point \(c\) of the mean value theorem for \(x^3\)?
