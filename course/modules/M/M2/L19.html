Second-order Taylor expansion and the Hessian matrix

A first-order approximation of a function near a point is a plane, and it cannot see curvature. Risk and sensitivity work asks how an output bends as an input moves, and with two or more inputs the bending has a pure part for each input and a mixed part for each pair. This lesson collects the second partial derivatives into one array (Definition 1.1), proves when its mixed entries can be swapped, and proves the second-order Taylor expansion with a remainder that is small against the square of the step.

1Second partial derivatives and the Hessian

Let \(f\) be a real function of two real variables on an open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2\). Its partial derivatives \(f_x\) and \(f_y\), defined in Lesson L16, are functions on \(U\). Writing \(f_{xy}\) means \(\partial_y(\partial_x f)\), the derivative first in \(x\) and then in \(y\), and \(f_{yx}\) means \(\partial_x(\partial_y f)\). Each partial derivative of a partial derivative is a second partial derivative.

Definition 1.1 (Hessian matrix)

Let \(f\) be twice differentiable at a point \(p\) of \(U\). Its Hessian matrix at \(p\) is the array of second partial derivatives

\[H(p) = \begin{pmatrix} f_{xx}(p) & f_{xy}(p) \\ f_{yx}(p) & f_{yy}(p) \end{pmatrix},\]

where \(f_{xx} = \partial_x(\partial_x f)\) and \(f_{yy} = \partial_y(\partial_y f)\). The Hessian is used here only through its entries and through the quadratic form \(h^{\top} H h\) written out in Theorem 3.1; no other property of arrays is used.

Example 1.2 (a Hessian by hand)

Let \(f(x, y) = x^3 + 2xy^2 - y\). Then \(f_x = 3x^2 + 2y^2\) and \(f_y = 4xy - 1\). Differentiating again, \(f_{xx} = 6x\), \(f_{xy} = \partial_y(3x^2 + 2y^2) = 4y\), \(f_{yx} = \partial_x(4xy - 1) = 4y\), and \(f_{yy} = 4x\). At \(p = (1, 2)\) the Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 8 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\). The two mixed entries agree here, as Theorem 2.1 says they must under its hypotheses.

2When the mixed partials agree

Theorem 2.1 (symmetry of the mixed partials)

Let \(f\) be a real function on an open disc \(N\) centred at \(p = (a, b)\). Assume that \(f_x\), \(f_y\) and \(f_{xy}\) exist at every point of \(N\), and that \(f_{xy}\) is continuous at \(p\). Then \(f_{yx}(p)\) exists and \(f_{yx}(p) = f_{xy}(p)\).

Proof

For \(h, k\) small enough that \((a + h, b + k) \in N\), put \(\Delta(h, k) = f(a+h, b+k) - f(a+h, b) - f(a, b+k) + f(a, b)\).

Step 1 (a double mean value argument). Let \(\varphi(x) = f(x, b+k) - f(x, b)\). Its derivative at \(x\) is \(f_x(x, b+k) - f_x(x, b)\), which exists, so \(\varphi\) is differentiable on the interval between \(a\) and \(a + h\), and \(\Delta = \varphi(a+h) - \varphi(a)\). The mean value theorem gives \(\xi\) between \(a\) and \(a+h\) with \(\Delta = h\,[f_x(\xi, b+k) - f_x(\xi, b)]\). Let \(\psi(y) = f_x(\xi, y)\). Its derivative \(\psi'(y) = f_{xy}(\xi, y)\) exists on \(N\), so the mean value theorem gives \(\eta\) between \(b\) and \(b+k\) with \(\psi(b+k) - \psi(b) = k\,f_{xy}(\xi, \eta)\). Hence

\[\Delta(h, k) = hk\, f_{xy}(\xi, \eta), \qquad |\xi - a| \le |h|,\; |\eta - b| \le |k|.\]

Step 2 (the bound). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). By continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at \(p\) there is \(\delta > 0\), with the disc of radius \(\delta\) inside \(N\), such that \(|f_{xy}(q) - f_{xy}(p)| < \varepsilon\) for \(|q - p| < \delta\). If \(0 < |h| < \delta/2\) and \(0 < |k| < \delta/2\), then \((\xi, \eta)\) lies within distance \(\delta\) of \(p\), so \(\left|\Delta(h,k)/(hk) - f_{xy}(p)\right| < \varepsilon\).

Step 3 (the limit in \(k\) at fixed \(h\)). Fix \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta/2\). Since \(f_y\) exists at \((a+h, b)\) and at \((a, b)\), \(\Delta(h, k)/k = [f(a+h, b+k) - f(a+h, b)]/k - [f(a, b+k) - f(a, b)]/k\) tends to \(f_y(a+h, b) - f_y(a, b)\) as \(k \to 0\). Letting \(k \to 0\) in the bound of Step 2 gives \(\left|\bigl(f_y(a+h, b) - f_y(a, b)\bigr)/h - f_{xy}(p)\right| \le \varepsilon\) for every \(0 < |h| < \delta/2\).

The left side is the difference quotient that defines \(f_{yx}(p) = \partial_x(\partial_y f)(p)\). Since \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary, that limit exists and equals \(f_{xy}(p)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (the hypothesis cannot be dropped)

Let \(f(x, y) = xy(x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)\) for \((x, y) \ne (0, 0)\), and \(f(0, 0) = 0\). Off the origin the quotient is a rational function with a non-vanishing denominator, so \(f_x\), \(f_y\), \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) exist there. At the origin, \(f(0, y) = 0\) for \(y \ne 0\), and for \(y \ne 0\) the expansion \(f(h, y) = hy(h^2 - y^2)/(h^2 + y^2)\) gives \(\lim_{h \to 0} [f(h, y) - f(0, y)]/h = -y\). The same limit is \(-y\) at \(y = 0\), so \(f_x(0, y) = -y\) for every \(y\). Likewise \(f(x, 0) = 0\) and \(f(x, k)/k \to x\) as \(k \to 0\) for \(x \ne 0\), with \(f_y(x, 0) = x\) for every \(x\). Therefore \(f_{xy}(0, 0) = \partial_y(f_x)(0, 0) = -1\) and \(f_{yx}(0, 0) = \partial_x(f_y)(0, 0) = 1\). The mixed partials differ at the origin.

The first partials \(f_x\) and \(f_y\), and the mixed partial \(f_{xy}\), exist on a disc around the origin. Since the conclusion of Theorem 2.1 fails at the origin, its hypotheses must fail there too, and the only one that can fail is the continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at the origin. So \(f_{xy}\) is discontinuous at the origin, and the example shows that the continuity hypothesis is needed.

3The second-order Taylor expansion

Let \(f\) be twice differentiable on an open disc \(N\) centred at \(p\), with gradient \(\nabla f = (f_x, f_y)\) and Hessian \(H\). For a vector \(h = (h_1, h_2)\), write \(\nabla f(p) \cdot h = f_x(p) h_1 + f_y(p) h_2\) and \(h^{\top} H(p) h = \sum_{i,j} h_i H_{ij}(p) h_j\). Below, \(o(s^k)\) denotes a quantity whose quotient by \(s^k\) tends to \(0\) as \(s \to 0\).

Theorem 3.1 (second-order expansion with a remainder)

Let \(f\) be twice differentiable on an open disc \(N\) centred at \(p\), and assume the Hessian \(H\) is continuous at \(p\). For every \(h\) with \(p + h \in N\),

\[f(p + h) = f(p) + \nabla f(p) \cdot h + \tfrac12 h^{\top} H(p) h + R(h), \qquad |R(h)| \le 2\,|h|^2\,\omega(|h|),\]

where \(\omega(r) = \sup\{\max_{i,j} |H_{ij}(p+q) - H_{ij}(p)| : |q| \le r\}\). Since \(\omega(r) \to 0\) as \(r \to 0\), \(R(h)/|h|^2 \to 0\).

Proof

Fix \(h\) and put \(g(t) = f(p + th)\) for \(t\) in an interval containing \([0, 1]\). By the chain rule \(g'(t) = \nabla f(p+th) \cdot h\) and \(g''(t) = h^{\top} H(p+th)\, h\). Put \(\varphi(t) = g(t) - g(0) - t\,g'(0) - \tfrac12 t^2 g''(0)\). Then \(\varphi(0) = 0\), \(\varphi'(0) = 0\), and \(\varphi''(t) = g''(t) - g''(0) = h^{\top}\bigl(H(p+th) - H(p)\bigr) h\).

The mean value theorem applied to \(\varphi\) on \([0, 1]\), and then to \(\varphi'\) on \([0, s_1]\), gives \(s_1 \in (0, 1)\) and \(s_2 \in (0, s_1)\) with \(\varphi(1) = \varphi'(s_1) = s_1\,\varphi''(s_2)\). So \(|\varphi(1)| \le \sup_{0 \le t \le 1} |\varphi''(t)|\).

Each entry of \(H(p+th) - H(p)\) is at most \(\omega(|h|)\) in absolute value when \(|th| \le |h|\), and \(|h^{\top} M h| \le (|h_1| + |h_2|)^2 \max_{ij}|M_{ij}| \le 2|h|^2 \max_{ij}|M_{ij}|\). Hence \(|\varphi''(t)| \le 2|h|^2\omega(|h|)\). Since \(g(1) = f(p+h)\) and \(g'(0) = \nabla f(p)\cdot h\), \(\varphi(1) = R(h)\) and the bound follows. Continuity of \(H\) at \(p\) gives \(\omega(r) \to 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.2 (the expansion along one direction)

Fix a unit vector \(d\) and let \(g(s) = f(p + sd)\). Theorem 3.1 with \(h = sd\) gives \(g(s) = g(0) + g'(0)s + \tfrac12 g''(0)s^2 + o(s^2)\), with \(g'(0) = \nabla f(p) \cdot d\) and \(g''(0) = d^{\top} H(p) d\). The coefficient of \(s^2\) is \(\tfrac12 g''(0) = \tfrac12 d^{\top} H(p)\, d = \tfrac12\bigl(d_1^2 f_{xx}(p) + 2 d_1 d_2 f_{xy}(p) + d_2^2 f_{yy}(p)\bigr)\), so the mixed partial enters it whenever \(d_1 d_2 \ne 0\).

Proposition 3.3 (the next term is cubic)

Let \(g\) be three times differentiable on an interval containing \([0, s]\), with \(g'''\) continuous at \(0\), and put \(E(s) = g(s) - g(0) - g'(0)s - \tfrac12 g''(0)s^2\). Then \(E(s) = \tfrac16 g'''(0)s^3 + o(s^3)\). If \(g'''(0) \ne 0\), then \(E(s)/E(s/2) \to 8\) as \(s \to 0\).

Proof

By the Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder (Lesson L08), \(E(s) = \tfrac16 g'''(\theta s)s^3\) for some \(\theta \in (0, 1)\). Continuity of \(g'''\) at \(0\) gives \(g'''(\theta s) \to g'''(0)\) as \(s \to 0\), which is the first claim. If \(g'''(0) \ne 0\), then \(E(s)/E(s/2) = \dfrac{\tfrac16 g'''(0)s^3 + o(s^3)}{\tfrac1{48} g'''(0)s^3 + o(s^3)} \to 8\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · The gradient, the Hessian and the expansion errors of a SIMULATED function

The function is \(f(x, y) = e^x y + x y^2\), a SIMULATED test function, not market data. A central difference of a function \(u(x, y)\) in the \(x\)-direction, with step \(h > 0\), is \((u(x + h, y) - u(x - h, y))/(2h)\), and the \(y\)-direction is defined in the same way. Its closed-form gradient and Hessian are \((e^x y + y^2,\; e^x + 2xy)\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} e^x y & e^x + 2y \\ e^x + 2y & 2x \end{pmatrix}\). Predicted before running: at the base point \((0.5, 0.25)\) the Hessian entries are \(f_{xx} = e^{0.5}/4 \approx 0.41218\), \(f_{xy} = e^{0.5} + 0.5 \approx 2.14872\) and \(f_{yy} = 1\). Central differences of the closed gradient at step \(10^{-5}\) should reproduce the Hessian to \(10^{-10}\) or better, and the two mixed differences should agree to the same order, because \(f\) is smooth. Along the unit direction \(d = (0.6, -0.8)\), the first-order error should fall by a factor near 4 when the step halves. The second-order error should fall by a factor near 8 if the third derivative \(g'''(0)\) of \(g(s) = f(p + sd)\) is non-zero, by Proposition 3.3. The lab does not compute \(g'''(0)\), so the printed ratios are the only evidence on that point.

"""M2 lab L19: the gradient, the Hessian and the second-order Taylor expansion of a two-variable function.

SIMULATED: the function f(x, y) = exp(x) * y + x * y**2 is a test function chosen for this lab. It is not
market data and it models no security.
Part A checks the closed-form gradient and Hessian of f against central differences, and reports the
symmetry of the finite-difference Hessian. Part B measures the error of the first-order and the second-order
Taylor expansions of f along one fixed unit direction, at step sizes s, s/2, s/4 and s/8, and prints the ratio
of successive errors. Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

Point = tuple[float, float]
Matrix = tuple[tuple[float, float], tuple[float, float]]

BASE: Point = (0.5, 0.25)
DIRECTION: Point = (0.6, -0.8)  # a unit vector: 0.6 * 0.6 + 0.8 * 0.8 = 1
STEPS: tuple[float, ...] = (0.1, 0.05, 0.025, 0.0125)


def f(p: Point) -> float:
    """The test function f(x, y) = exp(x) * y + x * y**2."""
    x, y = p
    return math.exp(x) * y + x * y * y


def gradient_closed(p: Point) -> Point:
    """The closed-form gradient (f_x, f_y) at p."""
    x, y = p
    return (math.exp(x) * y + y * y, math.exp(x) + 2.0 * x * y)


def hessian_closed(p: Point) -> Matrix:
    """The closed-form Hessian [[f_xx, f_xy], [f_yx, f_yy]] at p."""
    x, y = p
    e = math.exp(x)
    return ((e * y, e + 2.0 * y), (e + 2.0 * y, 2.0 * x))


def fd_hessian(p: Point, h: float) -> Matrix:
    """Hessian by central differences of the closed gradient: entry (i, j) differentiates gradient component i along j.

    The step h must be positive; a step of zero or less raises ValueError.
    """
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    rows: list[list[float]] = []
    for i in range(2):
        row: list[float] = []
        for j in range(2):
            up = [p[0], p[1]]
            down = [p[0], p[1]]
            up[j] += h
            down[j] -= h
            plus = gradient_closed((up[0], up[1]))[i]
            minus = gradient_closed((down[0], down[1]))[i]
            row.append((plus - minus) / (2.0 * h))
        rows.append(row)
    return ((rows[0][0], rows[0][1]), (rows[1][0], rows[1][1]))


def along(p: Point, d: Point, s: float) -> Point:
    """The point p + s * d."""
    return (p[0] + s * d[0], p[1] + s * d[1])


def first_order_error(p: Point, d: Point, s: float) -> float:
    """f(p + s d) minus its first-order Taylor polynomial f(p) + s * grad f(p) . d."""
    g = gradient_closed(p)
    return f(along(p, d, s)) - (f(p) + s * (g[0] * d[0] + g[1] * d[1]))


def second_order_error(p: Point, d: Point, s: float) -> float:
    """f(p + s d) minus its second-order Taylor polynomial f(p) + s grad f(p) . d + (s**2 / 2) d^T H d."""
    g = gradient_closed(p)
    hess = hessian_closed(p)
    quad = d[0] * (hess[0][0] * d[0] + hess[0][1] * d[1]) + d[1] * (hess[1][0] * d[0] + hess[1][1] * d[1])
    return f(along(p, d, s)) - (f(p) + s * (g[0] * d[0] + g[1] * d[1]) + 0.5 * s * s * quad)


def main() -> None:
    g = gradient_closed(BASE)
    print(f"base point (x, y) = {BASE}")
    print(f"closed-form gradient: f_x = {g[0]:.6f}, f_y = {g[1]:.6f}")

    hess = hessian_closed(BASE)
    print(f"closed-form Hessian: f_xx = {hess[0][0]:.6f}, f_xy = {hess[0][1]:.6f}, f_yy = {hess[1][1]:.6f}")

    h = 1e-5
    fd = fd_hessian(BASE, h)
    hess = hessian_closed(BASE)
    worst = max(abs(fd[i][j] - hess[i][j]) for i in range(2) for j in range(2))
    print(f"central-difference Hessian at step {h:g}: largest error against closed form {worst:.2e}")
    asymmetry = abs(fd[0][1] - fd[1][0])
    print(f"central-difference Hessian asymmetry |f_xy - f_yx| at the same step: {asymmetry:.2e}")

    print(f"unit direction d = {DIRECTION}, norm check {DIRECTION[0] ** 2 + DIRECTION[1] ** 2:.6f}")
    previous_first = previous_second = 0.0
    for k, s in enumerate(STEPS):
        e1 = first_order_error(BASE, DIRECTION, s)
        e2 = second_order_error(BASE, DIRECTION, s)
        line = f"step s = {s:<7g} first-order error {e1: .6e}  second-order error {e2: .6e}"
        if k > 0:
            line += f"  ratios {previous_first / e1:.4f} {previous_second / e2:.4f}"
        print(line)
        previous_first, previous_second = e1, e2

4Where the canon breaks

The statement of Theorem 2.1 has two hypotheses: the partials \(f_x\), \(f_y\) and \(f_{xy}\) exist at every point of the disc \(N\), and \(f_{xy}\) is continuous at \(p\). Theorem 2.1 uses a hypothesis on \(f_{xy}\) at one point, not a hypothesis that \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable on the whole disc. Example 2.2 shows the failure that the hypothesis prevents.

  • Symmetry needs a hypothesis. Example 2.2 has both mixed partials exist near the origin and differ at it, so no symmetry can be assumed for an arbitrary twice-differentiated function. A proof of symmetry must check the continuity hypothesis at the point of interest.
  • A finite-difference table is not a proof of symmetry. Lab 1 prints a mixed-difference asymmetry of 1.94e-11. That number is a computed size at one step, and it cannot show symmetry at a point where the hypothesis fails. Lesson L13 treats the error of finite differences in general.
  • The remainder is little-o, not a constant times a cube, under Theorem 3.1. Theorem 3.1 gives \(R(h)/|h|^2 \to 0\) under twice differentiability on the disc together with continuity of \(H\) at \(p\). The cubic rate of Proposition 3.3 needs a third derivative in one direction, which Theorem 3.1 does not assume.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The mixed partials \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are always equal.”

    Example 2.2 computes \(f_{xy}(0,0) = -1\) and \(f_{yx}(0,0) = 1\) for \(f(x, y) = xy(x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)\). Theorem 2.1 gives equality under continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at the point, and the example shows that the hypothesis is needed.

  2. “The second-order term has no cross term, because it is only the second derivative times \(h^2\).”

    The quadratic form in Theorem 3.1 is \(\tfrac12 h^{\top} H h = \tfrac12(f_{xx} h_1^2 + 2 f_{xy} h_1 h_2 + f_{yy} h_2^2)\), and the cross term \(f_{xy} h_1 h_2\) is present whenever \(f_{xy}(p) \ne 0\). Lab 1 uses \(f_{xy}(0.5, 0.25) = e^{0.5} + 0.5 \approx 2.149\), so the cross term is not zero, and dropping it would change the second-order errors the lab prints.

  3. “A small asymmetry in a numerical Hessian shows that Theorem 2.1 fails.”

    Lab 1 prints an asymmetry of 1.94e-11 for a smooth function at step 1e-5. The mixed partials of that function are equal by Theorem 2.1, and the asymmetry is a finite-difference error of the computed quotients. A counterexample needs a point where the hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 fails, as in Example 2.2.

Mechanics

M1 · The Hessian of a cubic at a point

Let \(f(x, y) = x^3 + 2xy^2 - y\). Compute \(\nabla f\) and the Hessian at \((1, 2)\).

Solution

\(f_x = 3x^2 + 2y^2\), which is \(3 + 8 = 11\) at \((1, 2)\), and \(f_y = 4xy - 1\), which is \(8 - 1 = 7\). Then \(f_{xx} = 6x = 6\), \(f_{xy} = 4y = 8\), \(f_{yx} = 4y = 8\) and \(f_{yy} = 4x = 4\). The Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 8 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\), and the mixed entries agree as Example 1.2 requires.

M2 · The expansion of a quadratic is exact

Let \(f(x, y) = x^2 + xy + y^2\). Show that the second-order expansion at \((0, 0)\) has remainder zero for every \(h\).

Solution

The Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) everywhere, and \(\nabla f(0,0) = 0\). Then \(\tfrac12 h^{\top} H h = \tfrac12(2h_1^2 + 2h_1h_2 + 2h_2^2) = h_1^2 + h_1 h_2 + h_2^2 = f(h)\), so \(R(h) = 0\) for every \(h\), which is consistent with Theorem 3.1.

M3 · A second derivative along a direction

For \(f(x, y) = x^2 y\) at \((1, 1)\) and the direction \(d = (1, 0)\), compute \(g''(0)\) for \(g(s) = f((1,1) + s d)\), and check it against \(f_{xx}\).

Solution

Here \(g(s) = (1 + s)^2 \cdot 1\), so \(g''(s) = 2\) and \(g''(0) = 2\). Remark 3.2 gives \(g''(0) = d^{\top} H d = f_{xx}(1,1)\), and \(f_{xx} = 2y = 2\) at \((1,1)\), which agrees.

P1 · Symmetry for a polynomial

Show directly that \(f_{xy} = f_{yx}\) everywhere for \(f(x, y) = x^2 y^3\), without citing Theorem 2.1.

Solution

\(f_x = 2xy^3\), so \(f_{xy} = \partial_y(2xy^3) = 6xy^2\). Also \(f_y = 3x^2y^2\), so \(f_{yx} = \partial_x(3x^2y^2) = 6xy^2\). The two mixed partials are equal at every point, which is the claim for this polynomial.

P2 · The remainder of a one-variable quadratic

Let \(g(t) = a + bt + ct^2\). Show that \(\varphi(t) = g(t) - g(0) - t g'(0) - \tfrac12 t^2 g''(0)\) is identically zero, and explain what this says about Theorem 3.1 in one variable.

Solution

Here \(g(0) = a\), \(g'(0) = b\) and \(g''(0) = 2c\). Then \(\varphi(t) = a + bt + ct^2 - a - bt - \tfrac12 t^2 \cdot 2c = 0\). A quadratic equals its second-order expansion, so the remainder of Theorem 3.1 vanishes when \(f\) is a quadratic, as in Problem M2.

P3 · The Hessian of a product of exponential and linear terms

For \(f(x, y) = e^x y\), compute every entry of \(H\) and verify that it is symmetric, with a proof that uses only differentiation.

Solution

\(f_x = e^x y\) and \(f_y = e^x\). Then \(f_{xx} = e^x y\), \(f_{xy} = \partial_y(e^x y) = e^x\), \(f_{yx} = \partial_x(e^x) = e^x\) and \(f_{yy} = \partial_y(e^x) = 0\). The mixed entries agree, so \(H = \begin{pmatrix} e^x y & e^x \\ e^x & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) is symmetric at every point.

Empirical

E1 · Why the printed asymmetry is not a counterexample

Lab 1 prints an asymmetry of 1.94e-11 between the two mixed finite differences at step 1e-5. Explain why this does not contradict Theorem 2.1, and give the size of the rounding error that a difference at this step carries.

Solution

The lab's mixed entries are finite-difference quotients, not the analytic mixed partials, so they carry truncation and rounding errors. Theorem 2.1 says the analytic partials are equal for this smooth \(f\), and the quotients need not be equal to the same accuracy. A rounding error near \(\varepsilon/h\) with \(\varepsilon \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}\) at \(h = 10^{-5}\) is about \(2.2 \times 10^{-11}\), which is the size of the printed asymmetry.

E2 · The ratio of first-order errors (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints first-order errors whose successive ratios approach 4. Explain from Taylor's theorem why halving the step divides the first-order error by about 4, and say which term of the expansion produces that error.

Solution

The first-order error is \(R_1(s) = f(p+sd) - f(p) - s\nabla f(p) \cdot d = \tfrac12 g''(0)s^2 + o(s^2)\), a multiple of \(s^2\). Halving \(s\) divides \(s^2\) by 4, so the ratio tends to 4. The printed ratios 3.9509, 3.9750 and 3.9874 rise toward 4, consistent with this.

E3 · The second-order ratios (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints second-order ratios 7.8836, 7.9429 and 7.9717. Which term of the expansion makes the error, and what limit do the ratios approach?

Solution

After the second-order term is removed, the error is \(\tfrac16 g'''(0)s^3\) plus smaller terms, so the cubic term produces it. Proposition 3.3 gives a limit of 8 when \(g'''(0) \ne 0\). The printed ratios increase toward 8 at the four step sizes shown, and the lab does not establish the limit by itself.

Research

R1 · Which hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 fails in Example 2.2?

Example 2.2 has both mixed partials differ at the origin. Identify which hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 fails, and explain why its failure is forced by the conclusion.

Solution

The hypotheses are existence of \(f_x\), \(f_y\), \(f_{xy}\) near \(p\) and continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at \(p\). The first holds in Example 2.2. If \(f_{xy}\) were continuous at the origin, the theorem would force \(f_{yx}(0,0) = f_{xy}(0,0)\), contradicting \(-1 \ne 1\). So continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at the origin must fail, and the example is a case of a discontinuous mixed partial.

R2 · Extending Theorem 3.1 to three variables

State the hypotheses and the bound for the expansion of \(f\) on \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and identify which step of the proof of Theorem 3.1 changes.

Solution

Assume \(f\) is twice differentiable on a ball around \(p\), with the \(3 \times 3\) Hessian continuous at \(p\). The bound becomes \(|R(h)| \le c\,|h|^2\,\omega(|h|)\) with \(c\) depending only on the number of variables, and \(\omega\) defined by the maximum over entries. The step that changes is the entry bound \(|h^{\top} M h| \le c|h|^2 \max|M_{ij}|\), whose constant grows with the dimension. The mean value argument is unchanged.

R3 · When a quadratic form decides the sign of a change

Suppose \(\nabla f(p) = 0\), \(H\) is continuous at \(p\), and \(d^{\top} H(p) d > 0\) for every unit vector \(d\). Explain what Theorem 3.1 says about \(f(p + sd) - f(p)\) for small \(s\), and what it does not say about a fixed step.

Solution

Let \(m = \min\{d^{\top} H(p) d : |d| = 1\}\). The minimum exists because the unit circle is compact and the quadratic form is continuous, and \(m > 0\) by hypothesis. With \(\nabla f(p) = 0\), Theorem 3.1 gives \(f(p + sd) - f(p) \ge \tfrac12 s^2 m - 2s^2\omega(|s|) = s^2\bigl(\tfrac12 m - 2\omega(|s|)\bigr)\) for every unit \(d\), which is positive for every \(s \ne 0\) with \(\omega(|s|) < m/4\). That threshold does not depend on \(d\). The statement is about small steps, since \(\omega(|s|) \to 0\) is a limit, and it says nothing about a fixed step such as \(s = 0.1\) unless the threshold is checked for that step.

  1. What is the Hessian matrix of \(f\) at \(p\)?

    Options

    Definition 1.1: the Hessian collects \(f_{xx}\), \(f_{xy}\), \(f_{yx}\) and \(f_{yy}\) at \(p\).

  2. Which condition makes \(f_{xy}(p) = f_{yx}(p)\) follow from Theorem 2.1?

    Options

    Theorem 2.1 lists these hypotheses. Example 2.2 shows that the continuity hypothesis cannot be dropped.

  3. For \(f(x, y) = x^2 y\), what is \(f_{xy}\) at \((1, 1)\)?

    \(f_x = 2xy\) and \(\partial_y(2xy) = 2x\), which is \(2\) at \(x = 1\).

  4. In Theorem 3.1, what does \(R(h)/|h|^2\) tend to as \(h \to 0\)?

    Options

    The theorem gives \(|R(h)| \le 2|h|^2\omega(|h|)\), and \(\omega(r) \to 0\) by continuity of \(H\) at \(p\).

  5. For \(f(x, y) = x^2 + 3xy\) and the direction \(d = (1, 0)\), what is \(g''(0)\) where \(g(s) = f(p + sd)\)?

    \(g''(0) = d^{\top} H d = f_{xx} = 2\), since \(d = (1, 0)\).

  6. If the second-order error is \(E(s) = c s^3 + o(s^3)\) with \(c \ne 0\), what does \(E(s)/E(s/2)\) tend to?

    Options

    \(E(s)/E(s/2) = \dfrac{cs^3 + o(s^3)}{c s^3/8 + o(s^3)} \to 8\).

  7. For \(f(x, y) = x^3 y\), what is \(f_{xy}\) at \((2, 1)\)?

    \(f_x = 3x^2 y\), so \(f_{xy} = 3x^2\), which is \(12\) at \(x = 2\).

  8. In the counterexample \(f = xy(x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)\) with \(f(0,0) = 0\), what are the two mixed partials at the origin?

    Options

    Example 2.2: \(f_x(0,y) = -y\) gives \(f_{xy}(0,0) = -1\), and \(f_y(x,0) = x\) gives \(f_{yx}(0,0) = 1\).

  9. Why does the second-order expansion need the cross term \(f_{xy}\)?

    Options

    Theorem 3.1: \(\tfrac12 h^{\top} H h = \tfrac12(f_{xx} h_1^2 + 2 f_{xy} h_1 h_2 + f_{yy} h_2^2)\).

  10. For \(f(x, y) = e^x y\), what is \(f_{xx}\) at \((0, 2)\)?

    \(f_{xx} = e^x y\), which is \(e^0 \cdot 2 = 2\) at \((0, 2)\).
