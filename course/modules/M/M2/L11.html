Integration by Parts and Substitution

Many integrals cannot be read off a list of standard antiderivatives, and the two rules of this lesson turn such an integral into a simpler one. Integration by parts moves a derivative from one factor to the other. Substitution rewrites the integral in a new variable, and it changes the limits as well as the integrand. Both rules are consequences of the fundamental theorem of Lesson 10, and each has hypotheses that must be checked before the rule is used. This lesson states the hypotheses, proves each rule, and checks the identities numerically in Lab 1.

1Linearity and polynomials

Lemma 1.1 (linearity)

Let \(f\) and \(g\) be integrable on \([a, b]\), and let \(c\) be a real number. Then \(f + g\) and \(cf\) are integrable on \([a, b]\), and \[ \int_a^b (f + g) = \int_a^b f + \int_a^b g, \qquad \int_a^b cf = c\int_a^b f. \]

Proof

Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Since \(I_* = I^*\) for \(f\), there are partitions \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) with \(U(f, P_1) - L(f, P_1) < \varepsilon\) and \(U(g, P_2) - L(g, P_2) < \varepsilon\). Let \(R\) be the partition of all points of \(P_1\) and \(P_2\). By Lemma 1.1 of Lesson 9, refining does not increase \(U - L\), so both differences are below \(\varepsilon\) on \(R\). On each subinterval of \(R\), \(\sup(f + g) \le \sup f + \sup g\) and \(\inf(f+g) \ge \inf f + \inf g\). Hence \(U(f+g, R) - L(f+g, R) \le [U(f,R) - L(f,R)] + [U(g,R) - L(g,R)] < 2\varepsilon\). Since \(\varepsilon\) is arbitrary, \(f + g\) is integrable. Moreover \(L(f,R) + L(g,R) \le L(f+g,R) \le \int (f+g) \le U(f+g,R) \le U(f,R) + U(g,R)\), while \(\int f\) and \(\int g\) lie in the brackets \([L(f,R), U(f,R)]\) and \([L(g,R), U(g,R)]\). So \(\bigl|\int(f+g) - \int f - \int g\bigr| < 2\varepsilon\), and the sum formula follows. For the scalar case, if \(c \ge 0\) then \(U(cf, P) = cU(f,P)\) and \(L(cf,P) = cL(f,P)\) for every partition, so \(cf\) is integrable and \(\int cf = c\int f\). If \(c < 0\), then \(U(cf,P) = cL(f,P)\) and \(L(cf,P) = cU(f,P)\), which gives the same conclusion with the roles of the two sums swapped.

Corollary 1.2 (polynomials)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), for every polynomial \(p(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} c_k x^k\) and every \(b > 0\), \(p\) is integrable on \([0, b]\), and \(\int_0^b p = \sum_{k=0}^{m} c_k\, b^{k+1}/(k+1)\).

Proof

Each power \(x^k\) is nondecreasing on \([0, b]\), as shown in the proof of Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 10, so it is integrable by Theorem 2.2 of Lesson 9. For \(b = 0\) both sides are 0 by the convention of Lesson 9, Definition 1.3. Lemma 1.1 makes every finite combination integrable, and Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 10 gives the value of each term.

2Integration by parts

Theorem 2.1 (integration by parts)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(a < b\), and let \(u\) and \(v\) be differentiable on an open interval \(I\) that contains \([a, b]\). Suppose that \(u'v\) and \(uv'\) are integrable on \([a, b]\). Then \[ \int_a^b u(x)\,v'(x)\,dx = \bigl[uv\bigr]_a^b - \int_a^b u'(x)\,v(x)\,dx, \] where \([uv]_a^b = u(b)v(b) - u(a)v(a)\).

Proof

Let \(F = uv\). It is differentiable on \(I\) by the product rule of Lesson 5, so it is continuous on \([a, b]\) (differentiable functions are continuous, Lesson 4). Its derivative is \(F' = u'v + uv'\) on \(I\). By Lemma 1.1, \(f = u'v + uv'\) is integrable on \([a, b]\), so Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 10 gives \(\int_a^b (u'v + uv') = F(b) - F(a)\). Lemma 1.1 splits the left side into \(\int_a^b u'v + \int_a^b uv'\). Rearranging gives the formula.

Example 2.2 (a Beta-type integral)

Take \(u(x) = x^2\) and \(v'(x) = (1 - x)^3\), so \(v(x) = -(1 - x)^4/4\). Both \(u'v = -x(1-x)^4/2\) and \(uv' = x^2(1-x)^3\) are polynomials, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\) by Corollary 1.2. The boundary term is \([uv]_0^1 = 0\), because \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\). So \[ \int_0^1 x^2(1-x)^3\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 x(1-x)^4\,dx. \] Expanding \(x(1-x)^4 = x - 4x^2 + 6x^3 - 4x^4 + x^5\) and integrating term by term with Corollary 1.2 gives \(1/2 - 4/3 + 3/2 - 4/5 + 1/6 = 1/30\). Hence \(\int_0^1 x^2(1-x)^3\,dx = 1/60\), which Lab 1, part A checks numerically.

Example 2.3 (a boundary term that does not vanish)

Take \(u(x) = x\) and \(v(x) = x\), so \(u'v = 1\cdot x\) and \(uv' = x\). Then \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = [x^2]_0^1 - \int_0^1 x\,dx = 1 - \int_0^1 x\,dx\). Hence \(2\int_0^1 x\,dx = 1\), and \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = 1/2\), which Corollary 1.2 confirms. The boundary term is 1, not 0.

3Substitution

Theorem 3.1 (change of variables)

Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(a < b\) and \(g(a) \le g(b)\). Assume:

  1. \(g\) is differentiable on an open interval \(I\) that contains \([a, b]\);
  2. \(f\) has an antiderivative \(F\) on an open interval \(J\) with \(g(I) \subseteq J\), so \(F' = f\) on \(J\);
  3. \(f\) is integrable on \([g(a), g(b)]\), and \(t \mapsto f(g(t))\,g'(t)\) is integrable on \([a, b]\).

Then \(\displaystyle \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(x)\,dx = \int_a^b f(g(t))\,g'(t)\,dt\).

Proof

The first part of the argument does not need \(g(a) < g(b)\). The points \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\) lie in \(g(I) \subseteq J\), and \(J\) is an interval, so \([g(a), g(b)] \subseteq J\), and \(F\) is continuous there. Let \(H = F \circ g\). It is differentiable on \(I\), and by the chain rule \(H'(t) = F'(g(t))\,g'(t) = f(g(t))\,g'(t)\). Thus \(H\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), and by hypothesis 3 the function \(f(g(t))g'(t)\) is integrable there. Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 10 gives \[ \int_a^b f(g(t))\,g'(t)\,dt = H(b) - H(a) = F(g(b)) - F(g(a)). \] If \(g(a) = g(b)\), the left side is 0 and so is \(F(g(b)) - F(g(a))\). If \(g(a) < g(b)\), Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 10 applied to \(F\) on \([g(a), g(b)]\), with hypothesis 3 for integrability, gives \(\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f = F(g(b)) - F(g(a))\). In both cases the two sides agree, which proves the claim.

Example 3.2 (\(x^2\) with \(g(t) = t^3 + t\))

Let \(f(x) = x^2\), \(F(x) = x^3/3\), \(g(t) = t^3 + t\), \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\). Here \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g(1) = 2\), \(g\) is differentiable on \(I = \R\), and \(J = \R\) works for \(F\). The function \(f(g(t))g'(t) = (t^3 + t)^2(3t^2 + 1) = 3t^8 + 7t^6 + 5t^4 + t^2\) is a polynomial, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\). Both sides equal \(8/3\): \(\int_0^2 x^2\,dx = 8/3\) and \(\int_0^1 (3t^8 + 7t^6 + 5t^4 + t^2)\,dt = 1/3 + 1 + 1 + 1/3 = 8/3\).

Example 3.3 (\(x\) with \(g(t) = 1 + t^2\))

Let \(f(x) = x\), \(F(x) = x^2/2\), \(g(t) = 1 + t^2\), \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\). Then \(g(0) = 1\), \(g(1) = 2\), and \(f(g(t))g'(t) = 2t(1 + t^2) = 2t + 2t^3\). Both sides are \(3/2\): \(\int_1^2 x\,dx = (4 - 1)/2\), and \(\int_0^1 (2t + 2t^3)\,dt = 1 + 1/2\).

"""M2 L11 lab 1: integration by parts and substitution checked by midpoint quadrature.

Part A checks the integration-by-parts identity for u(x) = x^2 and v'(x) = (1 - x)^3 on [0, 1], with the
antiderivative v(x) = -(1 - x)^4 / 4, so that the identity reads int_0^1 x^2 (1 - x)^3 dx = [u v]_0^1 - int_0^1 2x
v(x) dx. Part B checks the substitution identity with g(t) = t^3 + t on [0, 1] and f(x) = x^2: the integral of f over
[g(0), g(1)] = [0, 2] equals the integral of f(g(t)) g'(t) over [0, 1]. Part C prints the integral of x^2 over [0, 1],
the left side of Theorem 3.1 with the limits [g(0), g(1)] = [0, 2] replaced by [a, b] = [0, 1]; that value is
different. Each integral is evaluated by the midpoint rule with N = 100000 subintervals, and each result is compared
with the exact value from the page: 1/60 for part A and 8/3 for part B. Standard library only; no randomness and no
network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections.abc import Callable

N = 100_000
TOL = 1e-6


def midpoint(f: Callable[[float], float], a: float, b: float, n: int = N) -> float:
    """Midpoint-rule approximation of the integral of f over [a, b] with n equal subintervals."""
    h = (b - a) / n
    return h * sum(f(a + (i + 0.5) * h) for i in range(n))


def part_a() -> None:
    lhs = midpoint(lambda x: x * x * (1 - x) ** 3, 0.0, 1.0)

    def u(x: float) -> float:
        return x * x

    def v(x: float) -> float:
        return -((1 - x) ** 4) / 4

    # The boundary term [uv]_0^1 = u(1) v(1) - u(0) v(0), computed from u and v.
    boundary = u(1.0) * v(1.0) - u(0.0) * v(0.0)
    # -int u' v with u'(x) = 2x, computed from the integrand.
    rhs = boundary + midpoint(lambda x: -2.0 * x * v(x), 0.0, 1.0)
    exact = 1 / 60
    print(f"part A: midpoint int_0^1 x^2 (1 - x)^3 dx = {lhs:.10f}, exact 1/60 = {exact:.10f}")
    print(f"part A: right side [uv] - int u'v = {rhs:.10f}")
    print(f"part A: left side equals right side to {TOL:g}: {abs(lhs - rhs) < TOL}")
    print(f"part A: left side equals 1/60 to {TOL:g}: {abs(lhs - exact) < TOL}")


def part_b() -> None:
    lhs = midpoint(lambda x: x * x, 0.0, 2.0)
    rhs = midpoint(lambda t: (t**3 + t) ** 2 * (3 * t * t + 1), 0.0, 1.0)
    exact = 8 / 3
    print(f"part B: int_0^2 x^2 dx (midpoint) = {lhs:.10f}, exact 8/3 = {exact:.10f}")
    print(f"part B: int_0^1 f(g(t)) g'(t) dt (midpoint) = {rhs:.10f}")
    print(f"part B: the two sides agree to {TOL:g}: {abs(lhs - rhs) < TOL}")
    print(f"part B: both equal 8/3 to {TOL:g}: {abs(lhs - exact) < TOL and abs(rhs - exact) < TOL}")


def part_c() -> None:
    unchanged = midpoint(lambda x: x * x, 0.0, 1.0)
    print(f"part C: int_0^1 x^2 dx with the limits unchanged (midpoint) = {unchanged:.10f}")
    print(f"part C: that value is 1/3, not 8/3: {abs(unchanged - 1 / 3) < TOL}")


def main() -> None:
    part_a()
    part_b()
    part_c()

Every integral here is a midpoint sum with \(N = 100000\) subintervals, so each printed value is an approximation with error far below \(10^{-6}\), and the check is numerical. The identities themselves are proved in Theorem 2.1 and Theorem 3.1. Part A checks the right side as the lab computes it, namely \(\tfrac12\int_0^1 x(1-x)^4\,dx\), which is \(-\int u'v\) with the boundary term zero. The lab does not test any function outside the polynomials used, and it does not test the hypotheses of the theorems, which the page checks by hand.

4Where the canon breaks

  • The change of variables moves the limits. Under \(x = g(t)\), the limits of integration become \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\). Example 3.2 has limits 0 and 2 on the \(x\)-side and 0 and 1 on the \(t\)-side. Lab 1, part C shows that leaving the limits at \([0, 1]\) gives \(1/3\), not \(8/3\).
  • The boundary term is not always zero. Example 2.3 has a boundary term of 1. In Example 2.2 the boundary term vanishes because \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\).
  • The hypotheses are checked, not assumed. Theorem 3.1 needs \(f(g(t))g'(t)\) to be integrable, which is a separate condition from the integrability of \(f\). This lesson checks it in each example by polynomial form (Corollary 1.2): the integrands of Examples 2.2, 2.3, 3.2 and 3.3 are polynomials. It proves nothing for a general composite.

5Misconceptions

  1. “In integration by parts the boundary term always vanishes.”

    In Example 2.3, \([uv]_0^1 = [x^2]_0^1 = 1\), and the boundary term is what makes the integral come out to \(1/2\). Dropping it would give \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = -\int_0^1 x\,dx\), which is false. The term vanishes in Example 2.2 because \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\).

  2. “Substitution does not change the limits of integration.”

    Theorem 3.1 has the limits \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\) on the left side and \(a\) and \(b\) on the right side. In Example 3.2, \(\int_0^2 x^2\,dx = 8/3\) and \(\int_0^1 x^2\,dx = 1/3\), and the substitution identity pairs \(8/3\) with the limits \(0\) and \(2\). Lab 1, part C prints \(1/3\) for the wrong limits.

  3. “Integration by parts applies to any two differentiable functions.”

    Theorem 2.1 requires \(u'v\) and \(uv'\) to be integrable on \([a, b]\). Theorem 2.1 lists the integrability of \(u'v\) and \(uv'\) as a separate hypothesis, and this lesson does not derive it from the differentiability of \(u\) and \(v\). Each application checks it, using polynomials on \([0, b]\) with \(b > 0\) (Corollary 1.2), monotone functions (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2), and sums and scalar multiples of integrable functions (Lemma 1.1).

Mechanics

M2-L11 · Integration by parts with \(u = x\)

Evaluate \(\int_0^1 x(1-x)^2\,dx\) by Theorem 2.1, with \(u = x\) and \(v' = (1-x)^2\).

Solution

Take \(v(x) = -(1-x)^3/3\), which has derivative \((1-x)^2\). The boundary term is \([uv]_0^1 = 1 \cdot 0 - 0 = 0\), since \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\). Then \(\int_0^1 x(1-x)^2\,dx = -\int_0^1 1 \cdot v\,dx = \frac13\int_0^1 (1-x)^3\,dx = \frac13 \cdot \frac14 = \frac1{12}\). Direct expansion, \(1/2 - 2/3 + 1/4\), gives the same value.

M2-L11 · Substitution with \(g(t) = 1 + t^2\)

Evaluate \(\int_0^1 2t(1+t^2)^2\,dt\) by Theorem 3.1 with \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(g(t) = 1 + t^2\).

Solution

Here \(g(0) = 1\) and \(g(1) = 2\), and \(g'(t) = 2t\), so the integrand is \(f(g(t))g'(t)\). Theorem 3.1 gives \(\int_0^1 2t(1+t^2)^2\,dt = \int_1^2 x^2\,dx = (8 - 1)/3 = 7/3\). Expanding directly, \(2t + 4t^3 + 2t^5\) integrates to \(1 + 1 + 1/3 = 7/3\).

M2-L11 · Integration by parts with a cubic factor

Evaluate \(\int_0^1 x^3(1-x)\,dx\) by Theorem 2.1.

Solution

Let \(u = x^3\) and \(v' = 1 - x\), so \(v = -(1-x)^2/2\). The boundary term is 0, since \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\). Then \(\int_0^1 x^3(1-x)\,dx = \frac{3}{2}\int_0^1 x^2(1-x)^2\,dx\). Expanding \(x^2(1-x)^2 = x^2 - 2x^3 + x^4\) gives \(1/3 - 1/2 + 1/5 = 1/30\), so the integral is \(3/60 = 1/20\). Direct expansion \(1/4 - 1/5 = 1/20\) agrees.

M2-L11 · Negation of an integral

Prove from the definitions that if \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(-f\) is integrable and \(\int_a^b (-f) = -\int_a^b f\).

Solution

For any partition \(P\), the supremum of \(-f\) on a subinterval is the negative of the infimum of \(f\), so \(U(-f,P) = -L(f,P)\) and \(L(-f,P) = -U(f,P)\). Hence \(U(-f,P) - L(-f,P) = U(f,P) - L(f,P)\), which shows that \(-f\) is integrable. Taking the supremum of \(L(-f,P) = -U(f,P)\) over \(P\) gives \(-\inf_P U(f,P) = -\int_a^b f\), so \(\int_a^b(-f) = -\int_a^b f\).

M2-L11 · Substitution for a linear map

Let \(c > 0\) and \(d\) be constants. Assume that \(f\) has an antiderivative on \(\R\), that \(f\) is integrable on \([ca + d, cb + d]\), and that \(t \mapsto f(ct + d)\) is integrable on \([a, b]\). Prove \(\int_{ca+d}^{cb+d} f(x)\,dx = c\int_a^b f(ct+d)\,dt\), using Theorem 3.1.

Solution

Let \(g(t) = ct + d\), which is differentiable on \(\R\), with \(g' = c\), and \(g(a) = ca + d \le cb + d = g(b)\) because \(c > 0\). Take \(I = J = \R\) and let \(F\) be the assumed antiderivative of \(f\). Then \(f(g(t))g'(t) = c\,f(ct+d)\), which is integrable on \([a, b]\) by Lemma 1.1 and the assumption on \(f(ct+d)\). Theorem 3.1 gives \(\int_{ca+d}^{cb+d} f = \int_a^b c\,f(ct+d)\,dt\), and Lemma 1.1 moves the constant \(c\) outside.

M2-L11 · The sup of a sum

Show that for bounded \(f\) and \(g\) on a subinterval \(S\), \(\sup_S (f+g) \le \sup_S f + \sup_S g\), and that \(\inf_S (f+g) \ge \inf_S f + \inf_S g\).

Solution

For every \(x \in S\), \(f(x) + g(x) \le \sup_S f + \sup_S g\), so the supremum of \(f + g\) on \(S\) is at most the right side. Similarly, \(f(x) + g(x) \ge \inf_S f + \inf_S g\) for every \(x\), so the infimum is at least the right side. These are the two inequalities used in the proof of Lemma 1.1.

Empirical

M2-L11 · The number of midpoint parts for \(x^2\) on \([0, 2]\)

For the midpoint rule with \(N\) parts applied to \(x^2\) on \([0, 2]\), the error equals \(2/(3N^2)\) exactly. Find the smallest \(N\) for which the error is at most \(10^{-6}\).

Solution

On a subinterval of width \(h\) with midpoint \(m\), the midpoint rule error of \(x^2\) is \(\int_{m-h/2}^{m+h/2} x^2\,dx - h m^2 = \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} t^2\,dt = h^3/12\), because the linear term in \(t\) integrates to zero. With \(N\) parts, \(h = 2/N\), so the total error is \(N \cdot (2/N)^3/12 = 2/(3N^2)\). The condition \(2/(3N^2) \le 10^{-6}\) means \(N^2 \ge 666\,667\), so \(N \ge 817\). Checking, \(N = 816\) gives \(1.0012 \times 10^{-6}\), which fails, and \(N = 817\) gives \(9.988 \times 10^{-7}\), which passes.

M2-L11 · The residual in part B

From Lab 1, part B, the midpoint value of the substitution's right side is \(2.6666666663\), and the exact value is \(8/3\). Compute the residual.

Solution

\(8/3 = 2.6666666667\) to ten decimals, so the residual is \(2.6666666667 - 2.6666666663 = 4 \times 10^{-10}\). This is far below the \(10^{-6}\) tolerance of the lab.

M2-L11 · The factor \(1/2\) in Example 2.2

Lab 1, part A prints \(1/60\) for the left side. Check that \(\frac12\int_0^1 x(1-x)^4\,dx\) equals \(1/60\) from the value \(\int_0^1 x(1-x)^4\,dx = 1/30\) that Example 2.2 derives.

Solution

Half of \(1/30\) is \(1/60 = 0.0166666\ldots\), which is the printed value \(0.0166666667\) to ten decimals. The integral \(\int_0^1 x(1-x)^4\,dx = 1/30\) comes from the term-by-term expansion in Example 2.2, so the check uses the page's own computation and the lab's quadrature.

Research

M2-L11 · The Beta-type integral in general

For integers \(m, n \ge 0\), prove by induction on \(n\) that \(\int_0^1 x^m (1-x)^n\,dx = m!\,n!/(m+n+1)!\), for every \(m\).

Solution

Write \(I(m,n)\) for the integral. For \(n = 0\), \(I(m,0) = 1/(m+1) = m!\,0!/(m+1)!\). Assume the formula for \(n\) and all \(m\). Integrate by parts with \(u = (1-x)^{n+1}\) and \(v' = x^m\), so \(v = x^{m+1}/(m+1)\). The boundary term vanishes, since \(u(1) = 0\) and \(v(0) = 0\). Then \(I(m, n+1) = \frac{n+1}{m+1}I(m+1, n)\). By the hypothesis, \(I(m+1,n) = (m+1)!\,n!/(m+n+2)!\), so \(I(m,n+1) = (n+1)!\,m!/(m+n+2)!\), which is the formula for \(n+1\).

M2-L11 · Substitution that scales the variable

Use Theorem 3.1 with \(g(t) = bt\), \(b > 0\), and \(f(x) = x^k\) on \([0, b]\), to prove \(\int_0^b x^k\,dx = b^{k+1}/(k+1)\), checking each hypothesis.

Solution

Take \(a = 0\), \(g(0) = 0\), \(g(1) = b\), with \(I = J = \R\) and \(F(x) = x^{k+1}/(k+1)\), so that \(F' = f\) on \(J\). Hypothesis 1 holds since \(g\) is linear, hypothesis 2 holds since \(g(I) \subseteq \R = J\), and \(f = x^k\) is integrable on \([0, b]\) by Corollary 1.2. The integrand \(f(g(t))g'(t) = b^k t^k \cdot b = b^{k+1}t^k\) is a polynomial, hence integrable on \([0,1]\). Theorem 3.1 gives \(\int_0^b x^k\,dx = \int_0^1 b^{k+1}t^k\,dt = b^{k+1}/(k+1)\), which agrees with Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 10.

M2-L11 · Checking Theorem 3.1 for a squaring map

Check Theorem 3.1 for \(g(t) = t^2\) on \([0,1]\) and \(f(x) = x\), and state which hypothesis carries the integrability of the composite.

Solution

Here \(g(0) = 0\), \(g(1) = 1\), and \(g\) is differentiable on \(I = \R\). Take \(J = \R\) and \(F(x) = x^2/2\). Then \(f(g(t))g'(t) = t^2 \cdot 2t = 2t^3\), a polynomial, hence integrable, and hypothesis 3 is satisfied. The left side is \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = 1/2\), and the right side is \(\int_0^1 2t^3\,dt = 2/4 = 1/2\). Hypothesis 3 has two integrability conditions: \(f\) on \([g(0), g(1)] = [0, 1]\), which holds because \(f = x\) is monotone, and the composite \(f(g(t))g'(t) = 2t^3\) on \([0, 1]\). The second is the one that carries the integrability of the composite.
