Constrained optimization and the envelope theorem

  • Uses: derivative (L04), partial derivative (L16), implicit-function theorem (L21)
  • Introduces: envelope theorem
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result

A firm, a household or a portfolio chooses a quantity to make an objective as large as possible, and the best achievable value depends on a parameter such as a price, a rate or a budget. The question "how much does the best value change when the parameter moves?" is answered by solving for the optimal choice and differentiating. The envelope theorem of Definition 1.1 gives the derivative of the best value as the partial derivative of the objective at the optimal choice, without differentiating that choice. This lesson proves it for one choice variable, shows how a constraint is handled by substitution, and shows the corner where the result fails.

1The value of a maximization and its derivative

Let \(J\) and \(I\) be open intervals, with \(x\) in \(J\) the choice and \(a\) in \(I\) the parameter. Let \(f\) be a real function on \(J \times I\). For each \(a\), the value function is \(V(a) = \max_{x \in J} f(x, a)\) whenever the maximum exists, and a maximizer is a point \(\hat{x}(a) \in J\) with \(f(\hat{x}(a), a) = V(a)\).

Definition 1.1 (the envelope theorem)

The envelope theorem is the statement that, under stated hypotheses, the derivative of the value function \(V\) at \(a\) equals the partial derivative \(f_a\) of the objective at the optimal choice \(\hat{x}(a)\), with the choice held at its optimum and not differentiated.

Lemma 1.2 (interior maximum, first-order condition)

Let \(\varphi\) be differentiable on an open interval \(J\), and let \(\hat{x} \in J\) be a maximizer of \(\varphi\) on \(J\). Then \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) = 0\).

Proof

If \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) > 0\), then \(\bigl(\varphi(\hat{x} + t) - \varphi(\hat{x})\bigr)/t \to \varphi'(\hat{x}) > 0\) as \(t \to 0\), so for small \(t > 0\) the quotient is positive and \(\varphi(\hat{x} + t) > \varphi(\hat{x})\). That contradicts maximality. If \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) < 0\), the same argument with \(t < 0\) gives \(\varphi(\hat{x} + t) > \varphi(\hat{x})\) for small \(t < 0\), again a contradiction. So \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 1.3 (envelope theorem, one choice variable)

Assume the following. (H1) \(f\) is continuously differentiable on \(J \times I\). (H2) For each \(a \in I\), \(\hat{x}(a) \in J\) is a maximizer of \(f(\cdot, a)\) on \(J\). (H3) The function \(\hat{x}\) is differentiable on \(I\). Then \(V(a) = f(\hat{x}(a), a)\) is differentiable on \(I\), and

\[V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a).\]

Proof

For fixed \(a\), the function \(\varphi(x) = f(x, a)\) is differentiable on \(J\), and \(\hat{x}(a)\) maximizes it by (H2). By Lemma 1.2, \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\) for every \(a \in I\).

The map \(a \mapsto (\hat{x}(a), a)\) is differentiable by (H3), and \(f\) is differentiable by (H1). The chain rule therefore applies to \(V(a) = f(\hat{x}(a), a)\), giving

\[V'(a) = f_x(\hat{x}(a), a)\,\hat{x}^{\prime}(a) + f_a(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0 \cdot \hat{x}^{\prime}(a) + f_a(\hat{x}(a), a).\]

The first term vanishes because \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\), so \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.4 (where (H3) comes from)

Suppose \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable, \(\hat{x}(a_0)\) is a point where \(f_x = 0\) and \(f_{xx} \ne 0\), and \(\hat{x}\) is continuous at \(a_0\). Apply Theorem 1.2 of Lesson L21 to \(F(a, x) = f_x(x, a)\), with \(a\) as the independent variable and \(x\) as the dependent one, at \((a_0, \hat{x}(a_0))\), where the partial derivative of \(F\) in \(x\) is \(f_{xx} \ne 0\). The theorem gives a differentiable function \(\tilde{x}\) on an interval \(I\) around \(a_0\), with values in an interval \(J\) around \(\hat{x}(a_0)\), and \(f_x(\tilde{x}(a), a) = 0\). For \(a\) near \(a_0\), \(\hat{x}(a)\) lies in \(J\) by continuity, and \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\) by Lemma 1.2. The zero of \(f_x(\cdot, a)\) in \(J\) is unique, so \(\hat{x}(a) = \tilde x(a)\) there, which gives (H3) locally.

Example 1.5 (a logarithmic objective)

Let \(f(x, a) = a\ln(1 + x) - x\) on \(J = (-1, \infty)\) and \(I = (0, \infty)\). Here \(f_x = a/(1+x) - 1\), which vanishes exactly at \(x = a - 1\), and \(f_{xx} = -a/(1+x)^2 < 0\). So \(f(\cdot, a)\) is strictly concave, and its unique critical point \(\hat{x}(a) = a - 1\) is its global maximizer on \(J\), which gives (H2). The map \(\hat{x}\) is differentiable, which gives (H3). The value is \(V(a) = a\ln a - (a - 1)\), so \(V'(a) = \ln a\). Theorem 1.3 predicts \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a) = \ln(1 + \hat{x}(a)) = \ln a\), which agrees.

"""M2 lab L22: the envelope theorem, checked on two maximization problems with closed-form answers.

SIMULATED: both objectives are test functions chosen for this lab. They are not market data.
Part A: maximize f(x, a) = a * ln(1 + x) - x over x in [0, 10], for a in {1.5, 2, 3, 4}. The maximizer is x_hat = a - 1
and the value function is V(a) = a ln a - (a - 1), so V'(a) = ln a. The lab finds x_hat by golden-section search, then
compares a central difference of V (step 1e-4) with ln a and with the partial derivative f_a(x_hat, a) = ln(1 + x_hat).
Part B: maximize x * y subject to x + y = a. The constraint is absorbed by substituting y = a - x, which leaves the
unconstrained problem F(x, a) = x * (a - x). The value is V(a) = a**2 / 4, so V'(a) = a / 2. The lab compares the
central difference of V with a / 2, and it prints the naive partial derivative of x * y with respect to a at fixed x
and y, which is 0, to show why the constraint cannot be ignored.
Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from collections.abc import Callable

PARAMETERS_A: tuple[float, ...] = (1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0)
PARAMETERS_B: tuple[float, ...] = (1.0, 2.0, 3.0)
STEP = 1e-4
GOLDEN = (math.sqrt(5.0) - 1.0) / 2.0


def golden_max(fun: Callable[[float], float], lo: float, hi: float, tol: float = 1e-12) -> float:
    """The maximizer of a concave function fun on [lo, hi], by golden-section search to width tol.

    The interval must satisfy lo < hi and tol > 0; otherwise ValueError is raised.
    """
    if not lo < hi:
        raise ValueError(f"need lo < hi, got {lo} and {hi}")
    if tol <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the tolerance must be positive, got {tol}")
    a, b = lo, hi
    c = b - GOLDEN * (b - a)
    d = a + GOLDEN * (b - a)
    fc, fd = fun(c), fun(d)
    while b - a > tol:
        if fc > fd:
            b, d, fd = d, c, fc
            c = b - GOLDEN * (b - a)
            fc = fun(c)
        else:
            a, c, fc = c, d, fd
            d = a + GOLDEN * (b - a)
            fd = fun(d)
    return (a + b) / 2.0


def objective_a(x: float, a: float) -> float:
    """f(x, a) = a * ln(1 + x) - x for x > -1."""
    return a * math.log(1.0 + x) - x


def maximizer_a(a: float) -> float:
    """The maximizer x_hat of f(x, a) over [0, 10], by golden-section search."""
    return golden_max(lambda x: objective_a(x, a), 0.0, 10.0)


def maximizer_b(a: float) -> float:
    """The maximizer x_hat of x * (a - x) over [0, a], by golden-section search."""
    return golden_max(lambda x: x * (a - x), 0.0, a)


def value_a(a: float) -> float:
    """V(a) = max over x in [0, 10] of f(x, a), computed numerically."""
    x_star = maximizer_a(a)
    return objective_a(x_star, a)


def value_b(a: float) -> float:
    """V(a) = max over x of x * (a - x), computed numerically on [0, a]; the constraint y = a - x is substituted."""
    x_star = maximizer_b(a)
    return x_star * (a - x_star)


def central_difference(v: Callable[[float], float], a: float, h: float) -> float:
    """The central difference (v(a + h) - v(a - h)) / (2 h). The step h must be positive."""
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    return (v(a + h) - v(a - h)) / (2.0 * h)


def main() -> None:
    print("Part A: maximize a*ln(1 + x) - x over x in [0, 10]; V'(a) should equal ln(a)")
    for a in PARAMETERS_A:
        x_star = maximizer_a(a)
        fd = central_difference(value_a, a, STEP)
        partial = math.log(1.0 + x_star)
        error = max(abs(fd - math.log(a)), abs(partial - math.log(a)))
        print(
            f"a = {a:<4} x_hat = {x_star:.8f} (a - 1 = {a - 1.0:.1f}) V' central difference {fd:.8f} "
            f"ln(a) {math.log(a):.8f} f_a(x_hat, a) {partial:.8f} max error {error:.1e}"
        )
    print("Part B: maximize x*y subject to x + y = a; V'(a) should equal a/2")
    for a in PARAMETERS_B:
        x_star = maximizer_b(a)
        fd = central_difference(value_b, a, STEP)
        reduced_partial = x_star
        naive_partial = 0.0  # x * y has no a in it, so at fixed x and y its partial in a is zero
        print(
            f"a = {a:<4} x_hat = {x_star:.8f} V' central difference {fd:.8f} a/2 {a / 2.0:.8f} "
            f"reduced F_a(x_hat, a) {reduced_partial:.8f} naive partial of x*y in a {naive_partial:.8f} "
            f"error {abs(fd - a / 2.0):.1e}"
        )

2Constraints absorbed into the parameter

When the constraint depends on the parameter, the naive partial derivative can be wrong, as Example 2.2 shows. The fix is to solve the constraint for one choice variable and substitute, which leaves an unconstrained problem.

Proposition 2.1 (a constraint solved for one variable)

Let \(f\) be continuously differentiable on an open set containing the relevant points, and let the constraint be \(y = c(x, a)\) with \(c\) continuously differentiable. Put \(F(x, a) = f(x, c(x, a), a)\). Assume that \(F\) satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem 1.3 with maximizer \(\hat{x}(a)\), and put \(y^*(a) = c(\hat{x}(a), a)\). Then the problem of maximizing \(f\) subject to \(y = c(x, a)\) has value \(V(a) = F(\hat{x}(a), a)\), and

\[V'(a) = f_y\bigl(\hat{x}(a), y^*(a), a\bigr)\,c_a\bigl(\hat{x}(a), a\bigr) + f_a\bigl(\hat{x}(a), y^*(a), a\bigr).\]

Proof

Along feasible points \(y = c(x, a)\), so the value is \(\max_x F(x, a)\), and Theorem 1.3 applies to \(F\), giving \(V'(a) = F_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\). The chain rule gives \(F_a(x, a) = f_y(x, c(x,a), a)\,c_a(x,a) + f_a(x, c(x,a), a)\), and substituting \(x = \hat{x}(a)\) gives the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (maximize \(xy\) on the line \(x + y = a\))

The constraint is \(y = c(x, a) = a - x\), so \(c_a = 1\). Then \(F(x, a) = x(a - x)\) and \(F_x = a - 2x\), which vanishes at \(\hat{x}(a) = a/2\), with \(F_{xx} = -2 < 0\). So \(\hat{x}(a) = a/2\) is the maximizer, \(y^*(a) = a/2\), and \(V(a) = a^2/4\), giving \(V'(a) = a/2\). Proposition 2.1 gives \(f_y c_a + f_a = (a/2)(1) + 0 = a/2\), which agrees. The naive partial \(f_a\) of \(xy\) at fixed \(x\) and \(y\) is zero, and it misses the term \(f_y c_a\).

Example 2.3 (a corner where the result fails)

Let \(f(x, a) = ax\) on \(x \in [0, 1]\), so \(V(a) = \max_{x \in [0,1]} ax\). For \(a > 0\) the unique maximizer is \(\hat{x} = 1\), an endpoint, and \(V(a) = a\). For \(a < 0\) the unique maximizer is \(\hat{x} = 0\), and \(V(a) = 0\). At \(a = 0\) every \(x\) in \([0,1]\) is a maximizer, and \(V(0) = 0\). The one-sided derivatives are \(V'(0^+) = 1\) and \(V'(0^-) = 0\), so \(V\) is not differentiable at \(0\).

  • The naive partial ignores the constraint. In Example 2.2 the constraint moves \(y\) with \(a\), and a derivative that ignores this gives zero where the answer is \(a/2\).
  • Theorem 1.3 needs a differentiable selection of maximizers on an open interval. Hypotheses (H2) and (H3) require \(\hat{x}(a)\) to maximize \(f(\cdot, a)\) and to be differentiable, and \(J\) is open, so the maximizer is interior. Example 2.3 has a closed domain \([0, 1]\), so the theorem does not apply there. At \(a > 0\) the formula still gives \(f_a(1, a) = 1\), which equals \(V'(a) = 1\). At \(a = 0\) the maximizer is not unique, and the formula gives \(f_a(1, 0) = 1\) on one side and \(f_a(0, 0) = 0\) on the other, matching the two one-sided derivatives.
  • The value function can have a kink. In Example 2.3 the kink occurs at \(a = 0\), the one parameter value where the maximizer is not unique. The theorem does not claim differentiability of \(V\) at such a point.

3Misconceptions

  1. “When a constraint depends on \(a\), the derivative of the value is still the partial of the objective in \(a\).”

    Example 2.2 has objective \(xy\), with partial \(0\) in \(a\), and the true derivative is \(a/2\). Proposition 2.1 shows the missing term \(f_y c_a\), and Lab 1 Part B prints the zero naive partial next to the correct central difference.

  2. “The envelope theorem needs no assumption about the optimum.”

    Theorem 1.3 uses (H2), that \(\hat{x}(a)\) maximizes \(f(\cdot,a)\). Example 2.3 shows that at \(a = 0\), where the maximizer is not unique, the value function has no derivative, so the formula cannot hold there.

  3. “A small error in the optimal choice ruins the derivative of the value.”

    Lab 1 prints \(\hat{x}\) with an error of about \(10^{-8}\), and the central difference of \(V\) still matches \(\ln a\) to the printed digits. At an interior maximum, where the first derivative in \(x\) is zero, a location error \(e\) changes the objective by about \(\tfrac12 |f_{xx}|\,e^2\) to leading order. At \(a = 1.5\), \(\hat{x} = 0.5\) and \(f_{xx} = -a/(1+\hat{x})^2 = -0.666667\) (Example 1.5), so a location error of \(10^{-8}\) changes the objective by about \(0.5 \times 0.666667 \times 10^{-16} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-17}\) (Section 1, interpretation of Lab 1).

Mechanics

M1 · The envelope of a quadratic objective

Let \(f(x, a) = ax - x^2/2\). Find \(\hat{x}(a)\), \(V(a)\) and \(V'(a)\), and check that \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\).

Solution

\(f_x = a - x = 0\) gives \(\hat{x}(a) = a\), and \(f_{xx} = -1 < 0\) makes it the maximizer. Then \(V(a) = a^2 - a^2/2 = a^2/2\), so \(V'(a) = a\). Also \(f_a = x\), which at \(\hat{x} = a\) is \(a\), so the two agree.

M2 · A shifted objective

Let \(f(x, a) = -(x - a)^2 + 3x\). Find \(V'(a)\) by the envelope theorem and check it by differentiating \(V\) directly.

Solution

\(f_x = -2(x - a) + 3 = 0\) gives \(\hat{x} = a + \tfrac32\), and \(f_{xx} = -2 < 0\). Then \(V(a) = -\tfrac94 + 3a + \tfrac92 = 3a + \tfrac94\), so \(V'(a) = 3\). The envelope formula gives \(f_a = 2(x - a) = 2 \cdot \tfrac32 = 3\), which agrees.

M3 · Maximize \(xy\) on the line \(x + 2y = a\)

Find \(V'(a)\) for the constrained problem of maximizing \(xy\) subject to \(x + 2y = a\), using Proposition 2.1.

Solution

Solve the constraint as \(y = c(x, a) = (a - x)/2\), so \(c_a = 1/2\). Then \(F(x, a) = x(a-x)/2\), with \(F_x = (a - 2x)/2 = 0\) at \(\hat{x} = a/2\), and \(F_{xx} = -1 < 0\). So \(y^* = a/4\) and \(V(a) = a^2/8\). The formula gives \(f_y c_a + f_a = (a/2)(1/2) + 0 = a/4\), and \(V'(a) = a/4\) directly.

Proof

P1 · The chain-rule step

Using only the chain rule and Lemma 1.2, prove that \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\) under (H1) to (H3).

Solution

Lemma 1.2 applied to \(f(\cdot, a)\) for each \(a\) gives \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\). The chain rule applied to \(a \mapsto f(\hat{x}(a), a)\) gives \(f_x \cdot \hat{x}^{\prime} + f_a\), and the first term is zero, so \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\).

P2 · The corner value function

For Example 2.3, show that \(V(a) = \max(a, 0)\) and that \(V\) has one-sided derivatives \(1\) and \(0\) at \(a = 0\).

Solution

For \(a > 0\) the largest value of \(ax\) on \([0,1]\) is at \(x = 1\), giving \(a\). For \(a < 0\) it is at \(x = 0\), giving \(0\). So \(V(a) = \max(a, 0)\). At \(0\), \(V(h) - V(0) = h\) for \(h > 0\), giving right derivative \(1\), and \(V(-h) - V(0) = 0\) for \(h > 0\), giving left derivative \(0\).

P3 · Why the constrained formula has two terms

For a constraint \(y = c(x, a)\), explain in one sentence why \(f_a\) alone is not the derivative of the value, and identify the term that the substitution adds.

Solution

Along the constraint, \(y\) moves with \(a\) through \(c_a\), so the objective changes through \(y\) as well as through its explicit dependence on \(a\). The added term is \(f_y c_a\), which accounts for the change in \(y\) forced by the constraint.

Empirical

E1 · The location error of the maximizer (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints \(\hat{x} = 0.50000001\) for \(a = 1.5\), while the exact maximizer is \(0.5\). Explain why the search is off by about \(10^{-8}\) and why the value of the maximum is much more accurate.

Solution

Near a maximum, \(f(\hat{x} + t, a) - f(\hat{x}, a)\) is of order \(t^2\), so differences between nearby points are at the level of machine precision once \(t\) is about the square root of that precision, roughly \(10^{-8}\). The search therefore resolves the location only to that scale. The value at a point within \(10^{-8}\) of the maximum differs from the maximum by about \(10^{-16}\), which is why the value is accurate.

E2 · The naive partial (Lab 1)

Lab 1 Part B prints \(0\) for the naive partial of \(xy\) in \(a\), and the central difference of \(V\) equals \(a/2\). State which quantity the naive partial computes and which the central difference computes.

Solution

The naive partial holds \(x\) and \(y\) fixed, and \(xy\) has no \(a\), so it is zero. The central difference differentiates \(V(a) = a^2/4\), in which the constraint moves \(x\) and \(y\) with \(a\). Their values differ by \(a/2\), which is the term \(f_y c_a\) of Proposition 2.1.

E3 · Comparing the two columns (Lab 1)

At \(a = 1.5\) Lab 1 prints a central difference of \(V\) and an \(f_a\) value that differ in the last digit. Explain which of these depends on the printed \(\hat{x}\), and why.

Solution

The \(f_a\) value is \(\ln(1 + \hat{x})\), which depends on \(\hat{x}\) directly, so the error in \(\hat{x}\) of about \(10^{-8}\) passes into it. The central difference of \(V\) uses only the computed values of \(V\), which are insensitive to the location error to first order. That is why the two columns differ at the last digit.

Research

R1 · Where the implicit-function theorem enters

Identify the single hypothesis of Theorem 1.3 that needs the implicit-function theorem, and state the extra condition on \(f_{xx}\) under which that result gives it.

Solution

Hypothesis (H3), differentiability of \(\hat{x}\), is the one that needs the implicit-function theorem. By Remark 1.4, the condition \(f_{xx}(\hat{x}(a_0), a_0) \ne 0\) at a point of continuity of \(\hat{x}\) gives it, using Theorem 1.2 of Lesson L21. Without that condition, the implicit-function argument does not apply, and \(\hat{x}\) may fail to be differentiable.

R2 · Several choice variables

State what the envelope theorem says when the choice \(x\) is a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), using the gradient with respect to \(x\), and name the matrix of second derivatives that the differentiability of \(\hat{x}\) needs. Lesson L19 defines that matrix.

Solution

The formula becomes \(\nabla_a V(a) = \nabla_a f(\hat{x}(a), a)\) with the vector of parameter partials, and the first-order condition is \(\nabla_x f(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\). The differentiability of \(\hat{x}\) needs the Hessian matrix in \(x\), which must be invertible for the implicit-function argument of Remark 1.4 to apply in the vector case. The Hessian is the matrix defined in Lesson L19.

R3 · Sensitivity without solving

Explain why the envelope theorem lets an analyst compute the sensitivity of an optimal value without computing \(\hat{x}(a)\), and state what the analyst must still be able to do for the formula to be useful.

Solution

The formula \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\) needs only the optimal choice at the point of interest, not its derivative, so a numerical optimizer that returns \(\hat{x}\) is enough. The analyst must still find the maximizer at each point and know that the hypotheses hold there, in particular that \(\hat{x}(a)\) is a differentiable maximizer there (H2 and H3). Example 2.3 shows that \(V\) can fail to be differentiable at a parameter where the maximizer is not unique.
