The Gradient and the Directional Derivative

  • Uses: the partial derivative (Lesson L16), the mean value theorem of Lesson L06, the Euclidean norm, dot product and Cauchy–Schwarz inequality of Lesson L15, and the limit of a function of several variables (Lesson L15)
  • Introduces: gradient
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A sensitivity along a chosen direction is a question about a function of several variables, and the answer should follow from a single vector of partial derivatives. The gradient is that vector. This lesson defines it, defines the directional derivative as a limit along a line, proves under a continuity hypothesis that the directional derivative is the dot product of the gradient with the direction, and shows that the gradient points in the steepest direction when steepness is measured with the Euclidean norm.

1The gradient

Definition 1.1 (gradient)

Let \(f\) be a real function on an open set \(U\) in \(\R^n\), and let \(a \in U\) be a point where every partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x_k(a)\) exists. The gradient of \(f\) at \(a\) is the vector

\[\nabla f(a) = \Bigl(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}(a), \dots, \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}(a)\Bigr) \in \R^n.\]

Assumption 1. The derivative of \(\sin\) is \(\cos\), the derivative of \(\cos\) is \(-\sin\), and \(|\cos t| \le 1\) for every real \(t\). This page assumes these facts and does not prove them. Example 1.2, Example 2.3, the Lab 1 hypothesis, Problem E2 and Problem R1 use them, and each says so. Problem E2 uses all three.

Example 1.2 (the function of Lab 1)

Let \(f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin y\). Under Assumption 1, holding one variable fixed, \(\partial f/\partial x = 2xy\) and \(\partial f/\partial y = x^2 + \cos y\). At \(a = (1, 0.5)\) the gradient is \(\nabla f(a) = (2 \cdot 1 \cdot 0.5,\; 1 + \cos 0.5) = (1,\; 1.8775825619)\). Its length is \(\sqrt{1 + 1.8775825619^2} = 2.1272790782\), and its direction makes the angle \(\arctan(1.8775825619/1) = 61.9602\) degrees with the positive \(x\)-axis.

2The directional derivative

Definition 2.1 (directional derivative)

Let \(f\) be defined on an open set \(U\) in \(\R^n\), let \(a \in U\), and let \(u\) be a vector of length \(|u| = 1\). The directional derivative of \(f\) at \(a\) in the direction \(u\) is

\[D_u f(a) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(a + t u) - f(a)}{t},\]

when this limit exists. Taking \(u = e_i\) gives \(D_{e_i} f(a) = \partial f/\partial x_i(a)\), so partial derivatives are directional derivatives in the coordinate directions.

Definition 2.1 uses the values on the line through \(a\) in direction \(u\). Theorem 2.2 relates these values to the partial derivatives, and it needs a continuity hypothesis, as Section 4 shows.

Theorem 2.2 (directional derivative from the gradient)

Let \(f\) be defined on an open ball \(B\) about \(a\) in \(\R^n\). Assume that every partial derivative \(\partial f/\partial x_k\) exists at every point of \(B\), and that each \(\partial f/\partial x_k\) is continuous at \(a\). Then for every unit vector \(u\), the directional derivative \(D_u f(a)\) exists and

\[D_u f(a) = \nabla f(a) \cdot u = \sum_{k=1}^{n} u_k \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_k}(a).\]

Proof

Let \(0 < |t| < r\), where \(B = B(a, r)\). Put \(p_0 = a\) and \(p_k = a + (t u_1, \dots, t u_k, 0, \dots, 0)\) for \(k = 1, \dots, n\), so that \(p_n = a + tu\) and \(p_k - p_{k-1} = t u_k e_k\). Each \(p_k\) lies in \(B\), because its distance from \(a\) is at most \(|t|\,|u| = |t|\). The telescoping sum gives

\[f(a + tu) - f(a) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} \bigl(f(p_k) - f(p_{k-1})\bigr).\]

Fix \(k\) with \(u_k \ne 0\). The function \(s \mapsto f(p_{k-1} + s e_k)\) is defined for \(s\) between \(0\) and \(t u_k\), and its points stay in \(B\). Its derivative at \(s\) is \(\partial f/\partial x_k\) at \(p_{k-1} + s e_k\), which exists by hypothesis. So the function is differentiable on the open interval between \(0\) and \(t u_k\) and continuous on the closed one. The mean value theorem of Lesson L06 gives \(\theta_k\) in \((0, 1)\) with

\[f(p_k) - f(p_{k-1}) = t u_k \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_k}(q_k), \qquad q_k = p_{k-1} + \theta_k t u_k e_k.\]

If \(u_k = 0\), the \(k\)th term is zero, and the same identity holds with \(q_k = p_{k-1}\). Dividing the telescoping sum by \(t\) gives

\[\frac{f(a + tu) - f(a)}{t} = \sum_{k=1}^{n} u_k \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_k}(q_k).\]

Each \(q_k\) lies in \(B\) and satisfies \(|q_k - a| \le |t|\), so \(q_k \to a\) as \(t \to 0\). Since \(\partial f/\partial x_k\) is continuous at \(a\), \(\partial f/\partial x_k(q_k) \to \partial f/\partial x_k(a)\). The finite sum therefore converges to \(\sum_k u_k\,\partial f/\partial x_k(a)\), which is the claim. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.3 (the function of Lab 1 at \(a = (1, 0.5)\))

Under Assumption 1, the function of Example 1.2 has continuous partial derivatives everywhere, so Theorem 2.2 applies at \(a = (1, 0.5)\) with any ball about \(a\). For the direction \(u = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), under Assumption 1 the directional derivative is \(D_u f(a) = \cos\theta + (1 + \cos 0.5)\sin\theta\). Lab 1 checks this on 360 directions by a central difference along the line.

3The steepest direction

Theorem 3.1 (steepest ascent in the Euclidean norm)

Let \(f\) satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem 2.2 at \(a\), and let \(g = \nabla f(a) \ne 0\). Among unit vectors \(u\), the directional derivative \(D_u f(a)\) is largest exactly when \(u = g/|g|\), and its largest value is \(|g|\).

Proof

By Theorem 2.2, \(D_u f(a) = u \cdot g\) for each unit \(u\). Since \(|u| = 1\), expanding the square gives \(|g - (u \cdot g)u|^2 = |g|^2 - 2(u \cdot g)^2 + (u \cdot g)^2 = |g|^2 - (u \cdot g)^2\). The left side is non-negative, so \((u \cdot g)^2 \le |g|^2\), and therefore \(u \cdot g \le |g|\). If \(u \cdot g = |g|\), the left side above is zero, so \(g = (u \cdot g)u = |g|\,u\), which gives \(u = g/|g|\). Conversely, for \(u = g/|g|\), \(u \cdot g = |g|^2/|g| = |g|\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 3.1 uses the Euclidean length of the unit vectors. Section 4 shows that a different norm on the directions gives a different steepest direction.

Lab 1 · The directional derivative equals the gradient dotted with the unit vector

Predicted before running. For \(f(x, y) = x^2 y + \sin y\) at \(a = (1, 0.5)\), Example 1.2 (under Assumption 1) gives \(\nabla f(a) = (1, 1.8775826)\), with length \(2.1272791\) and direction \(61.96\) degrees. Theorem 2.2 predicts that each central difference along the line through \(a\) in direction \(u\), with step \(t = 10^{-6}\), agrees with \(\nabla f(a) \cdot u\) up to the truncation and rounding errors of Lesson L13, which are of order \(10^{-9}\) or less here. Theorem 3.1 predicts that the largest of the 360 sampled values occurs at the sampled angle nearest to \(61.96\) degrees, which is \(62\) degrees, and that the largest sampled value is close to \(|\nabla f(a)|\).

"""M2 lab L17: the directional derivative equals the gradient dotted with the unit vector.

Standard library only. The function is F(x, y) = x^2 y + sin(y), with gradient
(2 x y, x^2 + cos y). At the point a = (1, 0.5) the lab takes 360 unit vectors u at angles of one
degree. For each u it compares a central difference of F along the line a + t u, with t = 1e-6,
against the dot product of the gradient with u. It then finds the angle at which the central
difference is largest and reports it beside the angle of the gradient. Nothing is random.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def big_f(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """F(x, y) = x^2 y + sin(y)."""
    return x * x * y + math.sin(y)


def gradient(x: float, y: float) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """The gradient (F_x, F_y) = (2 x y, x^2 + cos y)."""
    return (2.0 * x * y, x * x + math.cos(y))


def unit(angle_deg: int) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """The unit vector at the given angle in degrees, measured from the positive x-axis."""
    theta = math.radians(angle_deg)
    return (math.cos(theta), math.sin(theta))


def directional_central(a: tuple[float, float], u: tuple[float, float], t: float) -> float:
    """(F(a + t u) - F(a - t u)) / (2 t), a central difference along the line through a in direction u."""
    x0, y0 = a
    ux, uy = u
    plus = big_f(x0 + t * ux, y0 + t * uy)
    minus = big_f(x0 - t * ux, y0 - t * uy)
    return (plus - minus) / (2.0 * t)


def main() -> None:
    a = (1.0, 0.5)
    t = 1e-6
    gx, gy = gradient(*a)
    worst = 0.0
    best_angle = 0
    best_value = -math.inf
    for angle in range(360):
        u = unit(angle)
        numeric = directional_central(a, u, t)
        exact = gx * u[0] + gy * u[1]
        worst = max(worst, abs(numeric - exact))
        if numeric > best_value:
            best_value = numeric
            best_angle = angle
    grad_angle = math.degrees(math.atan2(gy, gx))
    magnitude = math.hypot(gx, gy)
    print("directions tested: 360, one degree apart")
    print(f"max error of central difference against gradient dot u below 1e-6: {worst < 1e-6}")
    print(f"gradient at (1, 0.5): ({gx:.6f}, {gy:.6f})")
    print(f"gradient magnitude: {magnitude:.6f}")
    print(f"angle of the gradient: {grad_angle:.2f} degrees")
    print(f"sampled direction of largest directional derivative: {best_angle} degrees")
    agrees = abs(best_value - magnitude) < 1e-4
    print(f"largest sampled directional derivative agrees with the magnitude to 1e-4: {agrees}")

4Where the canon breaks

The gradient formula of Theorem 2.2 has a hypothesis, and the steepest direction of Theorem 3.1 depends on a choice of norm.

  • Existence in some directions does not give the gradient formula. Take \(g(x, y) = xy/(x^2 + y^2)\) with \(g(0, 0) = 0\), the function of Lesson L15. Along \(u = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\), \(g(tu) = \cos\theta\sin\theta\) for every \(t \ne 0\). So \((g(tu) - g(0))/t = \cos\theta\sin\theta/t\), which has no limit when \(\cos\theta\sin\theta \ne 0\). The directional derivative exists only in the four axis directions, where it is \(0\). The partial derivatives at the origin are both \(0\), by Proposition 3.1 of Lesson L16, so the gradient at the origin is \(0\). The formula would give \(0\) for the diagonal directions, where no directional derivative exists to compare with. Problem R2 gives a different function whose directional derivatives exist in every direction, and they are not linear in \(u\), so no gradient formula describes them.
  • The formula can fail without the continuity hypothesis. Section 2 proves the formula under the hypothesis that the partials are continuous at \(a\). Problem R2 gives a function that violates that continuity at the origin, and whose directional derivative there is not linear in \(u\), so the formula does not hold for it.
  • Steepest depends on the norm. Theorem 3.1 measures steepness by the Euclidean length of the direction. Problem R1 uses the largest-coordinate norm, \(\max(|u_1|, |u_2|)\). At \(a = (1, 0.5)\) the steepest unit direction for that norm is the corner \((1, 1)\), at \(45\) degrees, not the gradient direction at \(61.96\) degrees. The choice of norm is part of the statement.
  • Numerical directional derivatives need the step analysis of Lesson L13. The lab's central difference along a line has the truncation and rounding errors of Lesson L13. At the step used in Lab 1 the rounding part is of order \(10^{-10}\) and the truncation part about \(10^{-12}\) (Problem E2).

5Misconceptions

  1. “The gradient says nothing about directions other than the coordinate axes.”

    Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2.2, the gradient determines \(D_u f(a)\) for every unit \(u\), as the dot product \(\nabla f(a) \cdot u\). Under Assumption 1, Example 2.3 gives the formula for the function of Lab 1 in every direction.

  2. “If the partial derivatives exist, every directional derivative exists.”

    Section 4 gives a function, \(g\) of Lesson L15, whose partials exist at the origin and whose directional derivatives fail to exist for every direction with \(\cos\theta\sin\theta \ne 0\). Existence of the partials does not give existence of the others.

  3. “The gradient points in the steepest direction whatever the norm.”

    Theorem 3.1 proves it for the Euclidean norm. Section 4 shows that the largest-coordinate norm gives a different steepest direction, so the statement depends on the norm used to measure directions.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · The gradient of a polynomial

Find \(\nabla f(1, 2)\) for \(f(x, y) = x^2 + 3xy\).

Solution

\(\partial f/\partial x = 2x + 3y\) and \(\partial f/\partial y = 3x\). At \((1, 2)\) these are \(2 + 6 = 8\) and \(3\). So \(\nabla f(1, 2) = (8, 3)\).

M2 · A directional derivative from the gradient

For \(f\) of Problem M1 and the unit vector \(u = (3/5, 4/5)\), compute \(D_u f(1, 2)\) from Theorem 2.2.

Solution

\(D_u f(1, 2) = \nabla f(1, 2) \cdot u = 8 \cdot \tfrac35 + 3 \cdot \tfrac45 = \tfrac{24}{5} + \tfrac{12}{5} = \tfrac{36}{5} = 7.2\). The partials are continuous, so Theorem 2.2 applies.

M3 · The steepest direction for a quadratic

For \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) at \((3, 4)\), find the gradient, its length, and the unit vector of steepest ascent.

Solution

\(\nabla f = (2x, 2y) = (6, 8)\), of length \(10\). By Theorem 3.1 the unit vector of steepest ascent is \(g/|g| = (6/10, 8/10) = (3/5, 4/5)\), which points away from the origin, as expected for a bowl.

Proof

P1 · The two-variable case of Theorem 2.2

Write out the proof of Theorem 2.2 for \(n = 2\), and state the point \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) at which the partial derivatives are evaluated.

Solution

Let \(u = (u_1, u_2)\). The telescoping sum is \(f(a + tu) - f(a) = [f(a_1 + t u_1, a_2) - f(a_1, a_2)] + [f(a_1 + tu_1, a_2 + t u_2) - f(a_1 + t u_1, a_2)]\). The first bracket is \(t u_1\,\partial_1 f(q_1)\) with \(q_1 = (a_1 + \theta_1 t u_1, a_2)\), by the mean value theorem in the first variable. The second bracket is \(t u_2\,\partial_2 f(q_2)\) with \(q_2 = (a_1 + t u_1, a_2 + \theta_2 t u_2)\). Both \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) are within \(|t|\) of \(a\), so both tend to \(a\), and continuity of the partials at \(a\) gives the limit \(u_1 \partial_1 f(a) + u_2 \partial_2 f(a)\).

P2 · The steepest direction in the plane

Let \(g = |g|(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)\) be a non-zero vector in \(\R^2\), and let \(u = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\). Show that \(u \cdot g = |g|\cos(\theta - \varphi)\), and use it to find the maximizing \(\theta\).

Solution

\(u \cdot g = |g|(\cos\theta\cos\varphi + \sin\theta\sin\varphi) = |g|\cos(\theta - \varphi)\) by the subtraction formula for cosine. The cosine is largest, with value \(1\), when \(\theta - \varphi\) is a multiple of \(2\pi\). So the maximum is \(|g|\), attained at \(\theta = \varphi\), which is \(u = g/|g|\). This agrees with Theorem 3.1.

P3 · A linear function

Let \(f(x, y) = x + 2y\). Prove directly from Definition 2.1 that \(D_u f(a) = u_1 + 2u_2\) for every point \(a\) and every unit vector \(u\), and check it against \(\nabla f \cdot u\).

Solution

\(f(a + tu) - f(a) = (a_1 + tu_1 + 2a_2 + 2tu_2) - (a_1 + 2a_2) = t(u_1 + 2u_2)\). Dividing by \(t\) gives \(u_1 + 2u_2\) for every \(t \ne 0\), so the limit is \(u_1 + 2u_2\). The gradient is \(\nabla f = (1, 2)\), and \(\nabla f \cdot u = u_1 + 2u_2\), which agrees.

Empirical

E1 · The steepest sampled direction (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the angle of the gradient as \(61.96\) degrees and the largest sampled direction as \(62\) degrees. Explain why these agree, and what the agreement does not show.

Solution

Among the integer angles, the directional derivative \(\cos(\theta - 61.96^\circ)\) times \(|g|\) is largest at the integer nearest \(61.96\), which is \(62\). So the sampled maximum must be at \(62\) degrees, and the printed values agree by Theorem 3.1. The agreement does not show the exact maximizing angle, which is \(61.96\) degrees, because the grid is one degree wide. It also does not show anything about the angles between the samples.

E2 · Error sizes of the central difference (Lab 1)

Lab 1 uses step \(t = 10^{-6}\) for the central difference along the line. Estimate the truncation and rounding parts of the error for this function at \(a\), and say whether the printed agreement within \(10^{-6}\) is consistent with them.

Solution

The truncation part is \(\tfrac{t^2}{6}\) times the third derivative of \(\varphi(s) = f(a + s u)\). Under Assumption 1, the only non-zero third partial derivatives of \(f = x^2 y + \sin y\) are \(\partial^3 f/\partial x^2\partial y = 2\) and \(\partial^3 f/\partial y^3 = -\cos y\), so \(\varphi''' = 6u_1^2 u_2 - u_2^3 \cos y\), bounded in absolute value by \(6 + |\cos y| \le 6 + 1 = 7\) (using \(|\cos y| \le 1\) from Assumption 1) for \(y\) near \(a\). The truncation part is at most \(\tfrac{10^{-12}}{6} \cdot 7 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-12}\). The rounding part is \(\delta/t\), where \(\delta\) is about the half spacing of doubles near \(|f| \approx 1\), which is about \(10^{-16}\). So the rounding part is of order \(10^{-10}\). Both are below \(10^{-6}\), so the printed agreement is consistent with them.

E3 · The length check (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the length of the gradient as \(2.127279\) and states that the largest sampled directional derivative agrees with it to \(10^{-4}\). Explain why the sampled maximum is slightly below the length, and why the difference is below \(10^{-4}\) at a 1-degree grid.

Solution

The sampled maximum is \(|g|\cos\delta\) where \(\delta\) is the angle between the best sample and the gradient direction. The best sample is at \(62\) degrees, so \(\delta = 0.0398\) degrees, which is \(6.9 \times 10^{-4}\) radians. Then \(1 - \cos\delta \approx \delta^2/2 = 2.4 \times 10^{-7}\), and \(|g|(1 - \cos\delta) \approx 5 \times 10^{-7}\). That is below \(10^{-4}\), which is why the check is True.

Research

R1 · Steepness in the largest-coordinate norm

Measure directions by the largest-coordinate norm, so that the unit vectors satisfy \(\max(|u_1|, |u_2|) = 1\). Under Assumption 1, at \(a = (1, 0.5)\), with \(\nabla f(a) = (1, 1.8775826)\), find the unit vector that maximizes \(D_u f(a) = \nabla f(a) \cdot u\), and compare it with the gradient direction.

Solution

For \(u\) in this unit ball, \(\nabla f \cdot u = u_1 + 1.8775826\,u_2\). Its largest value over \(\max(|u_1|,|u_2|) \le 1\) is \(|1| + |1.8775826| = 2.8775826\), attained at the corner \(u = (1, 1)\). The Euclidean gradient direction is \(g/|g| = (0.4701, 0.8826)\). Rescaled to the largest-coordinate unit ball, it becomes \((0.5326, 1)\), with value \(0.5326 + 1.8776 = 2.4102\) (unrounded \(2.410182\)), which is below \(2.8776\). So the steepest direction in this norm is \((1, 1)\), at \(45\) degrees, not \(61.96\) degrees. The statement of steepest ascent depends on the norm chosen for directions.

R2 · A directional derivative that is not linear

Let \(f(x, y) = x^2 y/(x^2 + y^4)\) for \((x, y) \ne (0, 0)\), and \(f(0, 0) = 0\). Show that both partial derivatives at the origin are \(0\), and that \(D_u f(0) = \sin\theta\) when \(\cos\theta \ne 0\), while \(D_{(0, 1)} f(0) = 0\). Explain why this shows that Theorem 2.2 does not apply at the origin.

Solution

On the \(x\)-axis \(f(t, 0) = 0\) and on the \(y\)-axis \(f(0, t) = 0\), so both partials at the origin are \(0\). For \(u = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\) with \(\cos\theta \ne 0\) and \(t \ne 0\), \(f(tu) = t\cos^2\theta\sin\theta/(\cos^2\theta + t^2\sin^4\theta)\), so \((f(tu) - 0)/t \to \sin\theta\) as \(t \to 0\). For \(u = (0, 1)\), \(f(0, t) = 0\), so \(D_{(0,1)}f(0) = 0\), while \(\sin(\pi/2) = 1\). If Theorem 2.2 applied, the directional derivative would be \(\nabla f(0)\cdot u = 0\) for every \(u\), which fails for \(\cos\theta \ne 0\) and \(\sin\theta \ne 0\). So its hypotheses fail at the origin. The partials are not continuous there: for \((x, y) \ne (0, 0)\), \(\partial f/\partial x = 2xy^5/(x^2 + y^4)^2\), and on the curve \(x = y^2\) this equals \(1/(2y)\), which is unbounded as \(y \to 0\).

R3 · Gradient descent steps

A method moves from \(a\) to \(a - \eta\,\nabla f(a)\) for a step \(\eta > 0\). Using Theorem 3.1, state the direction of this step, and say which step sizes make the first-order change in \(f\) negative, under the hypotheses of Theorem 2.2.

Solution

The step is along \(-\nabla f(a)\), which by Theorem 3.1 applied to \(-u\) is the direction of steepest descent in the Euclidean norm. The first-order change in \(f\) is \(\nabla f(a) \cdot (-\eta\nabla f(a)) = -\eta|\nabla f(a)|^2\), which is negative for every \(\eta > 0\) when \(\nabla f(a) \ne 0\). This first-order statement is the directional derivative. Whether a particular step size reduces \(f\) by the full amount depends on the curvature of \(f\), which this lesson does not analyse.

    Theorem 2.2 states both hypotheses, and Section 4 shows the formula can fail without them.

  6. For \(g(x, y) = xy/(x^2 + y^2)\), what does the directional derivative at the origin do for the direction \((1, 1)/\sqrt2\)?

    Options

    On the diagonal \(g(tu) = 1/2\), so the quotient is \(1/(2t)\), which is unbounded as \(t \to 0\).

  7. Under the largest-coordinate norm, what is the steepest direction at \((1, 0.5)\) for the function of Lab 1?

  8. For \(f(x, y) = x + 2y\), what is \(D_u f(a)\) when \(u = (1, 0)\)?

    \(D_{(1,0)} f = \partial f/\partial x = 1\) for this function. Check: \(u_1 + 2u_2 = 1 + 0 = 1\) (Problem P3).

  9. Which statement about the directional derivative at the origin for \(g\) is correct?
