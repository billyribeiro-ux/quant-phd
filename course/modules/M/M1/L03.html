Exponential and Logarithm Functions

  • Uses: direct proof (Lesson 2), program notation conventions (P0.1, not yet released), and secondary-school algebra of powers, which this lesson uses as prose
  • Introduces: exponential and logarithm functions
  • Ttheorem

  • Bpublished, not settled

Continuously compounded growth and discounting are exponentials of time, and the question "how long until the amount doubles?" is a logarithm. Solving for the time, or for the rate that produces a given growth factor, is an identity read backwards. This lesson states the three properties of the exponential that the unit takes as given, derives the other rules it uses from them, and checks the identities numerically in Lab 1. The checks test the floating-point functions, and the proofs establish the identities.

1The properties taken as given

Assumptions A1 to A3 (the exponential)

The unit takes the exponential function \(x \mapsto e^x\), from the real numbers to the real numbers, as given with three properties:

  • A1 (positivity). \(e^x > 0\) for every real \(x\).
  • A2 (addition). \(e^{a + b} = e^a e^b\) for all real \(a\) and \(b\).
  • A3 (inverse). For every \(y > 0\) there is exactly one real \(x\) with \(e^x = y\).

The unit does not construct the exponential here, by a series or by any other method. Every result below follows from A1 to A3 and the definitions. The number \(e\) is defined as \(e^1\).

Definition 1.1 (natural logarithm)

For \(y > 0\), the natural logarithm \(\ln y\) is the unique real \(x\) with \(e^x = y\). It is defined by A3 and is defined only for positive arguments. The exponential and the logarithm are the two functions of this lesson.

Proposition 1.2 (the exponential of zero)

\(e^0 = 1\).

Proof

By A2 with \(a = b = 0\), \(e^0 = e^{0 + 0} = e^0 e^0\). Write \(u = e^0\), so \(u = u^2\) and \(u(u - 1) = 0\). By A1, \(u > 0\), so \(u \ne 0\), and therefore \(u - 1 = 0\). Hence \(e^0 = 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.3 (negative exponents)

For every real \(x\), \(e^{-x} = 1/e^x\).

Proof

By A2, \(e^x e^{-x} = e^{x + (-x)} = e^0\), and \(e^0 = 1\) by Proposition 1.2. So \(e^x e^{-x} = 1\). By A1, \(e^x \ne 0\), so dividing both sides by \(e^x\) gives \(e^{-x} = 1/e^x\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.4 (the logarithm inverts the exponential)

For every real \(x\), \(\ln(e^x) = x\). For every \(y > 0\), \(e^{\ln y} = y\).

Proof

Let \(x\) be real. By A1, \(e^x > 0\), so \(\ln(e^x)\) is defined and is the unique real \(z\) with \(e^z = e^x\). The number \(x\) itself has this property, so \(z = x\) by uniqueness, and \(\ln(e^x) = x\). For \(y > 0\), the definition of \(\ln y\) says that \(e^{\ln y} = y\) directly. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.5 (equal exponentials have equal exponents)

If \(e^u = e^v\) for real \(u\) and \(v\), then \(u = v\).

Proof

Apply \(\ln\) to both sides. By Proposition 1.4, \(\ln(e^u) = u\) and \(\ln(e^v) = v\), and the two sides are equal, so \(u = v\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.6 (rules for the logarithm)

(a) \(\ln 1 = 0\). (b) For \(a, b > 0\), \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\). (c) For \(y > 0\), \(\ln(1/y) = -\ln y\).

Proof

(a) By Proposition 1.2, \(e^0 = 1\), so \(0\) is a real number with \(e^0 = 1\). By the uniqueness in A3, \(\ln 1 = 0\). (b) Let \(x = \ln a\) and \(z = \ln b\), which exist because \(a, b > 0\). By Proposition 1.4, \(e^x = a\) and \(e^z = b\). By A2, \(e^{x + z} = e^x e^z = ab\). So \(x + z\) is a real number whose exponential is \(ab\), and by uniqueness \(\ln(ab) = x + z = \ln a + \ln b\). (c) Apply (b) with \(a = y\) and \(b = 1/y\). The left side is \(\ln 1 = 0\) by (a), so \(\ln y + \ln(1/y) = 0\), which gives \(\ln(1/y) = -\ln y\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.7 (the exponential of a double rate)

For every real \(u\), \(e^{2u} = (e^u)^2\). So if the rate is doubled and the time is held fixed, the growth factor \(e^{rt}\) of the rate \(r\) over time \(t\) is squared.

Proof

By A2 with \(a = b = u\), \(e^{2u} = e^{u + u} = e^u e^u = (e^u)^2\). Put \(u = rt\). Then the factor at rate \(2r\) after time \(t\) is \(e^{2rt} = (e^{rt})^2\), which is the square of the factor at rate \(r\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Doubling time and the rate of a growth factor

Proposition 2.1 (doubling time)

Let \(r > 0\) and let \(t\) be real. Then \(e^{rt} = 2\) holds exactly when \(t = (\ln 2)/r\).

Proof

By Proposition 1.4 and the uniqueness in A3, \(e^{u} = 2\) holds exactly when \(u = \ln 2\). Take \(u = rt\). Then \(e^{rt} = 2\) holds exactly when \(rt = \ln 2\). Since \(r > 0\), \(rt = \ln 2\) holds exactly when \(t = (\ln 2)/r\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.2 (the rate that produces a factor)

Let \(F > 0\) be a growth factor after time \(t > 0\). Then \(F = e^{rt}\) holds exactly when \(r = (\ln F)/t\).

Proof

By Proposition 1.4, \(e^{rt} = F\) holds exactly when \(rt = \ln F\), by the uniqueness in A3. Dividing by \(t > 0\) gives \(r = (\ln F)/t\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.3 (a rate of 5 percent over ten years)

At a continuously compounded rate of 0.05 for 10 years the growth factor is \(e^{0.5}\), by Proposition 2.2 read forward. Lab 1 prints its value to four decimals. The factor is a number computed from the exponential, and the lesson does not evaluate it by hand. The growth it represents is the factor minus 1.

Remark 2.4 (a rate needs its convention)

The growth factor after one year is \(e^r\) under continuous compounding and \(1 + i\) under an annual effective rate \(i\). The two conventions can give different factors for the same stated number, so a rate is meaningful only with its convention named. The conversion between them is the subject of Lesson 4.

Lab 1 · The exponential and logarithm identities on a grid

Predicted from Propositions 1.2 to 2.2 before running. On the grid \(-3, -2.5, \dots, 3\) there are 13 values, so the addition rule for the exponential should hold for \(13^2 = 169\) ordered pairs, and \(\ln(e^x) = x\) for all 13 values. On the six positive values \(0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 10, 100\), \(e^{\ln y} = y\) holds for all six. The product rule \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\) should hold for \(6^2 = 36\) pairs. At a rate of 0.05 the doubling time is \((\ln 2)/0.05 = 20 \ln 2\) years, and \(e^{rt}\) at that time should equal 2. The factor after 10 years is \(e^{0.5}\).

"""M1 lab L03: the exponential and logarithm identities, checked to a stated tolerance.

Standard library only. No randomness and no network. The identities are checked on finite grids of
inputs with math.exp and math.log, which are floating-point approximations. A check on a grid is
numerical evidence that the identity holds to the tolerance on that grid. It does not prove the
identity, and the proofs on the page establish the identities from the stated properties.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

TOL = 1e-12


def close(x: float, y: float) -> bool:
    """True when |x - y| <= TOL * max(1, |y|): an absolute tolerance near 1, a relative one above 1."""
    return abs(x - y) <= TOL * max(1.0, abs(y))


def grid(lo: float, hi: float, step: float) -> list[float]:
    """Evenly spaced values from lo to hi inclusive, as lo + i * step for integers i >= 0."""
    count = round((hi - lo) / step)
    return [lo + i * step for i in range(count + 1)]


def product_rule(values: list[float]) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """Count pairs (a, b) from values where exp(a + b) is close to exp(a) * exp(b). Returns (holds, pairs)."""
    holds = pairs = 0
    for a in values:
        for b in values:
            pairs += 1
            holds += close(math.exp(a + b), math.exp(a) * math.exp(b))
    return holds, pairs


def log_of_exp(values: list[float]) -> int:
    """Count x in values with log(exp(x)) close to x."""
    return sum(close(math.log(math.exp(x)), x) for x in values)


def exp_of_log(values: list[float]) -> int:
    """Count y in values (all positive) with exp(log(y)) close to y."""
    return sum(close(math.exp(math.log(y)), y) for y in values)


def log_product(values: list[float]) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """Count pairs (a, b) of positive values with log(a * b) close to log(a) + log(b). Returns (holds, pairs)."""
    holds = pairs = 0
    for a in values:
        for b in values:
            pairs += 1
            holds += close(math.log(a * b), math.log(a) + math.log(b))
    return holds, pairs


def doubling_time(rate: float) -> float:
    """The time t with exp(rate * t) = 2, namely log(2) / rate, for a continuously compounded rate > 0."""
    if rate <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"doubling_time needs a rate above 0, got {rate}")
    return math.log(2.0) / rate


def main() -> None:
    xs = grid(-3.0, 3.0, 0.5)
    holds, pairs = product_rule(xs)
    print(f"grid for x: {len(xs)} values from -3 to 3 in steps of 0.5")
    print(f"exp(a + b) equals exp(a) * exp(b) to tolerance: {holds} of {pairs} pairs")
    print(f"log(exp(x)) equals x to tolerance: {log_of_exp(xs)} of {len(xs)} values")

    ys = [0.1, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 10.0, 100.0]
    print(f"exp(log(y)) equals y to tolerance: {exp_of_log(ys)} of {len(ys)} values")
    holds, pairs = log_product(ys)
    print(f"log(a * b) equals log(a) + log(b) to tolerance: {holds} of {pairs} pairs")

    rate = 0.05
    t = doubling_time(rate)
    print(f"continuously compounded rate {rate}: doubling time log(2)/rate = {t:.4f} years")
    print(f"exp(rate * t) at that time, to 4 decimals: {math.exp(rate * t):.4f}")
    print(f"exp(rate * t) equals 2 to tolerance: {close(math.exp(rate * t), 2.0)}")
    growth = math.exp(0.05 * 10)
    print(f"exp(0.05 * 10), the growth factor after 10 years at rate 0.05: {growth:.4f}")

3Where the canon breaks

This unit starts from three assumptions, and four points follow from that choice.

  • The starting properties are assumed. A1 to A3 are taken as given, and the unit does not show that a function with these properties exists or that it is unique up to the stated form. A reader who wants that construction must go to a source outside this lesson.
  • Floating-point identities are approximate. math.exp and math.log return binary floating-point approximations, so an identity holds to a tolerance and not exactly. Lab 1's tolerance is a statement about the implementation, and it is not a statement about the mathematics.
  • A rate without its convention is ambiguous. Remark 2.4 shows that the same number can be a continuous rate or an effective annual rate, with different factors. A quoted rate should carry its convention, and a rate read without one has no determined factor.
  • The logarithm exists only for positive arguments. Definition 1.1 defines \(\ln y\) for \(y > 0\) only. A computation that takes the logarithm of zero or of a negative number has left the domain of the function, and the code of Lab 1 never does it.

4Misconceptions

  1. “The logarithm turns a sum into a sum, so \(\ln(a + b) = \ln a + \ln b\).”

    The logarithm turns a product into a sum: \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\), by Proposition 1.6(b). It does not turn a sum into a sum. Take \(a = b = 1\). Then \(\ln(a + b) = \ln 2\), while \(\ln a + \ln b = 0\), and \(\ln 2 \ne 0\) because \(e^0 = 1 \ne 2\) by Proposition 1.2 and the uniqueness in A3.

  2. “Doubling the rate doubles the growth factor.”

    Proposition 1.7 gives \(e^{2rt} = (e^{rt})^2\), so the factor at the doubled rate is the square of the old factor. The factor is not doubled. Proposition 2.1 shows the consequence for doubling time: the time to reach a factor of 2 at rate \(2r\) is half the time at rate \(r\), since \((\ln 2)/(2r)\) is half of \((\ln 2)/r\).

  3. “\(\ln(1/y)\) equals \(1/\ln y\).”

    Proposition 1.6(c) gives \(\ln(1/y) = -\ln y\), with a minus sign and no reciprocal. The reciprocal of the logarithm is a different function. The proof uses \(\ln 1 = 0\) from Proposition 1.6(a) and the product rule, and Lab 1 checks the product rule on 36 pairs of positive values.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · Solving for the time

Solve \(e^{5t} = 3\) for \(t\) in closed form.

Solution

By Proposition 1.4 and the uniqueness in A3, \(e^{5t} = 3\) holds exactly when \(5t = \ln 3\). Dividing by 5 gives \(t = (\ln 3)/5\). The answer is a closed form, and the lesson does not evaluate it by hand.

M2 · Solving for the rate

A factor of \(1.5\) is reached after 4 years at a continuously compounded rate \(r\). Find \(r\) in closed form.

Solution

By Proposition 2.2 with \(F = 1.5\) and \(t = 4\), \(r = (\ln 1.5)/4\). This is the continuously compounded rate, and the convention in Remark 2.4 applies to it.

M3 · Simplifying a product of exponentials

Simplify \(e^{a} e^{-a} e^{3}\) for real \(a\), giving the result in a form with no product of exponentials.

Solution

By A2, \(e^a e^{-a} = e^{a + (-a)} = e^0\), which is 1 by Proposition 1.2. So the product is \(1 \cdot e^3 = e^3\). The result does not depend on \(a\).

Proof

P1 · Exponent of a difference

Prove that \(e^{x - y} = e^x / e^y\) for all real \(x\) and \(y\).

Solution

By A2, \(e^{x - y} e^y = e^{(x - y) + y} = e^x\). Since \(e^y > 0\) by A1, divide both sides by \(e^y\) to get \(e^{x - y} = e^x / e^y\). This is a direct proof in the sense of Lesson 2, Definition 1.2.

P2 · The logarithm of a reciprocal

Prove that \(\ln(1/y) = -\ln y\) for \(y > 0\) directly from Propositions 1.4 and 1.6(a), without citing the product rule as a black box.

Solution

Let \(x = \ln y\) and \(z = \ln(1/y)\). By Proposition 1.4, \(e^x = y\) and \(e^z = 1/y\). By A2, \(e^{x + z} = e^x e^z = y \cdot (1/y) = 1\). Also \(e^0 = 1\) by Proposition 1.2, so \(e^{x + z} = e^0\), and Proposition 1.5 gives \(x + z = 0\). So \(z = -x\), which is \(\ln(1/y) = -\ln y\). This repeats the argument behind Proposition 1.6(c) at the level of exponentials.

P3 · Uniqueness of the exponent

Prove Proposition 1.5 as stated, and say which of A1 to A3 it uses.

Solution

Suppose \(e^u = e^v\). The number \(e^u\) is positive by A1, so \(\ln(e^u)\) is defined. By Proposition 1.4, \(\ln(e^u) = u\) and \(\ln(e^v) = v\). Since \(e^u = e^v\), the two logarithms are the same number, so \(u = v\). The argument uses A1 to make the logarithm defined and A3 for the uniqueness that defines it.

Empirical

E1 · The percentage gain from the printed factor (formula input)

Lab 1 prints the factor \(e^{0.5}\) as 1.6487 for a rate of 0.05 over 10 years. What percentage gain does the printed factor represent, to one decimal?

Solution

The gain is the factor minus 1, as a fraction of the principal of 1. From the unrounded factor, \(e^{0.5} - 1 = 0.6487213\), a gain of 64.87 percent, which is 64.9 percent to one decimal. From the printed value, \(1.6487 - 1 = 0.6487\), which is also 64.9 percent to one decimal. The one-decimal answer does not depend on which of the two values is used.

E2 · Checking the doubling time (formula input)

Lab 1 prints a doubling time of 13.8629 years at a rate of 0.05. Show that this equals \(20 \ln 2\) to four decimals, using only the lab's own computation of \(\ln 2\).

Solution

By Proposition 2.1, the doubling time at rate 0.05 is \((\ln 2)/0.05 = 20 \ln 2\). The lab computes \(\ln 2\) with math.log(2.0) and divides by the rate. A reader can run 20 * math.log(2.0) and see the printed 13.8629, which is the same number to four decimals. The check is a comparison of the implementation with the formula, and it needs no value typed into the page.

E3 · How many pairs the addition check tests (finite grid)

Lab 1 prints 169 of 169 pairs for the addition rule. Explain the 169 from the grid of inputs and confirm it.

Solution

The grid runs from \(-3\) to \(3\) in steps of \(0.5\), which gives \((3 - (-3))/0.5 + 1 = 13\) values. Ordered pairs of them number \(13^2 = 169\). The lab tests each ordered pair \((a, b)\), so the count of 169 is the count of pairs, and all 169 agree to tolerance.

Research

R1 · Why a grid check is not a proof

Lab 1's tolerance check passes on 169 pairs. Explain why this does not establish the addition rule for all real inputs, and say which result in the lesson does.

Solution

The grid is a finite set of real inputs, and the check tests the floating-point function on that set, to a tolerance. The addition rule concerns every real pair, including pairs no grid contains and pairs where an approximate value would differ. Proposition 1.2 and the derivation of the rules from A1 to A3 establish the identity for all real inputs, given the three assumptions. The grid check is consistent with those proofs and does not replace them.

R2 · Replacing the addition rule with a sum rule

Suppose a reader replaces A2 by the rule \(e^{a + b} = e^a + e^b\). Show that A1 then fails, so the replacement is inconsistent with the positivity the lesson assumes.

Solution

Put \(a = b = 0\) in the replacement: \(e^0 = e^0 + e^0\), so \(e^0 = 0\). But A1 requires \(e^0 > 0\), so the replacement contradicts A1. The reader's rule and the positivity assumption cannot both hold, so the sum rule cannot replace A2 in this unit.

R3 · Why the product rule is a theorem here

Explain why \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\) is a theorem in this lesson and not a definition of the logarithm.

Solution

The logarithm is defined by A3 as the unique real \(x\) with \(e^x = y\). The product rule is then derived: the sum \(\ln a + \ln b\) is a real number whose exponential, by A2, is \(ab\), and uniqueness identifies it as \(\ln(ab)\). The rule depends on the addition property of the exponential, so it is a consequence of A2 and A3 together, and a reader who changed A2 would lose it.
