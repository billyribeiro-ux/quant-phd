Compounding frequency and continuous compounding

A quoted interest rate states how often interest is credited, and that changes how fast a balance grows. A 5% nominal rate (the rate quoted per year before compounding, defined in §1) credited monthly gives a one-year growth of 0.0511618979, or 5.1162% a year, not 5% (Part A of Lab 1). This lesson fixes the compounding factors that L05 uses: the periodic factor, the effective annual rate, and the continuous factor \(e^{rt}\), each defined in §1. For a positive rate, it proves that more frequent compounding raises the one-year factor, and it shows that the factor stays below \(e^{r}\) under an assumption stated in §2. Lesson L05 turns the factor into a present value.

1Nominal rates, periods and the effective annual rate

Money is held in currency units (CU). One CU is 100 cents, and amounts in cents are whole numbers. Rates are decimals, so 5% is 0.05. Let \(r\) be the nominal annual rate, the rate quoted per year before any compounding. Let \(m\) be a whole number of equal compounding periods in a year, and let \(t\) be a time in years.

Definition 1.1 (compounding frequency and the effective annual rate)

Interest is credited \(m\) times a year, each time at the rate \(r/m\) on the balance at the start of that period. Over one year, one CU grows to the periodic factor \(F_m(r) = (1 + r/m)^m\). The compounding frequency and the effective annual rate are the frequency \(m\) and the one-year growth rate \(EAR_m(r) = F_m(r) - 1\), a decimal.

Definition 1.2 (continuous compounding)

Continuous compounding credits interest at every instant. Its factor over \(t\) years is \(e^{rt}\), where \(e\) is \(e^{1}\), the exponential function of L03. The effective annual rate of continuous compounding is \(e^{r} - 1\). This lesson defines the continuous factor. It does not derive it from a limit.

Remark 1.3 (the factor over several years)

Over \(t\) years with \(m\) periods a year, the factor is \((1 + r/m)^{mt}\), because \(mt\) is the number of periods. With \(m = 1\) the formula gives \((1 + r)^{t}\), the annual compounding factor. Lab 1 uses whole years, so that \(mt\) is a whole number of periods and the factor is a product of equal period factors.

2More frequent compounding raises the factor, and under the exponential bound stated below, stays below e^r

Proposition 2.1 uses the binomial expansion. Proposition 2.2 uses an inequality, which is stated as Assumption E in the next block. Neither uses a limit.

Assumption E (the exponential bound)

For every real \(x > 0\), \(1 + x < e^{x}\). L03 states the exponential only through A1 to A3, and this lesson does not derive E from them. E is taken here as an assumption of this page, not as a result of L03. It is used in Proposition 2.2, in the places that cite that proposition (Lab 1, Problem R2, the key-results table, quiz item 5 and the misconception in §5), and directly in Problem R3.

Proposition 2.1 (the factor increases with the compounding frequency)

Let \(r > 0\). Then \(F_{m}(r)\) is strictly increasing in \(m\) over the whole numbers \(m \ge 1\). Consequently \(EAR_{m}(r)\) is strictly increasing in \(m\).

Proof

By the binomial theorem, \(F_{m}(r) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} \binom{m}{k} (r/m)^{k}\). For \(0 \le k \le m\), \(\binom{m}{k} = \frac{m(m-1)\cdots(m-k+1)}{k!}\), so \[ F_{m}(r) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} \frac{r^{k}}{k!} \prod_{j=0}^{k-1}\left(1 - \frac{j}{m}\right). \] Compare \(F_{m}(r)\) with \(F_{m+1}(r)\). For \(k \le m\), every factor \(1 - j/m\) in the product with \(0 \le j \le k-1\) is positive, because \(j \le m - 1\). Also \(1 - j/(m+1) \ge 1 - j/m\), since \(j/(m+1) \le j/m\). A product of positive factors that are each at least as large is at least as large, so each term with \(k \le m\) is at least as large in \(F_{m+1}(r)\) as in \(F_{m}(r)\). The sum \(F_{m+1}(r)\) also has the term \(k = m + 1\), \[ \frac{r^{m+1}}{(m+1)!} \prod_{j=0}^{m}\left(1 - \frac{j}{m+1}\right). \] This term is strictly positive: \(r > 0\), and the factor for \(j = m\) equals \(1/(m+1) > 0\). Therefore \(F_{m+1}(r) > F_{m}(r)\). Since \(EAR_{m}(r) = F_{m}(r) - 1\), the effective annual rate increases with \(m\) as well.

Proposition 2.2 (the factor stays below e^r)

Under Assumption E, let \(r > 0\). For every whole number \(m \ge 1\), \(F_{m}(r) < e^{r}\).

Proof

Take \(x = r/m > 0\). Assumption E gives \(1 + r/m < e^{r/m}\), and \(0 < 1 + r/m\) because \(r > 0\). So \(0 < 1 + r/m < e^{r/m}\). Raising both sides to the positive whole power \(m\) keeps the strict inequality, since \(0 < a < b\) gives \(a^{m} < b^{m}\). It remains to show \((e^{r/m})^{m} = e^{r}\). By A2 and induction on \(k\), \(e^{k r/m} = e^{(k-1) r/m} e^{r/m}\) for \(k \ge 1\), so \(e^{m \cdot r/m} = (e^{r/m})^{m}\), that is, \(e^{r} = (e^{r/m})^{m}\). Therefore \(F_{m}(r) = (1 + r/m)^{m} < (e^{r/m})^{m} = e^{r}\).

Remark 2.3 (what these two results do not say)

Propositions 2.1 and 2.2 say that the factors rise with \(m\) and stay below \(e^{r}\). They do not say how close \(F_{m}(r)\) comes to \(e^{r}\) for a given \(m\). Part B of Lab 1 prints that gap for ten values of \(m\). The page does not prove that \(F_{m}(r)\) converges to \(e^{r}\). Part B shows the gap at those ten values, and nothing more is claimed.

Lab 1 · Compounding frequency and the continuous factor

The inputs are illustrative, not data: a nominal rate of 0.05 and a principal of 1,000,000 cents. Stated before running. Part A prints the effective annual rate at five compounding frequencies. Part B prints the one-year factor for ten values of \(m\), beside \(e^{0.05}\), with the error \(e^{0.05}\) minus the factor. Propositions 2.1 and 2.2 predict that the factor rises with \(m\), that every factor is below \(e^{0.05}\), and that every error is positive. Part C prints the ten-year value of the principal in whole cents under four conventions. The code uses no random draws, so its output is fixed.

"""M1 L04 lab 1: compounding frequency, the effective annual rate, and the factor e^(r t).

Inputs are illustrative, not data: a nominal annual rate r = 0.05 and a principal of 10,000.00 CU, which is
1,000,000 cents. No random draws are used, so every run prints the same lines.

Part A: the effective annual rate (1 + r/m)^m - 1 for m periods per year.
Part B: the one-year factor (1 + r/m)^m for growing m, beside e^r, with the error e^r minus the factor.
Part C: the value of the principal after 10 years, in whole cents, for annual, monthly, daily and continuous
compounding.

Every factor is a Decimal computed at 60 significant digits. CU is the currency unit; 1 CU is 100 cents.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from decimal import ROUND_HALF_EVEN, Decimal, getcontext

getcontext().prec = 60

RATE = Decimal("0.05")
PRINCIPAL_CENTS = 1_000_000
PERIODS = (1, 2, 4, 12, 52, 365, 1000, 10000, 100000, 1000000)


def periodic_factor(rate: Decimal, periods: int, years: int) -> Decimal:
    """Growth factor (1 + rate/periods)^(periods * years) for a nominal annual rate, equal periods, whole years."""
    return (Decimal(1) + rate / periods) ** (periods * years)


def continuous_factor(rate: Decimal, years: int) -> Decimal:
    """The continuously compounded factor e^(rate * years)."""
    return (rate * years).exp()


def to_cents(amount: Decimal) -> int:
    """The amount in whole cents, with a tie going to the even cent."""
    return int(amount.quantize(Decimal(1), rounding=ROUND_HALF_EVEN))


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A, B and C. Each line is a fixed string or a number, so the output is reproducible."""
    print("Part A: effective annual rate for a nominal 5% rate")
    for m in (1, 2, 4, 12, 365):
        ear = periodic_factor(RATE, m, 1) - 1
        print(f"periods {m} effective_annual_rate {ear:.10f}")

    print("Part B: one-year factor (1 + r/m)^m beside e^r")
    e_r = continuous_factor(RATE, 1)
    print(f"continuous_factor_one_year {e_r:.10f}")
    previous = Decimal(0)
    increasing = True
    below = True
    for m in PERIODS:
        factor = periodic_factor(RATE, m, 1)
        increasing = increasing and factor > previous
        below = below and factor < e_r
        previous = factor
        print(f"periods {m} factor {factor:.10f} error {e_r - factor:.10f}")
    print(f"factor_increases_with_periods {increasing}")
    print(f"factor_below_continuous {below}")

    print("Part C: value of 1,000,000 cents after 10 years, in whole cents")
    values: dict[str, int] = {}
    for label, m in (("annual", 1), ("monthly", 12), ("daily", 365)):
        values[label] = to_cents(PRINCIPAL_CENTS * periodic_factor(RATE, m, 10))
        print(f"{label} cents {values[label]}")
    values["continuous"] = to_cents(PRINCIPAL_CENTS * continuous_factor(RATE, 10))
    print(f"continuous cents {values['continuous']}")
    print(f"monthly_minus_annual_cents {values['monthly'] - values['annual']}")
    print(f"continuous_minus_daily_cents {values['continuous'] - values['daily']}")

3The continuous factor over time

Proposition 3.1 (time consistency of the continuous factor)

For every real \(r\) and every \(s, t \ge 0\), \(e^{r(s+t)} = e^{rs}\,e^{rt}\). In words, one CU compounded continuously for \(s\) years and then for \(t\) more years has the value of one CU compounded for \(s + t\) years.

Proof

By the sum law \(e^{a}e^{b} = e^{a+b}\) of L03, with \(a = rs\) and \(b = rt\), \(e^{rs}e^{rt} = e^{rs + rt} = e^{r(s+t)}\). Compounding for the two periods in turn multiplies the two factors, and the product is \(e^{r(s+t)}\).

Example 3.2 (a ten-year value in cents)

Take 1,000,000 cents (10,000.00 CU) at \(r = 0.05\) for \(t = 10\) years. Annual compounding gives the factor \(1.05^{10}\). With \(1.05 = 21/20\), the factor is \((21/20)^{10} = 16679880978201 / 10240000000000 = 1.62889462677744140625\) exactly. Then \(1{,}000{,}000 \times 1.62889462677744140625 = 1{,}628{,}894.6268\) cents, which is 1,628,895 cents to the nearest cent. Continuous compounding gives \(e^{0.5} = 1.6487212707\) for the same principal, and \(1{,}000{,}000 \times 1.6487212707 = 1{,}648{,}721.27\) cents, which is 1,648,721 cents. Part C of Lab 1 prints both values, with the monthly and daily values between them.

4Where the canon breaks

  • A rate quoted without its frequency. A nominal figure does not fix a one-year factor. Part A gives 0.05, 0.0506250000, 0.0509453369, 0.0511618979 and 0.0512674965 for the same nominal 5% at five frequencies. Any comparison of two quotes needs the frequency of each, or the effective annual rate.
  • Continuous compounding as a defined factor. Definition 1.2 sets the continuous factor to \(e^{rt}\). This page does not state that any account, loan or market credits interest continuously. The factor is a convention for comparing quotes. It is not a description of how any account credits interest.
  • One rate over the whole horizon. Every factor here uses one rate \(r\) for \(t\) years. A rate that changes with time needs a separate factor for each period, and this lesson does not treat it.
  • Periods that do not fit the horizon. Remark 1.3 counts periods, so it applies when \(mt\) is a whole number. For a non-whole \(mt\), the factor needs a real exponent, which this lesson does not define. It counts whole credits when \(mt\) is a whole number. Lab 1 uses whole years, so every periodic factor it prints is a product of whole periods. The continuous factors it prints are not periodic.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A 5% nominal rate compounded monthly is worth 5% a year.”

    The effective annual rate is \((1 + 0.05/12)^{12} - 1 = 0.0511618979\), which Part A of Lab 1 prints as 0.0511618979. The nominal rate is the per-year quote before compounding, and each credit earns interest in the periods after it. For a positive rate, compounding more often raises the effective rate (Proposition 2.1).

  2. “Going from daily to continuous compounding changes the value by as much as going from annual to monthly does.”

    On 1,000,000 cents for ten years at 5%, Part C of Lab 1 prints a gap of 18,114 cents from annual to monthly compounding, and 56 cents from daily to continuous (differences of the whole-cent values it prints). The one-year factor rises by 0.0001055986 from monthly (\(m = 12\)) to daily (\(m = 365\)), against 0.0011618979 from annual to monthly, which is 1.0511618979 minus 1.0500000000 (Part A and Part B of Lab 1).

  3. “More frequent compounding can push the factor above \(e^{r}\), since more credits always add value.”

    Proposition 2.2, under Assumption E, proves \(F_{m}(r) < e^{r}\) for every whole number \(m \ge 1\) and every \(r > 0\). Each extra compounding period raises the factor (Proposition 2.1), but no finite frequency reaches the continuous factor. Part B of Lab 1 shows every printed factor below \(e^{0.05}\).

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · The effective annual rate of 8% compounded monthly

A nominal rate of 8% is compounded monthly. Compute the effective annual rate \(EAR_{12}(0.08)\) to four decimal places.

Solution

Here \(r = 0.08\) and \(m = 12\), so \(EAR_{12}(0.08) = (1 + 0.08/12)^{12} - 1 = (1.0066666\ldots)^{12} - 1\). The periodic factor is \(1.00666\ldots^{12} = 1.0829995068\) to ten decimal places, so \(EAR_{12}(0.08) = 0.0829995068\). To four decimal places this is 0.0830, or 8.30% a year.

M2 · A continuous factor over three years

One thousand CU is invested at a continuous nominal rate of 6% for three years. Compute the value at the end of the term, in CU to two decimal places.

Solution

The factor is \(e^{rt}\) with \(r = 0.06\) and \(t = 3\), so the exponent is \(0.18\). Then \(e^{0.18} = 1.1972173631\). The value is \(1000 \times 1.1972173631 = 1197.2173631\) CU, which is 1197.22 CU to two decimal places, or 119,722 cents to the nearest cent.

M3 · The monthly rate that matches a continuous 5%

Find the nominal annual rate \(r_{12}\), compounded monthly with \(1 + r_{12}/12 > 0\), whose one-year factor equals the one-year continuous factor at \(r = 0.05\). Give it to six decimal places.

Solution

The condition is \((1 + r_{12}/12)^{12} = e^{0.05}\). Since \(1 + r_{12}/12 > 0\), \(1 + r_{12}/12\) is the positive twelfth root of \(e^{0.05}\), which is \(e^{0.05/12}\) (because \((e^{0.05/12})^{12} = e^{0.05}\) by A2). So \(r_{12} = 12\,(e^{0.05/12} - 1)\). Now \(e^{0.05/12} = 1.004175359291\) to twelve decimal places, so \(r_{12} = 12 \times 0.004175359291 = 0.050104311492\), which is 0.0501043115 to ten decimal places, and 0.050104 to six. Check: \((1 + 0.0501043115/12)^{12} = 1.0512710964\) to ten places, which equals \(e^{0.05}\).

Proof

P1 · Proposition 2.1 at the first step

Show directly that \(F_{2}(r) - F_{1}(r) = r^{2}/4\), and state what this gives for \(r > 0\).

Solution

Here \(F_{1}(r) = 1 + r\) and \(F_{2}(r) = (1 + r/2)^{2} = 1 + r + r^{2}/4\). Subtracting, \(F_{2}(r) - F_{1}(r) = r^{2}/4\). For \(r > 0\) this is strictly positive, so \(F_{2}(r) > F_{1}(r)\). This is the first step of Proposition 2.1, and the proof there covers every later step.

P2 · The factor is increasing in the rate

For a fixed whole number \(m \ge 1\), prove that \(F_{m}(r) = (1 + r/m)^{m}\) is strictly increasing in \(r\) over \(r \ge 0\).

Solution

Let \(0 \le r_{1} < r_{2}\). Then \(0 < 1 + r_{1}/m < 1 + r_{2}/m\), because \(m \ge 1\) makes \(r/m\) strictly increasing in \(r\). A positive base raised to the fixed positive whole power \(m\) is strictly increasing in the base, so \((1 + r_{1}/m)^{m} < (1 + r_{2}/m)^{m}\). Hence \(F_{m}(r_{1}) < F_{m}(r_{2})\).

P3 · The value of one CU at time zero

Let \(C\) be a cash amount compounded continuously at rate \(r\) for \(t\) years, so that it grows to \(C e^{rt}\). Show that Proposition 3.1 gives the same growth when the period is split at any \(s\) with \(0 \le s \le t\).

Solution

Split \(t = s + (t - s)\) with \(0 \le s \le t\), so \(t - s \ge 0\). Compounding for \(s\) years gives \(Ce^{rs}\). Compounding that amount for \(t - s\) more years gives \(Ce^{rs}e^{r(t-s)}\). By the sum law of L03, \(e^{rs}e^{r(t-s)} = e^{r(s + t - s)} = e^{rt}\). So the two-step growth is \(Ce^{rt}\), which is the one-step growth.

Empirical

E1 · The gap between monthly and daily effective rates

Read Part A of Lab 1. What is the difference between the effective annual rate for 365 periods and for 12 periods, in decimal and in percentage points?

Solution

Part A prints 0.0512674965 for 365 periods and 0.0511618979 for 12 periods. The difference is \(0.0512674965 - 0.0511618979 = 0.0001055986\). In percentage points this is \(0.01055986\), or 0.0106 percentage points to four places. Proposition 2.1 predicts the sign: the 365-period rate is the larger.

E2 · How close the factor gets at a large m

Read Part B of Lab 1 at \(m = 1{,}000{,}000\). To how many decimal places do the factor and \(e^{0.05}\) agree?

Solution

Part B prints the factor 1.0512710951 and \(e^{0.05}\) as 1.0512710964. The first eight decimal places of both are 05127109, and they differ at the ninth decimal place, where the factor has 5 and \(e^{0.05}\) has 6. So the factor and \(e^{0.05}\) agree to eight decimal places. The printed error, 0.0000000013, lies between \(10^{-9}\) and \(10^{-8}\), which is consistent with agreement to eight decimal places and not nine. The page does not claim that this agreement holds for every \(m\).

E3 · Cent gaps across the four conventions

Using Part C of Lab 1, find the cent gap from monthly to daily compounding and from daily to continuous compounding, and say which step is larger.

Solution

Part C prints 1,647,009 cents for monthly and 1,648,665 for daily. The gap is \(1648665 - 1647009 = 1656\) cents. Part C prints 1,648,721 for continuous, so the gap from daily to continuous is \(1648721 - 1648665 = 56\) cents. The step from monthly to daily, 1,656 cents, is larger than the step from daily to continuous, 56 cents. On the printed whole-cent values the ratio is \(1656/56 = 29.\overline{571428}\), or 29.57 to two places. From unrounded values the two gaps are 1,655.32 cents and 56.46 cents, a ratio of 29.32 to two places.

Research

R1 · Where the positivity of r enters Proposition 2.1

Identify the two places where the proof of Proposition 2.1 uses \(r > 0\), and say what fails when \(r < 0\).

Solution

The first use is in the term-by-term comparison. Each comparison of a term with \(k \le m\) multiplies the product of factors \(\prod_{j}(1 - j/m)\), which increases in \(m\), by \(r^{k}/k!\). Multiplying an increasing quantity by a nonnegative number keeps the direction, so the comparison needs \(r^{k} \ge 0\). The second use is the extra term for \(k = m + 1\), \(\frac{r^{m+1}}{(m+1)!}\prod_{j=0}^{m}(1 - j/(m+1))\). Its product is strictly positive, so its sign is the sign of \(r^{m+1}\), and the strict increase needs \(r^{m+1} > 0\). For \(r < 0\) the odd-power terms are negative, and a larger product makes them more negative. The term-by-term comparison then has no fixed direction, and the proof gives no conclusion about how \(F_{m}(r)\) changes with \(m\).

R2 · The continuous effective rate bounds every finite one

For \(r > 0\), show that \(EAR_{m}(r) < e^{r} - 1\) for every whole number \(m \ge 1\), and state the consequence for a lender who compares offers.

Solution

Proposition 2.2 (under Assumption E) gives \(F_{m}(r) < e^{r}\). Subtracting 1 from both sides, \(EAR_{m}(r) = F_{m}(r) - 1 < e^{r} - 1\). So no finite compounding frequency reaches the continuous effective rate at the same nominal rate. An offer compounded continuously at a nominal rate therefore pays a strictly higher one-year growth than any offer at that nominal rate compounded a finite number of times.

R3 · The nominal rate for m periods that matches a continuous rate

For \(r > 0\) and a whole number \(m \ge 1\), let \(r_{m}\) be a nominal rate compounded \(m\) times a year, with \(1 + r_{m}/m > 0\), whose one-year factor equals \(e^{r}\). Show that \(r_{m} = m(e^{r/m} - 1)\) and that \(r_{m} > r\).

Solution

The condition is \((1 + r_{m}/m)^{m} = e^{r}\). The hypothesis \(1 + r_{m}/m > 0\) selects the positive \(m\)-th root of \(e^{r}\). That root is \(e^{r/m}\), because \((e^{r/m})^{m} = e^{r}\) by A2 and \(e^{r/m} > 0\) by A1. A positive number has at most one positive \(m\)-th root, because \(x \mapsto x^{m}\) is strictly increasing on the positive reals (if \(0 < a < b\) then \(a^{m} < b^{m}\), as in the proof of Proposition 2.2), so the positive root is \(e^{r/m}\) and no other. So \(1 + r_{m}/m = e^{r/m}\) and \(r_{m} = m(e^{r/m} - 1)\). The hypothesis is needed: for even \(m\), \(1 + r_{m}/m = -e^{r/m}\) also satisfies the condition. For the inequality, Assumption E gives \(e^{x} - 1 > x\) for \(x > 0\). With \(x = r/m > 0\), \(e^{r/m} - 1 > r/m\). Multiplying by \(m > 0\) gives \(r_{m} > r\). For \(r = 0.05\) and \(m = 12\) this gives \(r_{12} = 0.0501043115\), which is the value in M3.

  3. One thousand CU is invested at a continuous rate of 6% for three years. What is its value in CU, to two decimal places?

    \(1000\,e^{0.18} = 1197.2173631\) CU, which is 1197.22 CU (Problem M2).

  4. For a fixed rate \(r > 0\), how does \(F_{m}(r) = (1 + r/m)^{m}\) change as the compounding frequency \(m\) grows?

    Options

    Each period credits less, but there are more periods, and the extra term in the binomial expansion is strictly positive (Proposition 2.1).

  5. Which bound holds for every whole number \(m \ge 1\) when \(r > 0\)?

    Options

    Proposition 2.2. Assumption E, \(1 + x < e^{x}\) for \(x > 0\), raised to the \(m\)-th power, gives the strict bound.

  6. One million cents compounded continuously at 5% for ten years is worth how many whole cents?

    \(1{,}000{,}000\,e^{0.5} = 1{,}648{,}721.27\), which is 1,648,721 cents (Example 3.2 and Part C of Lab 1).

  7. Which identity makes the continuous factor consistent across two periods, as Proposition 3.1 states?

    Options

    The sum law of L03 gives \(e^{a}e^{b} = e^{a+b}\) with \(a = rs\) and \(b = rt\).

  8. What is the effective annual rate of continuous compounding at \(r = 0.05\), to ten decimal places?

    \(e^{0.05} - 1 = 0.0512710964\) (Definition 1.2 and Part B of Lab 1).

  9. In Part C of Lab 1, which step adds more cents to 1,000,000 cents over ten years?

    Options

    Part C prints monthly minus annual as 18114 and continuous minus daily as 56 (Problem E3 gives the monthly-to-daily gap of 1,656 cents from the printed whole-cent values).
