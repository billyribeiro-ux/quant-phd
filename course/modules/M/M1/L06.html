Geometric Series and Proof by Induction

  • Uses: proof by contradiction
  • Introduces: proof by induction, geometric series
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled

  • Dlore

The sums in Lessons 7 and 8 add up payments that are multiplied by a fixed factor at each step. Such a sum has a closed form, but a closed form is only as good as its proof for every number of terms. Checking the first few cases does not establish it. This lesson proves the induction principle from the well-ordering principle, then proves the geometric-sum formula by induction. Lessons 7 and 8 reduce their formulas to this result.

1Proof by induction

Statements in this lesson are indexed by integers. Throughout, \(n_0\) is a fixed integer and \(n\) ranges over integers with \(n \ge n_0\). We take the well-ordering principle as the defining property of the natural numbers: every nonempty set of integers that are at least \(n_0\) has a least element. No proof of it is given.

Definition 1.1 (proof by induction)

Let \(P(n)\) be a statement for each integer \(n \ge n_0\). A proof by induction of \(P(n)\) for all \(n \ge n_0\) has two parts. The base case proves \(P(n_0)\). The inductive step proves that for every integer \(k \ge n_0\), the implication \(P(k) \Rightarrow P(k+1)\) holds. The hypothesis \(P(k)\) used inside the step is the inductive hypothesis.

Theorem 1.2 (the induction principle)

Let \(P(n)\) be a statement for each integer \(n \ge n_0\). If \(P(n_0)\) holds and \(P(k) \Rightarrow P(k+1)\) holds for every integer \(k \ge n_0\), then \(P(n)\) holds for every integer \(n \ge n_0\).

Proof

Let \(F = \{n \ge n_0 : P(n) \text{ is false}\}\). Suppose, for contradiction, that \(F\) is nonempty. By the well-ordering principle, \(F\) has a least element \(m\). The base case gives \(P(n_0)\), so \(m \ne n_0\), and therefore \(m - 1 \ge n_0\). Because \(m - 1 < m\) and \(m\) is the least element of \(F\), the integer \(m - 1\) is not in \(F\): \(P(m-1)\) holds. The inductive step with \(k = m - 1\) then gives \(P(m)\), so \(m\) is not in \(F\). This contradicts the choice of \(m\). Hence \(F\) is empty.

Example 1.3 (the sum of the first n integers)

Let \(P(n)\) be \(\sum_{j=1}^{n} j = n(n+1)/2\), for \(n \ge 1\).

Base case. For \(n = 1\), the left side is \(1\) and the right side is \(1 \cdot 2/2 = 1\).

Inductive step. Let \(k \ge 1\) and assume \(\sum_{j=1}^{k} j = k(k+1)/2\). Then \[ \sum_{j=1}^{k+1} j = \sum_{j=1}^{k} j + (k+1) = \frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1) = \frac{k(k+1) + 2(k+1)}{2} = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}. \] This is \(P(k+1)\). By Theorem 1.2, \(P(n)\) holds for all \(n \ge 1\).

Remark 1.4 (the two parts are both required)

The inductive step alone proves nothing. Take \(P(n)\) to be the statement \(n = n + 1\). For each \(k\), the statement \(P(k)\) is false, so \(P(k) \Rightarrow P(k+1)\) is true, yet no integer satisfies \(P(n)\). The base case is what starts the chain. Checking the base case and the step is a complete proof, while checking many instances without a step is not.

2The geometric series

Definition 2.1 (geometric series, finite)

For a real number \(q\) and an integer \(n \ge 1\), the geometric series of length \(n\) with ratio \(q\) is \[ S_n(q) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} q^k = 1 + q + q^2 + \cdots + q^{n-1}, \] where \(q^0 = 1\) for every \(q\), including \(q = 0\).

Theorem 2.2 (closed form of the finite geometric series)

Let \(q \ne 1\) be real. Then for every integer \(n \ge 1\), \[ S_n(q) = \frac{1 - q^{n}}{1 - q}. \]

Proof

Fix \(q \ne 1\), so that \(1 - q \ne 0\) and the right side is defined. Let \(P(n)\) be the statement \(S_n(q) = (1 - q^n)/(1 - q)\), for \(n \ge 1\).

Base case. \(S_1(q) = q^0 = 1\). The right side for \(n = 1\) is \((1 - q)/(1 - q) = 1\). So \(P(1)\) holds.

Inductive step. Let \(k \ge 1\) and assume \(S_k(q) = (1 - q^k)/(1 - q)\). By the definition of \(S_{k+1}\), which adds the term \(q^k\) to \(S_k\), \[ S_{k+1}(q) = S_k(q) + q^k = \frac{1 - q^k}{1 - q} + q^k = \frac{1 - q^k + q^k(1 - q)}{1 - q} = \frac{1 - q^{k+1}}{1 - q}. \] The last step expands \(q^k(1 - q) = q^k - q^{k+1}\), so the numerator becomes \(1 - q^{k+1}\). This is \(P(k+1)\).

By Theorem 1.2, \(P(n)\) holds for every \(n \ge 1\).

Corollary 2.3 (the case q = 1)

For \(q = 1\), \(S_n(1) = n\) for every integer \(n \ge 1\). The closed form of Theorem 2.2 is not defined at \(q = 1\).

Proof

Each of the \(n\) terms \(1^k\) equals \(1\), so \(S_n(1) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} 1 = n\). The denominator \(1 - q\) of Theorem 2.2 is zero when \(q = 1\), so that formula does not apply.

Example 2.4 (a worked sum with ratio one half)

Take \(q = 1/2\) and \(n = 4\). Directly, \(S_4(1/2) = 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 = 15/8\). The closed form gives \((1 - 1/16)/(1/2) = (15/16) \cdot 2 = 15/8\). The two agree, as Theorem 2.2 requires.

Theorem 2.2 is an identity between exact numbers. The next lab checks it for seven ratios and every length from 1 to 60, with exact fractions, so that no approximation can enter the comparison, and then runs the closed form in floating point to see how much accuracy it loses.

Lab 1 · The geometric-sum identity in exact arithmetic

Stated before running: for seven rational ratios \(q \ne 1\), including negative and fractional ones, and every length \(n\) from 1 to 60, the term-by-term sum and the closed form of Theorem 2.2 are equal as exact fractions. The lab also checks that the sum equals \(n\) when \(q = 1\). In floating point, the closed form should lose accuracy as \(q\) approaches 1, because the numerator \(1 - q^n\) then becomes a small difference of two nearly equal numbers.

"""M1 L06 lab 1: the geometric-sum identity, checked in exact arithmetic for n = 1 to 60.

Claim checked: for every rational q with q != 1 and every integer n >= 1,
    sum_{k=0}^{n-1} q**k  ==  (1 - q**n) / (1 - q).
Both sides are Fraction values, so the comparison is exact and no approximation enters.
A second part shows that the same closed form computed in floating point loses digits when q is
close to 1. Standard library only. No randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

QS: tuple[Fraction, ...] = (
    Fraction(1, 2),
    Fraction(2, 3),
    Fraction(-3, 2),
    Fraction(7, 5),
    Fraction(-1, 3),
    Fraction(0),
    Fraction(1, 1000),
)
N_MAX = 60


def direct_sum(q: Fraction, n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Return sum_{k=0}^{n-1} q**k, adding the terms one at a time."""
    total = Fraction(0)
    for k in range(n):
        total += q**k
    return total


def closed_form(q: Fraction, n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Return (1 - q**n) / (1 - q). Requires q != 1."""
    if q == 1:
        raise ValueError("the closed form needs q != 1")
    return (1 - q**n) / (1 - q)


def float_relative_error(q_float: float, n: int) -> float:
    """Relative error of the floating-point closed form, against the exact value at the same float q."""
    exact = closed_form(Fraction(q_float), n)  # Fraction(q_float) is the float's exact value
    approx = (1.0 - q_float**n) / (1.0 - q_float)
    return abs(approx - float(exact)) / float(exact)


def main() -> None:
    checks = 0
    for q in QS:
        for n in range(1, N_MAX + 1):
            if direct_sum(q, n) != closed_form(q, n):
                raise AssertionError(f"identity fails at q = {q}, n = {n}")
            checks += 1
    print(f"exact checks passed: {checks} (q takes {len(QS)} values, n = 1 to {N_MAX})")

    for n in range(1, N_MAX + 1):
        if direct_sum(Fraction(1), n) != n:
            raise AssertionError(f"the q = 1 sum should equal n, failed at n = {n}")
    print(f"q = 1: the sum equals n for n = 1 to {N_MAX} (checked exactly)")

    q, n = Fraction(1, 2), 3
    print(f"q = 1/2, n = 3: direct sum = {direct_sum(q, n)}, closed form = {closed_form(q, n)}")
    q, n = Fraction(-1), 10
    print(f"q = -1, n = 10: direct sum = {direct_sum(q, n)}, closed form = {closed_form(q, n)}")

    for q_float in (1.001, 1.0 + 1e-9):
        error = float_relative_error(q_float, 60)
        print(f"floating-point closed form at q = {q_float!r}, n = 60: relative error {error:.3e}")


main()
exact checks passed: 420 (q takes 7 values, n = 1 to 60)
q = 1: the sum equals n for n = 1 to 60 (checked exactly)
q = 1/2, n = 3: direct sum = 7/4, closed form = 7/4
q = -1, n = 10: direct sum = 0, closed form = 0
floating-point closed form at q = 1.001, n = 60: relative error 8.048e-16
floating-point closed form at q = 1.000000001, n = 60: relative error 1.059e-10

The exact checks passed for every ratio and length, so Theorem 2.2 holds for these 420 pairs; the lab checks the theorem and does not prove it, and the proof is in Section 2. The float lines are the one place in the lab where approximation enters: the closed form in floating point is accurate to about \(8 \times 10^{-16}\) at \(q = 1.001\) and to about \(1.1 \times 10^{-10}\) at \(q = 1 + 10^{-9}\), both at \(n = 60\). The second value is larger, as the cancellation explanation in Section 3 predicts. The lab does not show that floating-point error is small for every \(q\), and it does not show how the error depends on \(n\).

3Where the canon breaks

  • The closed form is undefined at ratio one. At \(q = 1\), Theorem 2.2 has a zero denominator. The sum is \(n\) (Corollary 2.3), and any code that uses \((1 - q^n)/(1 - q)\) must branch on \(q = 1\) before dividing.
  • Induction proves statements about integers only. The step \(P(k) \Rightarrow P(k+1)\) connects each integer to the next. It reaches \(n_0, n_0 + 1, n_0 + 2, \ldots\) and no other numbers. A statement about \(n = 1/2\) is outside the reach of Theorem 1.2.
  • Cancellation in floating point. When \(q\) is close to 1, \(1 - q^n\) is a small difference of two nearly equal numbers, and the floating-point error in \(q^n\) is then a larger share of the numerator, so the closed form loses relative accuracy. Lab 1 shows this at \(q = 1 + 10^{-9}\). The exact closed form is unaffected, so a financial engine that uses the formula with floats needs to know about this.
  • Theorem 2.2 is about finite sums. It says nothing about the sum over all \(n\). Lesson 7 handles infinite payment streams through the finite formula and an explicit remainder, and it does not use an infinite geometric sum as a theorem.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Checking the statement for the first few integers proves it for all of them.”

    A finite list of cases does not cover the infinitely many integers, and Theorem 1.2 needs the inductive step. The polynomial \(n^2 + n + 41\) gives a prime for each integer \(n\) from 0 to 39 (each value can be tested by trial division), and gives \(40^2 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41^2\) at \(n = 40\), which is not prime.

  2. “A proof of \(P(k) \Rightarrow P(k+1)\) is enough, because the base case is obvious.”

    Remark 1.4 gives a statement for which every step holds and no instance is true. The base case is a required part of the proof, not a formality.

  3. “The closed form \((1 - q^n)/(1 - q)\) also gives the right answer at \(q = 1\), where it becomes \(0/0\) and so has some value.”

    \(0/0\) has no value, and the sum at \(q = 1\) is \(n\) (Corollary 2.3). A formula that is undefined at a point cannot be used there without a separate case.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · A sum by the closed form

Evaluate \(\sum_{k=0}^{4} 3^k\) using Theorem 2.2, and check it by adding the five terms.

Solution

Here \(q = 3\) and \(n = 5\), so the closed form gives \((1 - 3^5)/(1 - 3) = (1 - 243)/(-2) = (-242)/(-2) = 121\). Directly, \(1 + 3 + 9 + 27 + 81 = 121\). The two agree.

M2 · A sum that starts at k = 2

Evaluate \(\sum_{k=2}^{6} 2^k\). Use the closed form for \(\sum_{k=0}^{6} 2^k\) and subtract the terms that are not wanted.

Solution

The sum from \(k = 0\) to \(6\) has \(n = 7\) terms, so it equals \((1 - 2^7)/(1 - 2) = (1 - 128)/(-1) = 127\). The wanted sum omits \(k = 0\) and \(k = 1\), whose terms are \(1\) and \(2\). So \(\sum_{k=2}^{6} 2^k = 127 - 3 = 124\). Directly, \(4 + 8 + 16 + 32 + 64 = 124\).

M3 · How many terms reach a target

For \(q = 1/2\), find the smallest \(n\) for which \(S_n(1/2) \ge 1.9\).

Solution

By Theorem 2.2, \(S_n(1/2) = (1 - 2^{-n})/(1/2) = 2(1 - 2^{-n})\). The condition \(2(1 - 2^{-n}) \ge 1.9\) is \(2^{-n} \le 0.05\). For \(n = 4\), \(S_4 = 2 \cdot 15/16 = 1.875 < 1.9\). For \(n = 5\), \(S_5 = 2 \cdot 31/32 = 1.9375 \ge 1.9\). The answer is \(n = 5\).

Proof

P1 · Sum of odd integers

Prove by induction that \(\sum_{j=1}^{n} (2j - 1) = n^2\) for every integer \(n \ge 1\).

Solution

Base case. For \(n = 1\), the left side is \(2 \cdot 1 - 1 = 1 = 1^2\). Inductive step. Assume \(\sum_{j=1}^{k} (2j - 1) = k^2\) for some \(k \ge 1\). Then \(\sum_{j=1}^{k+1} (2j - 1) = k^2 + (2(k+1) - 1) = k^2 + 2k + 1 = (k+1)^2\). By Theorem 1.2 the identity holds for all \(n \ge 1\).

P2 · An inequality by induction

Prove that \(2^n \ge n + 1\) for every integer \(n \ge 0\).

Solution

Base case. For \(n = 0\), \(2^0 = 1 \ge 0 + 1 = 1\). Inductive step. Assume \(2^k \ge k + 1\) for some \(k \ge 0\). Then \(2^{k+1} = 2 \cdot 2^k \ge 2(k+1) = 2k + 2\). Since \(k \ge 0\), \(2k + 2 \ge k + 2 = (k+1) + 1\). So \(2^{k+1} \ge (k+1) + 1\), which is the statement for \(k + 1\). By Theorem 1.2 the inequality holds for all \(n \ge 0\).

P3 · A sum that starts at one

For \(q \ne 1\) and \(n \ge 1\), show that \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} q^k = q(1 - q^n)/(1 - q)\), using Theorem 2.2 and no new induction.

Solution

Factor \(q\) out of each term: \(\sum_{k=1}^{n} q^k = q \sum_{k=1}^{n} q^{k-1} = q \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} q^j\), by the substitution \(j = k - 1\). Theorem 2.2 gives \(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} q^j = (1 - q^n)/(1 - q)\), so the sum equals \(q(1 - q^n)/(1 - q)\).

Empirical

E1 · The ratio one case in code

Lab 1 excludes \(q = 1\) from the closed form. Predict what a program that evaluates \((1 - q^n)/(1 - q)\) with \(q = 1.0\) in floating point does, and what it should return instead.

Solution

The denominator is \(1.0 - 1.0 = 0.0\). Python raises ZeroDivisionError for float division by zero, so the program stops rather than returning a wrong number. The correct value is \(n\), by Corollary 2.3, so the code should branch on \(q = 1\) and return \(n\) in that case.

E2 · Why the float error grows near one

In Lab 1 the float closed form at \(n = 60\) has relative error \(8 \times 10^{-16}\) at \(q = 1.001\) and \(1.1 \times 10^{-10}\) at \(q = 1 + 10^{-9}\). Explain why, using the size of the numerator \(1 - q^n\).

Solution

At \(q = 1 + 10^{-9}\) and \(n = 60\), \(q^{60} \approx 1 + 6 \times 10^{-8}\), so the numerator \(1 - q^{60}\) is about \(-6 \times 10^{-8}\). The computed \(q^{60}\) carries an absolute error of order \(10^{-16}\), because it is a number near 1 stored in double precision. That absolute error is a relative error of about \(10^{-16} / (6 \times 10^{-8}) \approx 1.7 \times 10^{-9}\) in the numerator. This is an estimate of the size of the effect, not the exact error: the observed \(1.1 \times 10^{-10}\) is smaller than this estimate. At \(q = 1.001\) the numerator is about \(-0.062\), so the same absolute error is a much smaller relative error, which matches the observed \(8 \times 10^{-16}\). The cause is the subtraction \(1 - q^n\), in which the leading digits cancel.

E3 · Check the closed form at ratio two

Compute \(\sum_{k=0}^{9} 2^k\) by the closed form, and check it by direct addition of the powers of two.

Solution

With \(q = 2\) and \(n = 10\), the closed form gives \((1 - 2^{10})/(1 - 2) = (1 - 1024)/(-1) = 1023\). Directly, the terms are \(1, 2, 4, \ldots, 512\), and their sum is \(2^{10} - 1 = 1023\). The two agree.

Research

R1 · A sum of squares

Prove by induction that \(\sum_{j=1}^{n} j^2 = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6\) for every integer \(n \ge 1\).

Solution

Base case. For \(n = 1\), the left side is \(1\) and the right side is \(1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3/6 = 1\). Inductive step. Assume the formula for \(k\). Then \(\sum_{j=1}^{k+1} j^2 = k(k+1)(2k+1)/6 + (k+1)^2 = (k+1)[k(2k+1) + 6(k+1)]/6\). The bracket is \(2k^2 + 7k + 6 = (k+2)(2k+3)\), so the sum is \((k+1)(k+2)(2(k+1)+1)/6\), which is the formula for \(k+1\). By Theorem 1.2 the formula holds for all \(n \ge 1\).

R2 · Why induction stops at the integers

Explain why Theorem 1.2 cannot prove a statement \(P(x)\) for every real number \(x \ge 0\), even when the implication \(P(x) \Rightarrow P(x + 1)\) holds for every real \(x \ge 0\).

Solution

Starting from a base point \(x_0\), the implication only produces \(x_0 + 1, x_0 + 2, \ldots\). Those points are spaced one apart, so for \(x_0 = 0\) the reachable set is the nonnegative integers and no other real. The number \(1/2\) is not reached from \(0\) by steps of \(1\). Covering the reals needs a different argument, such as a statement about intervals, and the well-ordering principle that Theorem 1.2 uses is about integers.

R3 · The ratio minus one

Let \(q = -1\). Use Theorem 2.2 to find \(S_n(-1)\) for every \(n \ge 1\), and state the answer in terms of the parity of \(n\).

Solution

Since \(q = -1 \ne 1\), Theorem 2.2 gives \(S_n(-1) = (1 - (-1)^n)/2\). If \(n\) is odd, \((-1)^n = -1\) and the sum is \(1\). If \(n\) is even, \((-1)^n = 1\) and the sum is \(0\). Directly, \(1 - 1 + 1 - \cdots\) with an odd number of terms ends on a \(+1\), and with an even number it cancels in pairs. The two methods agree.

  2. Compute \(\sum_{k=0}^{5} 2^k\).

    Theorem 2.2 with \(q = 2\) and \(n = 6\): \((1 - 64)/(1 - 2) = 63\).

  3. For which ratio \(q\) is the closed form \((1 - q^n)/(1 - q)\) not defined?

    Options

    The denominator is \(1 - q\), which is zero only at \(q = 1\) (Corollary 2.3).

  4. Compute \(S_3(1/2) = 1 + 1/2 + 1/4\) (two decimals).

    Directly, \(1 + 0.5 + 0.25 = 1.75\). The closed form gives \((1 - 1/8)/(1/2) = 1.75\).

  5. Compute \(\sum_{k=2}^{4} 3^k\).

    \(9 + 27 + 81 = 117\). The terms start at \(k = 2\), so \(3^1\) is not included.

    Options

    Theorem 1.2 needs both parts. The first option is a finite check and says nothing about \(n > 1000\).

  7. Compute \(\sum_{k=0}^{9} (-1)^k\), using Theorem 2.2 with \(q = -1\) and \(n = 10\).

    \((1 - (-1)^{10})/2 = (1 - 1)/2 = 0\). Ten alternating terms cancel in pairs.

  8. For \(q = 1\) and any \(n \ge 1\), \(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} q^k\) equals…

    Options

    Corollary 2.3. Each term is \(1^k = 1\).

  9. In the inductive step of Example 1.3, the identity \(\frac{k(k+1)}{2} + (k+1) = \frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{2}\) is used because…
