Some claims in this program have the form "every bond pays a coupon" or "some rate makes two schedules equal". Whether such a claim is true, and what would count against it, depends on which words such as "every" and "some" make the claim, and over which domain they range. This lesson fixes the meaning of those two words in logic, proves the rules for negating a quantified statement, shows that the order in which "every" and "some" appear matters, and separates what a finite check can refute from what it can establish.

1Sets, statements and predicates

A set is a collection of objects. We write \(x \in S\) to say that \(x\) is a member of \(S\). This lesson uses sets through membership and through the finite lists that Lab 1 enumerates. Sets of numbers, functions and sequences are used as ordinary mathematics, in prose, with no formal definition of their own.

Definition 1.1 (statement, predicate, domain)

A statement is a sentence that is either true or false. A domain \(D\) is a set of objects that a variable may take values in. A predicate \(P\) on \(D\) is a sentence \(P(x)\), with \(x\) a variable in \(D\), that becomes a statement once a value of \(x\) is given. A claim that uses a variable has no truth value until its domain is stated.

The domain matters. The claim "every \(x\) satisfies \(x^2 \ge x\)" is false when \(x\) ranges over the real numbers, since \(x = \tfrac12\) gives \(\tfrac14 < \tfrac12\). That single value refutes the claim on the reals. The same example says nothing about the integers, because \(\tfrac12\) is not an integer and is outside that domain. In Sections 3 to 5 and in Lab 1, each example, counterexample and check names its domain.

2The two quantifiers

Definition 2.1 (the quantifiers)

Let \(P\) be a predicate on a domain \(D\). The statement \(\forall x \in D\; P(x)\), read "for every \(x\) in \(D\), \(P(x)\)", is true exactly when \(P(x)\) is true for every \(x\) in \(D\). The statement \(\exists x \in D\; P(x)\), read "there exists \(x\) in \(D\) with \(P(x)\)", is true exactly when \(P(x)\) is true for at least one \(x\) in \(D\). The symbols \(\forall\) and \(\exists\) are the quantifiers.

Remark 2.2 (the empty domain)

If \(D\) has no members, then \(\forall x \in D\; P(x)\) is true, because no member fails \(P\), and \(\exists x \in D\; P(x)\) is false, because no member satisfies \(P\). This follows from Definition 2.1 and is the convention this unit uses. Readers who expect "every" to imply that something exists should note that the unit does not assume it.

3Negating a quantified statement

Theorem 3.1 (negating one quantifier)

Let \(P\) be a predicate on a domain \(D\). Then

\[\neg\bigl(\forall x \in D\; P(x)\bigr) \iff \exists x \in D\; \neg P(x), \qquad \neg\bigl(\exists x \in D\; P(x)\bigr) \iff \forall x \in D\; \neg P(x).\]

Proof

By Definition 2.1, \(\forall x \in D\; P(x)\) is false exactly when it is not the case that \(P(x)\) holds for every \(x\) in \(D\). That is the case exactly when there is some \(x\) in \(D\) for which \(P(x)\) fails, that is, for which \(\neg P(x)\) holds. So \(\neg(\forall x \in D\; P(x))\) holds exactly when \(\exists x \in D\; \neg P(x)\) holds. For the second identity, \(\exists x \in D\; P(x)\) is false exactly when no \(x\) in \(D\) satisfies \(P\). That is the case exactly when every \(x\) in \(D\) fails \(P\), which is \(\forall x \in D\; \neg P(x)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Applied to a claim such as "every bond in a stated portfolio pays a coupon", Theorem 3.1 gives the negation "some bond pays no coupon". The negation is not "every bond pays no coupon", which is a different and much stronger claim. Misconception 1 below shows the difference on a predicate that is true for some members of the domain and false for others.

Theorem 3.2 (negating a nested statement)

Let \(R(x, y)\) be a predicate of two variables \(x, y \in D\), that is, a binary relation on \(D\). Then

(a) \(\neg\bigl(\forall x \in D\; \exists y \in D\; R(x, y)\bigr) \iff \exists x \in D\; \forall y \in D\; \neg R(x, y)\), and

(b) \(\neg\bigl(\exists x \in D\; \forall y \in D\; R(x, y)\bigr) \iff \forall x \in D\; \exists y \in D\; \neg R(x, y)\).

Proof

(a) Put \(P(x) = \exists y \in D\; R(x, y)\). By Theorem 3.1, \(\neg(\forall x \in D\; P(x))\) holds exactly when \(\exists x \in D\; \neg P(x)\) holds. Now \(\neg P(x)\) is \(\neg\bigl(\exists y \in D\; R(x, y)\bigr)\), and by the second identity of Theorem 3.1, applied to the predicate \(y \mapsto R(x, y)\) for each fixed \(x\), this is equivalent to \(\forall y \in D\; \neg R(x, y)\). Substituting this equivalent for each \(x\) gives \(\exists x \in D\; \forall y \in D\; \neg R(x, y)\).

(b) Put \(Q(x) = \forall y \in D\; R(x, y)\). By the second identity of Theorem 3.1, \(\neg(\exists x \in D\; Q(x))\) holds exactly when \(\forall x \in D\; \neg Q(x)\) holds. For each fixed \(x\), the first identity of Theorem 3.1, applied to the predicate \(y \mapsto R(x, y)\), gives \(\neg Q(x) = \neg\bigl(\forall y \in D\; R(x, y)\bigr) \iff \exists y \in D\; \neg R(x, y)\). Substituting this equivalent for each \(x\) gives \(\forall x \in D\; \exists y \in D\; \neg R(x, y)\). \(\blacksquare\)

4The order of quantifiers

Example 4.1 (swapping the quantifiers changes the claim)

Let \(D = \mathbb{Z}_5\), the integers taken modulo 5, and let \(R(x, y)\) say that \(y = x + 1 \pmod 5\). For \(\forall x\, \exists y\, R(x, y)\): given any \(x\), take \(y = x + 1 \pmod 5\), which is in \(D\). So the statement is true. For \(\exists y\, \forall x\, R(x, y)\): take any \(y\) in \(D\) and put \(x = y\). Then \(R(y, y)\) says \(y = y + 1 \pmod 5\), which is false. So no \(y\) works for every \(x\), and the statement is false.

Proposition 4.2 (one direction of the swap)

If \(\exists y \in D\; \forall x \in D\; P(x, y)\) holds, then \(\forall x \in D\; \exists y \in D\; P(x, y)\) holds. The converse fails, as Example 4.1 shows.

Proof

Let \(y_0\) be a member of \(D\) with \(P(x, y_0)\) true for every \(x\) in \(D\). Take any \(x\) in \(D\). Then \(P(x, y_0)\) holds, so \(\exists y \in D\; P(x, y)\) holds with the witness \(y = y_0\). Since \(x\) was arbitrary, \(\forall x \in D\; \exists y \in D\; P(x, y)\) holds. Example 4.1 has the second statement true and the first false, so the converse does not hold. \(\blacksquare\)

5Finite domains and what a check establishes

Remark 5.1 (on a finite domain a universal claim is a finite list of checks)

Let \(D = \{d_1, \dots, d_n\}\). Then \(\forall x \in D\; P(x)\) is true exactly when \(P(d_1), \dots, P(d_n)\) are all true, and \(\exists x \in D\; P(x)\) is true exactly when at least one of them is. This is Definition 2.1 with \(D\) listed. So a check of every member of a finite domain settles the claim on that domain. It says nothing about members outside the domain.

Remark 5.1 also gives the logic of refutation. A universal claim \(\forall x \in D\; P(x)\) is false exactly when one member fails. A single counterexample in the domain refutes it. Passing many checks does not prove it on a larger domain. Lab 1 applies both facts.

Lab 1 · Quantified statements on finite domains

Predicted from the theorems before running. On the domain \(\{0, 1, 2, 3\}\) a predicate is a choice of True or False for each of the 4 members, so there are \(2^4 = 16\) predicates, and Theorem 3.1 says that "not for every" agrees with "there exists not" on all of them. On \(\{0, 1, 2\}\) a binary relation is a choice for each of the \(3 \times 3 = 9\) pairs, so there are \(2^9 = 512\) relations, and Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 say both negation identities hold for all of them. The relations with "for every \(x\) there exists \(y\) with \(R(x, y)\)" are counted by rows: each row must be a non-empty subset of the 3 members, which gives \(2^3 - 1 = 7\) choices, and the 3 rows are chosen independently, so \(7^3 = 343\). Example 4.1 predicts True and then False on \(\mathbb{Z}_5\). Last, for \(n^2 + n + 41\) at \(n = 40\) the value is \(1600 + 40 + 41 = 1681 = 41 \times 41\), so the search should find \(n = 40\) as the first failure; the 39 values from \(n = 1\) to \(39\) are checked, not assumed.

"""M1 lab L01: quantified statements over finite domains, checked by exhaustive enumeration.

Standard library only. No randomness and no network: every case is enumerated, so the output is
the same on every run. A check over a finite domain tests that domain only. It is evidence about
the finite case and says nothing about an infinite domain, and the page reads the output that way.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections.abc import Callable, Iterable, Sequence
from itertools import product

Pred1 = Callable[[int], bool]
Pred2 = Callable[[int, int], bool]


def forall(domain: Iterable[int], pred: Pred1) -> bool:
    """True when pred(x) holds for every x in domain. An empty domain gives True."""
    return all(pred(x) for x in domain)


def exists(domain: Iterable[int], pred: Pred1) -> bool:
    """True when pred(x) holds for at least one x in domain. An empty domain gives False."""
    return any(pred(x) for x in domain)


def negated(pred: Pred1) -> Pred1:
    """The predicate x -> not pred(x)."""

    def result(x: int) -> bool:
        return not pred(x)

    return result


def table_predicate(table: dict[int, bool]) -> Pred1:
    """The predicate given by a table of truth values, one per member of its domain."""

    def result(x: int) -> bool:
        return table[x]

    return result


def smallest_factor(n: int) -> int:
    """The least divisor d >= 2 of n >= 2, found by trial division. Input outside n >= 2 raises ValueError."""
    if n < 2:
        raise ValueError(f"smallest_factor needs n >= 2, got {n}")
    d = 2
    while d * d <= n:
        if n % d == 0:
            return d
        d += 1
    return n


def is_prime(n: int) -> bool:
    """True when n is a prime number. Integers below 2 are not prime."""
    return n >= 2 and smallest_factor(n) == n


def negation_check(domain: Sequence[int]) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """Count the predicates on domain for which not-forall agrees with exists-not, and the predicates tested.

    A predicate on a finite domain is an assignment of True or False to each element, so there are
    2 to the power len(domain) of them. Each one is tested.
    """
    tested = 0
    agree = 0
    for values in product((False, True), repeat=len(domain)):
        pred = table_predicate(dict(zip(domain, values, strict=True)))
        tested += 1
        left = not forall(domain, pred)
        right = exists(domain, negated(pred))
        agree += left == right
    return agree, tested


def forall_exists(rel: dict[tuple[int, int], bool], domain: Sequence[int]) -> bool:
    """True when for every x in domain some y in domain has R(x, y), with R given as a table."""
    return all(any(rel[(x, y)] for y in domain) for x in domain)


def exists_forall(rel: dict[tuple[int, int], bool], domain: Sequence[int]) -> bool:
    """True when some x in domain has R(x, y) for every y in domain, with R given as a table."""
    return any(all(rel[(x, y)] for y in domain) for x in domain)


def relation_check(size: int) -> tuple[int, int, int, int]:
    """Over binary relations R on {0, ..., size-1}, test two quantifier identities and count one property.

    Returns (agree_1, agree_2, total, count_forall_exists), where:
      agree_1: cases where not(forall x exists y R(x,y)) equals (exists x forall y not R(x,y));
      agree_2: cases where not(exists x forall y R(x,y)) equals (forall x exists y not R(x,y));
      total: the number of relations, 2 to the power size*size;
      count_forall_exists: relations with forall x exists y R(x,y).
    """
    domain = range(size)
    cells = [(x, y) for x in domain for y in domain]
    agree_1 = agree_2 = count = total = 0
    for bits in product((False, True), repeat=len(cells)):
        rel = dict(zip(cells, bits, strict=True))
        total += 1
        fe = forall_exists(rel, domain)
        ef = exists_forall(rel, domain)
        count += fe
        agree_1 += (not fe) == any(all(not rel[(x, y)] for y in domain) for x in domain)
        agree_2 += (not ef) == all(any(not rel[(x, y)] for y in domain) for x in domain)
    return agree_1, agree_2, total, count


def order_matters() -> tuple[bool, bool]:
    """On Z_5 with R(x, y) meaning y = x + 1 (mod 5): forall x exists y R, and exists y forall x R."""
    domain = range(5)

    def r(x: int, y: int) -> bool:
        return y == (x + 1) % 5

    forall_exists = forall(domain, lambda x: exists(domain, lambda y: r(x, y)))
    exists_forall = exists(domain, lambda y: forall(domain, lambda x: r(x, y)))
    return forall_exists, exists_forall


def first_non_prime(limit: int) -> tuple[int, int, int]:
    """The least n in 1..limit with n*n + n + 41 not prime, with the value and its smallest factor.

    Returns (n, value, smallest factor), or (0, 0, 0) if no n in range fails.
    """
    for n in range(1, limit + 1):
        value = n * n + n + 41
        if not is_prime(value):
            return n, value, smallest_factor(value)
    return 0, 0, 0


def main() -> None:
    d4 = [0, 1, 2, 3]
    agree, tested = negation_check(d4)
    print(f"domain {{0, 1, 2, 3}}: predicates tested {tested}")
    print(f"not-forall equals exists-not on {agree} of {tested} predicates")

    a1, a2, total, count = relation_check(3)
    print("domain {0, 1, 2}: binary relations tested", total)
    print(f"not-forall-exists equals exists-forall-not on {a1} of {total} relations")
    print(f"not-exists-forall equals forall-exists-not on {a2} of {total} relations")
    print(f"relations with forall x exists y R(x, y): {count}")

    fe, ef = order_matters()
    print("domain Z_5, R(x, y) meaning y = x + 1 mod 5")
    print(f"forall x exists y R(x, y): {fe}")
    print(f"exists y forall x R(x, y): {ef}")

    primes = sum(is_prime(n * n + n + 41) for n in range(1, 40))
    print(f"n from 1 to 39 with n*n + n + 41 prime: {primes} of 39")
    n, value, factor = first_non_prime(50)
    print(f"least n in 1 to 50 with n*n + n + 41 not prime: {n}")
    print(f"value at that n: {value} = {factor} x {value // factor}")

    stmt = forall(range(1, 11), lambda x: x * x >= x)
    print(f"statement for every x in 1 to 10, x*x >= x: {stmt}")

Every printed agreement is a finite enumeration, so each count is exact for its domain. The 16 and 512 agreements illustrate Theorems 3.1 and 3.2 on those domains; they do not replace the proofs, which hold for every domain. The first-failure line refutes the universal claim "for every \(n \ge 1\), \(n^2 + n + 41\) is prime": one counterexample is enough, by Remark 5.1. 6Where the canon breaks

This lesson states the quantifiers and their negation, then names four points that a bare statement of the rules leaves out.

  • Natural-language scope is ambiguous. "Every fund in a stated sample beats its benchmark in some year" can mean that each fund has some good year, or that one year is good for all funds. The two readings have different quantifier orders, and Example 4.1 shows that they can differ. Formal notation removes the ambiguity by fixing the order.
  • A domain is a choice. Definition 1.1 requires a domain, and empirical claims in the program can use domains that are sets of observations, such as firms in a sample, not every firm that could exist. A statement about the sample and a statement about the population are different claims. Moving from one to the other is an inference, and logic does not license it.
  • The empty-domain convention is a choice. Remark 2.2 makes a universal claim over an empty domain true. Some readers find this strange, and it matters when a filter removes every observation, because the claim "every observation in the filter has property P" then holds vacuously. Checks should report the size of the domain alongside the result.
  • A finite check is a different kind of evidence from a proof. Remark 5.1 shows that a check settles a finite domain and refutes a universal claim by a counterexample. It does not establish an infinite universal claim. Lab 1 shows a claim that holds on 39 values and fails at the 40th.

7Misconceptions

  1. “The negation of “every \(x\) satisfies \(P(x)\)” is “every \(x\) fails \(P(x)\)”.”

    Theorem 3.1 gives "there exists \(x\) that fails \(P(x)\)". Take \(D = \{0, 1\}\) and \(P(x)\) true at \(x = 0\) and false at \(x = 1\). Then "every \(x\) satisfies \(P(x)\)" is false, so its negation is true. The wrong form, "every \(x\) fails \(P(x)\)", is false, because \(x = 0\) satisfies \(P\). Lab 1 counts 16 predicates on four members where the correct negation agrees with the enumeration.

  2. “The order of \(\forall\) and \(\exists\) does not change the claim.”

    Example 4.1 has the statement \(\forall x\, \exists y\, R(x, y)\) true and \(\exists y\, \forall x\, R(x, y)\) false on the same domain and relation. Proposition 4.2 proves one direction. Lab 1 prints True for the first order and False for the second on \(\mathbb{Z}_5\).

  3. “Many passed checks prove a universal claim.”

    Remark 5.1 says a finite check settles only the listed domain. The polynomial \(n^2 + n + 41\) gives a prime for each \(n\) from 1 to 39 that Lab 1 checks, and gives \(1681 = 41 \times 41\) at \(n = 40\). One counterexample refutes "for every \(n \ge 1\), the value is prime", however many earlier checks passed.

8Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · Negating a universal claim about students

Let \(D\) be the set of students in a class and \(P(s)\) the statement "student \(s\) has passed the exam". Write the negation of "every student has passed" in symbols and in words.

Solution

The claim is \(\forall s \in D\; P(s)\). By Theorem 3.1 its negation is \(\exists s \in D\; \neg P(s)\): some student has not passed. The statement "no student has passed", which is \(\forall s \in D\; \neg P(s)\), is a different claim, and it is the wrong negation in Misconception 1.

M2 · Negating a nested statement over the integers

Let \(D\) be the integers and \(R(x, y)\) the statement \(y > x\). Write the negation of \(\forall x\, \exists y\, R(x, y)\) and decide whether the original claim or its negation is true.

Solution

By Theorem 3.2 (a) the negation is \(\exists x\, \forall y\, \neg R(x, y)\), that is, \(\exists x\, \forall y\, (y \le x)\). For the original claim, given any integer \(x\), the integer \(y = x + 1\) satisfies \(y > x\), so the claim is true. Its negation is then false: for any integer \(x\), the integer \(y = x + 1\) violates \(y \le x\). The two statements are consistent, since exactly one of a statement and its negation is true.

M3 · Counting predicates on a five-member domain

How many predicates are there on a domain with five members, where a predicate is a choice of True or False for each member?

Solution

Each of the five members independently takes one of two truth values, so there are \(2^5 = 32\) predicates. The Lab 1 count of 16 for four members is \(2^4\) by the same argument, and the lab's enumeration uses that count.

Proof

P1 · The second identity of Theorem 3.1

Prove from Definition 2.1 that \(\neg(\exists x \in D\; P(x))\) holds exactly when \(\forall x \in D\; \neg P(x)\) holds.

Solution

By Definition 2.1, \(\exists x \in D\; P(x)\) holds when some \(x\) in \(D\) satisfies \(P\). Its negation holds exactly when no \(x\) in \(D\) satisfies \(P\). No \(x\) satisfies \(P\) exactly when each \(x\) in \(D\) fails \(P\), and that is \(\forall x \in D\; \neg P(x)\) by Definition 2.1 applied to \(\neg P\). The two conditions are equivalent, which is the claim.

P2 · A universal claim on a finite domain is a conjunction

Let \(D = \{d_1, \dots, d_n\}\) with \(n \ge 1\). Show that \(\forall x \in D\; P(x)\) is equivalent to \(P(d_1) \wedge \dots \wedge P(d_n)\), and say what the empty conjunction gives when \(n = 0\).

Solution

By Definition 2.1, \(\forall x \in D\; P(x)\) holds exactly when \(P(x)\) holds for each member of \(D\). For \(D\) listed as \(d_1, \dots, d_n\), that is exactly when \(P(d_1)\), \(\dots\), \(P(d_n)\) all hold, which is the conjunction. For \(n = 0\) the conjunction has no terms and is true by convention, which agrees with Remark 2.2: the universal claim over the empty domain is true.

P3 · The forall-exists statement does not imply the exists-forall statement

Let \(D = \{a, b\}\) and \(P(x, y)\) the statement \(x \ne y\). Show that \(\forall x\, \exists y\, P(x, y)\) is true and \(\exists y\, \forall x\, P(x, y)\) is false.

Solution

For \(x = a\) take \(y = b\), and for \(x = b\) take \(y = a\). Each witness gives \(x \ne y\), so the forall-exists statement holds. For the exists-forall statement, take any \(y\) in \(D\) and put \(x = y\). Then \(P(y, y)\) says \(y \ne y\), which is false, so no \(y\) satisfies \(P(x, y)\) for every \(x\). The statement is false, which shows that the forall-exists statement does not imply the other in general.

Empirical

E1 · Where 343 comes from (finite enumeration)

Lab 1 prints the number of binary relations on \(\{0, 1, 2\}\) with \(\forall x\, \exists y\, R(x, y)\). Derive that number by hand and check it against the output.

Solution

A relation on three members is a choice of True or False for each of the nine pairs. The condition "for each \(x\), some \(y\) gives \(R(x, y)\)" concerns one row of three pairs at a time: a row must contain at least one True, and there are \(2^3 - 1 = 7\) such rows. The three rows are independent, so the count is \(7^3 = 343\). Lab 1 prints 343, which agrees with the count. This is a count of a finite set and is exact.

E2 · What the first-failure line does and does not show (finite enumeration)

Lab 1 prints the least \(n\) in 1 to 50 with \(n^2 + n + 41\) not prime, which is 40, and the value 1681. What does the output establish about \(n = 41\) and about \(n = 60\)?

Solution

The output establishes nothing about \(n = 41\) or \(n = 60\). The search stops at its first failure and prints only that value, and the lab does not test later values. The output does establish that the universal claim over \(n \ge 1\) is false, since \(n = 40\) is a counterexample (Remark 5.1). Any claim about other values would need its own check, and the search would be a finite list of checks, not a proof about all \(n\).

E3 · Changing the domain of the negation check (finite enumeration)

Suppose the negation check of Lab 1 is run on a five-member domain instead of four. State the number of predicates it tests, the number of agreements it should print, and the reason, without running the code.

Solution

The check enumerates \(2^5 = 32\) predicates, so it tests 32. Theorem 3.1 holds for every predicate on every domain, so each predicate gives agreement, and the expected output is 32 of 32. The enumeration is exact for the five-member domain, and the printed agreement would confirm the theorem on that domain. It would not change the proof.

Research

R1 · Formalizing an ambiguous claim

Write two formal readings of "every fund beats its benchmark in some year", where \(B(f, t)\) says that fund \(f\) beats its benchmark in year \(t\). State which reading a test of persistence needs, and which reading a test of breadth needs.

Solution

The first reading is \(\forall f\, \exists t\, B(f, t)\): each fund has at least one year in which it beats its benchmark. The second is \(\exists t\, \forall f\, B(f, t)\) with the phrase read as a single year that every fund beats, which is a different and much stronger claim. A test of breadth, asking whether the beating is spread across funds, uses the first form for each fund. A test of a common shock to all funds in one year uses the second. Example 4.1 shows that the two can disagree on the same data.

R2 · When does a finite check become evidence about a population?

A study checks a universal claim about the firms in its sample and reports that it holds for all of them. State precisely what logic guarantees, what it does not, and what kind of evidence the claim then is.

Solution

Logic guarantees that the claim holds for each firm in the sample, by Remark 5.1. It does not guarantee that the claim holds for firms outside the sample, because the sample is a finite domain and the population is a different domain. Moving from the sample to the population is an inference from data, and its strength depends on how the sample was drawn, not on quantifier logic. The claim is then an empirical claim with a graded strength, not a theorem.

R3 · What a counterexample search can and cannot settle

A colleague says that a search over \(n\) from 1 to \(10^9\) found no counterexample to a universal claim, so the claim is proved for practical purposes. Explain what is wrong with this, using Remark 5.1.

Solution

The search settles the listed domain, \(n\) from 1 to \(10^9\), and nothing beyond it. A universal claim over the integers has infinitely many members outside that range, and Remark 5.1 gives no information about them. A search can refute a universal claim by finding one counterexample, but a search with no counterexample proves nothing about the unsearched members. A proof must cover every member of the domain by an argument, not by enumeration, and the claim is then a theorem with a proof on the page.

  1. What is the negation of "every bond pays a coupon"?

    Options

    Theorem 3.1: the negation of \(\forall x\, P(x)\) is \(\exists x\, \neg P(x)\), which is "some bond pays no coupon".

  2. On an empty domain, what does \(\forall x \in \varnothing\; P(x)\) give under the unit's convention?

    Options

    Remark 2.2: no member fails \(P\), so the universal statement is true.

  3. How many predicates are there on a domain with five members?

    Each member takes True or False independently, so there are \(2^5 = 32\) predicates.

  4. What does a single counterexample establish about a universal claim?

    Options

    Remark 5.1: \(\forall x\, P(x)\) is false exactly when some member fails \(P\).

  5. On \(\mathbb{Z}_5\) with \(R(x, y)\) meaning \(y = x + 1 \pmod 5\), which statement is true?

    Options

    Example 4.1: the forall-exists statement is true and the exists-forall statement is false.

  6. How many binary relations are there on a two-member set?

    A relation chooses True or False for each of the \(2 \times 2 = 4\) pairs, so there are \(2^4 = 16\).

  7. Which statement implies the other, for a predicate \(P(x, y)\) on a fixed domain?

    Options

    Proposition 4.2: a single witness \(y_0\) for every \(x\) gives a witness for each \(x\), and the converse fails.

  8. What is \(40^2 + 40 + 41\)?

    \(1600 + 40 + 41 = 1681\), which equals \(41 \times 41\), so it is not prime.

  9. Why is "no student has passed" not the negation of "every student has passed"?

    Options

    The negation of "every student passed" is "some student did not pass" (Theorem 3.1). "No student passed" is a different, stronger claim.
