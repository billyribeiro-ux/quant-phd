Present value and the discount factor

A payment due later is worth less today, and a valuation needs a number for how much less. Section 1 defines the discount factor, which gives that number for one payment at one time, and the present value, which applies it to an amount. Its form depends on the compounding convention from L04, so one nominal rate gives different factors under different conventions. This lesson proves, under an assumption it states in §1, that the factor lies between zero and one for a positive rate and a positive time. It also shows that three conventions give three present values for the same payment (Lab 1).

1The discount factor and the present value

Money is held in currency units (CU), and amounts in cents are whole numbers, as in L04. Let \(r\) be a rate with a stated compounding convention, and let \(t \ge 0\) be a time in years.

Convention (the real power)

For a positive base \(b\) and a real exponent \(u\), this page defines \(b^{u} := e^{u \ln b}\). This is a convention of this page. L03 does not define a real power. Definition 1.1 and the rest of this lesson use it for fractional and negative real exponents.

Definition 1.1 (discount factor)

The discount factor for \(t\) is the value today of one CU due at time \(t\). It is the reciprocal of the growth factor of L04 over the same period. Under annual effective compounding at \(r\) it is \(DF_{A}(t) = (1 + r)^{-t}\). Under \(m\) equal periods a year at nominal rate \(r\) it is \((1 + r/m)^{-mt}\). Under continuous compounding at \(r\) it is \(e^{-rt}\).

Definition 1.2 (present value)

The present value of a cash amount \(C\) due at time \(t\) is \(C\,DF(t)\), in the units of \(C\). The present value of amounts \(C_{k}\) due at times \(t_{k}\) is the sum \(\sum_{k} C_{k}\,DF(t_{k})\), where \(DF\) is the discount factor under the stated convention.

Remark 1.3 (year fractions are inputs)

Each \(t\) is an input number of years. This lesson takes \(t\) as given and does not define how a pair of dates becomes a year fraction. A different count of days gives a different \(t\), and so a different factor.

Assumption E (the exponential bound)

For every real \(x > 0\), \(1 + x < e^{x}\). L03 states the exponential only through A1 to A3, and this lesson does not derive E from them. E is taken here as an assumption of this page. Three consequences are used below, each proved from E, A1, A2 and L03.

(i) \(e^{x} > 1\) for \(x > 0\), because \(e^{x} > 1 + x > 1\).

(ii) The exponential is strictly increasing. If \(u < v\), then \(e^{v} = e^{u} e^{v-u}\) by A2, and \(e^{u} > 0\) by A1 and \(e^{v-u} > 1\) by (i), so \(e^{v} > e^{u}\).

(iii) The logarithm is strictly increasing on the positive reals. If \(0 < a < b\) and \(\ln a \ge \ln b\), then \(a = e^{\ln a} \ge e^{\ln b} = b\) by (ii), which contradicts \(a < b\). Here \(e^{\ln y} = y\) is L03 Proposition 1.4.

Proposition 1.4 (the annual factor lies between zero and one)

Assume E. Let \(r > 0\). For every \(t > 0\), \(0 < (1 + r)^{-t} < 1\). Moreover, \(t \mapsto (1 + r)^{-t}\) is strictly decreasing for \(t \ge 0\).

Proof

Put \(b = 1 + r\), so \(b > 1\) because \(r > 0\). By the convention, \(b^{u} = e^{u \ln b}\) for every real \(u\). Since \(b > 1\), \(\ln b > 0\), because \(\ln 1 = 0\) (L03 Proposition 1.6(a)) and \(\ln\) is strictly increasing by (iii). Hence \(b^{u} > 0\) for every \(u\) by A1, and \(b^{u} > 1\) for \(u > 0\) by (i), since then \(u \ln b > 0\). For \(t > 0\), \(b^{-t} = e^{-t \ln b} = 1/e^{t \ln b} = 1/b^{t}\) by L03 Proposition 1.3, and \(b^{t} > 1\), so \(0 < b^{-t} < 1\). For \(0 \le s < t\), A2 gives \(e^{-s \ln b} = e^{(t-s) \ln b} \, e^{-t \ln b}\), that is, \(b^{-s} = b^{t-s} \, b^{-t}\). Since \(t - s > 0\), \(b^{t-s} > 1\), so \(b^{-s} > b^{-t}\).

Remark 1.5 (negative rates reverse both claims)

For \(-1 < r < 0\), \(0 < 1 + r < 1\), so \(\ln(1 + r) < 0\) by (iii) and \(\ln 1 = 0\). For \(t > 0\), the exponent \(-t\ln(1 + r)\) is positive, so \((1 + r)^{-t} = e^{-t\ln(1+r)} > 1\) by (i). By (ii) the factor increases in \(t\). Proposition 1.4 assumes \(r > 0\), and for \(-1 < r < 0\) both of its claims reverse.

2Three conventions for one rate

Take one number \(r > 0\) and compare three factors for the same time \(t\): the annual effective factor \((1 + r)^{-t}\), the semiannual factor \((1 + r/2)^{-2t}\), and the continuous factor \(e^{-rt}\). Write \(\delta = \ln(1 + r)\), the equivalent continuous rate of \(r\).

Proposition 2.1 (the equivalent continuous rate)

Let \(r > 0\) and \(\delta = \ln(1 + r)\). For every \(t \ge 0\), \((1 + r)^{-t} = e^{-\delta t}\). The continuous rate \(\delta\) gives the annual effective discount factor exactly.

Proof

By the convention for the real power, with \(b = 1 + r\) and \(u = -t\), \((1 + r)^{-t} = e^{-t \ln(1+r)} = e^{-\delta t}\).

Proposition 2.2 (a continuous rate equal to r discounts more)

Assume E. Let \(r > 0\) and \(t > 0\). Then \(e^{-rt} < (1 + r)^{-t}\). The continuous factor at the numerical rate \(r\) is strictly smaller than the annual effective factor at \(r\).

Proof

Let \(\delta = \ln(1 + r)\). Then \(e^{\delta} = 1 + r\) (L03 Proposition 1.4), and \(1 + r < e^{r}\) by E with \(x = r > 0\). So \(e^{\delta} < e^{r}\), and by (ii) \(\delta < r\). Multiplying by \(t > 0\) gives \(-rt < -\delta t\). By (ii) again, \(e^{-rt} < e^{-\delta t}\), and \(e^{-\delta t} = (1 + r)^{-t}\) by Proposition 2.1.

Remark 2.3 (the same number is not the same rate)

Under Assumption E, Proposition 2.2 says that treating \(r\) as a continuous rate discounts more than treating \(r\) as an annual effective rate. The two conventions are not interchangeable at one number. Lab 1 prints the annual factor and the continuous factor at 0.05 at five times, so the gap at each time is the difference of those two columns.

Proposition 2.4 (semiannual discounting is smaller than annual)

Assume E. Let \(r > 0\) and \(t > 0\). The semiannual factor \((1 + r/2)^{-2t}\) is strictly less than the annual effective factor \((1 + r)^{-t}\).

Proof

Let \(A = (1 + r/2)^{2} = 1 + r + r^{2}/4\) and \(B = 1 + r\). Then \(A - B = r^{2}/4 > 0\), so \(A > B > 0\). For \(t > 0\), the map \(x \mapsto x^{t} = e^{t\ln x}\) is strictly increasing on the positive reals. If \(0 < x < y\), then \(\ln x < \ln y\) by (iii), so \(t\ln x < t\ln y\), and \(e^{t\ln x} < e^{t\ln y}\) by (ii). So \(A^{t} > B^{t}\), and taking reciprocals gives \(A^{-t} < B^{-t}\). The semiannual factor is \(A^{-t}\) and the annual effective factor is \(B^{-t}\), which gives the claim.

Lab 1 · One 5% rate under three conventions

The inputs are illustrative, not data: a rate of 0.05 and a payment of 1,000,000 cents. For five times \(t\) from 0.5 to 10 years, the lab prints the annual, semiannual and continuous factors at 0.05, and the continuous factor at the equivalent rate \(\delta = \ln(1.05)\). Propositions 1.4, 2.1, 2.2 and 2.4 predict that the annual factor falls with \(t\) and lies in \((0, 1)\); that the equivalent-rate factor equals the annual factor; that the continuous factor at 0.05 and the semiannual factor both lie below the annual factor. The lab also prints the present values of 1,000,000 cents at \(t = 5\) in whole cents. The code uses no random draws.

"""M1 L05 lab 1: discount factors for one 5% rate under three conventions.

Inputs are illustrative, not data: a rate r = 0.05, a cash flow of 1,000,000 cents (10,000.00 CU) due after the
year fractions t listed below, and no random draws. A year fraction is an input number of years; this lab does not
define a day-count convention.

Conventions, for a rate r and a time t in years:
  annual effective:    DF = 1 / (1 + r)^t
  semiannual nominal:  DF = 1 / (1 + r/2)^(2t)
  continuous:          DF = e^(-r t)
The continuous factor is also computed at the equivalent continuous rate delta = ln(1 + r). There, e^(-delta t)
must equal the annual effective factor.

Every factor is a Decimal at 60 significant digits. CU is the currency unit; 1 CU is 100 cents.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from decimal import ROUND_HALF_EVEN, Decimal, getcontext

getcontext().prec = 60

RATE = Decimal("0.05")
FACE_CENTS = 1_000_000
YEARS = (Decimal("0.5"), Decimal("1"), Decimal("2"), Decimal("5"), Decimal("10"))


def df_annual(rate: Decimal, t: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """Annual effective discount factor 1/(1 + rate)^t, for a time t in years."""
    return 1 / (1 + rate) ** t


def df_semiannual(rate: Decimal, t: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """Discount factor with two compounding periods a year at nominal rate: 1/(1 + rate/2)^(2t)."""
    return 1 / (1 + rate / 2) ** (2 * t)


def df_continuous(rate: Decimal, t: Decimal) -> Decimal:
    """Continuous discount factor e^(-rate t)."""
    return (-rate * t).exp()


def to_cents(amount: Decimal) -> int:
    """The amount in whole cents, with a tie going to the even cent."""
    return int(amount.quantize(Decimal(1), rounding=ROUND_HALF_EVEN))


def main() -> None:
    """Print the equivalent continuous rate, the factor table, two checks, and the present values at t = 5."""
    delta = (1 + RATE).ln()
    print(f"equivalent_continuous_rate {delta:.10f}")
    gaps: list[Decimal] = []
    decreasing = True
    inside = True
    previous = Decimal(1)
    for t in YEARS:
        annual = df_annual(RATE, t)
        semi = df_semiannual(RATE, t)
        cont = df_continuous(RATE, t)
        equiv = df_continuous(delta, t)
        gaps.append(abs(equiv - annual))
        decreasing = decreasing and annual < previous
        inside = inside and Decimal(0) < annual < Decimal(1)
        previous = annual
        print(
            f"t {t} annual {annual:.10f} semiannual {semi:.10f} "
            f"continuous_at_5pct {cont:.10f} equivalent_continuous {equiv:.10f}"
        )
    print(f"max_gap_equivalent_vs_annual {max(gaps):.10f}")
    print(f"annual_decreasing_in_t {decreasing}")
    print(f"annual_between_zero_and_one {inside}")

    t5 = Decimal("5")
    print("present values at t = 5 of 1,000,000 cents, in whole cents")
    print(f"annual cents {to_cents(FACE_CENTS * df_annual(RATE, t5))}")
    print(f"semiannual cents {to_cents(FACE_CENTS * df_semiannual(RATE, t5))}")
    print(f"continuous cents {to_cents(FACE_CENTS * df_continuous(RATE, t5))}")

3A payment and its present value

Example 3.1 (one payment under three conventions)

Take 1,000,000 cents due in \(t = 5\) years at a rate of 0.05. The annual effective factor is \(1.05^{-5} = 0.7835261665\), so the present value is \(1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.7835261665 = 783{,}526.17\) cents, which is 783,526 cents to the nearest cent. The semiannual factor is \((1.025)^{-10} = 0.7811984017\), so the present value is \(781{,}198.40\) cents, which is 781,198 cents. The continuous factor is \(e^{-0.25} = 0.7788007831\), so the present value is \(778{,}800.78\) cents, which is 778,801 cents. Lab 1 prints all three whole-cent values.

Remark 3.2 (present value adds over payments)

Two amounts due at the same time \(t\) have present values that add to the present value of their sum, because \(DF(t)\) multiplies each amount: \((C_{1} + C_{2})\,DF(t) = C_{1}\,DF(t) + C_{2}\,DF(t)\).

4Where the canon breaks

  • The convention is part of the number. Example 3.1 gives three present values for one payment at the rate 0.05. The semiannual and continuous values differ by 2,397 cents at \(t = 5\) (781,198 minus 778,801, the printed whole-cent values; unrounded, 2,397.62 cents). A quoted rate without its convention does not fix a present value.
  • Year fractions are inputs. Remark 1.3 takes \(t\) as given. The rule that turns dates into years changes \(t\), and so changes the factor. This lesson does not choose a rule.
  • One rate for every date. Each factor here applies one rate \(r\) to one time \(t\). A rate that differs by maturity needs a separate factor for each \(t\), and this lesson does not treat it.
  • Certain payments. The factors discount an amount that is paid as stated. Whether a payment will arrive is a different question, and this lesson does not model it.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A discount factor can exceed one when the rate is positive.”

    Proposition 1.4, under Assumption E, proves \(0 < (1 + r)^{-t} < 1\) for every \(r > 0\) and \(t > 0\). The factor is a value today of one CU due later, and that value is smaller than one CU. Lab 1 prints annual factors from 0.9759000729 at \(t = 0.5\) down to 0.6139132535 at \(t = 10\). Its check that the annual factor decreases in \(t\) (annual_decreasing_in_t True) means that the five printed annual factors fall from one row to the next, so each lies between the first and the last. The check annual_between_zero_and_one True shows that both are below one.

  2. “A semiannual nominal rate of 5% and an annual effective rate of 5% give the same discount factor.”

    Proposition 2.4, under Assumption E, proves that the semiannual factor is strictly smaller. At \(t = 5\) Lab 1 prints 0.7811984017 for the semiannual factor and 0.7835261665 for the annual factor, and the present values are 781,198 and 783,526 cents. The two conventions differ by 2,328 cents (783,526 minus 781,198, the printed whole-cent values; unrounded, 2,327.76 cents).

  3. “Discounting at a continuous 5% gives the annual 5% factor, because both use the number 5%.”

    Proposition 2.2, under Assumption E, proves that \(e^{-0.05t} < (1.05)^{-t}\) for every \(t > 0\). At \(t = 1\), Lab 1 prints 0.9512294245 for the continuous factor and 0.9523809524 for the annual one. The annual factor equals the continuous factor at the equivalent rate \(\delta = \ln(1.05) = 0.0487901642\) (Proposition 2.1).

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · The annual factor over three years

Compute the annual effective discount factor \((1.04)^{-3}\) for a payment due in three years at 4%, to six decimal places.

Solution

The growth factor is \(1.04^{3} = 1.124864\). The discount factor is its reciprocal, \(1/1.124864 = 0.8889963587\). To six decimal places this is 0.888996.

M2 · A present value in cents

A payment of 250,000 cents is due in two years. The rate is 6% annual effective. Compute the present value in whole cents.

Solution

The discount factor is \(1/1.06^{2} = 1/1.1236 = 0.889996440014\) to twelve places, and its value to eight places is 0.88999644. Multiplying the eight-place value, \(250{,}000 \times 0.88999644 = 222{,}499.11\) cents. To the nearest cent, the present value is 222,499 cents.

M3 · A continuous present value

A payment of 500,000 cents is due at \(t = 1.5\) years. The rate is 4% continuous. Compute the present value in whole cents.

Solution

The continuous factor is \(e^{-rt} = e^{-0.04 \times 1.5} = e^{-0.06} = 0.9417645336\). The present value is \(500{,}000 \times 0.9417645336 = 470{,}882.27\) cents. To the nearest cent, 470,882 cents.

Proof

P1 · Present value adds over amounts at one date

Let \(C_{1}\) and \(C_{2}\) be amounts due at the same time \(t\), under one discount factor \(DF(t)\). Prove that the present value of the total equals the sum of the two present values.

Solution

The total is \(C_{1} + C_{2}\), so its present value is \((C_{1} + C_{2})\,DF(t)\). By the distributive law for real numbers this is \(C_{1}\,DF(t) + C_{2}\,DF(t)\). Each term is the present value of one amount, so the present value of the total is the sum of the two present values.

P2 · The factor undoes growth

For \(r > 0\) and a whole number \(t \ge 0\), show that a present value \(C\,(1+r)^{-t}\) compounded at \(r\) for \(t\) years returns \(C\).

Solution

Here \(t\) is a whole number, so compounding a principal \(P\) for \(t\) years at annual effective rate \(r\) gives \(P(1 + r)^{t}\), by Remark 1.3 of L04 with \(m = 1\). Put \(P = C(1 + r)^{-t}\). Then \(P(1+r)^{t} = C(1+r)^{-t}(1+r)^{t}\). For whole \(t\) the convention of §1 agrees with the ordinary power: by A2 and induction on \(t\), \(e^{t\ln(1+r)} = (e^{\ln(1+r)})^{t} = (1+r)^{t}\), using \(e^{\ln y} = y\) (L03 Proposition 1.4). So \((1+r)^{-t}(1+r)^{t} = e^{-t\ln(1+r)} e^{t\ln(1+r)}\), which by A2 and L03 Proposition 1.2 is \(e^{0} = 1\). Hence \(P(1+r)^{t} = C\).

P3 · The factor falls as the rate rises

For a fixed \(t > 0\), prove that \((1 + r)^{-t}\) is strictly decreasing in \(r\) over \(r \ge 0\).

Solution

Let \(0 \le r_{1} < r_{2}\). Then \(1 + r_{1} < 1 + r_{2}\), both positive. The map \(x \mapsto x^{t}\) is strictly increasing on the positive reals for \(t > 0\) (as in the proof of Proposition 2.4, under Assumption E), so \((1 + r_{1})^{t} < (1 + r_{2})^{t}\). Taking reciprocals reverses the inequality, so \((1 + r_{1})^{-t} > (1 + r_{2})^{-t}\).

Empirical

E1 · The semiannual and continuous gap at five years

Read Lab 1. What is the gap in cents between the semiannual and the continuous present values of 1,000,000 cents at \(t = 5\), and in CU?

Solution

Lab 1 prints 781,198 cents for the semiannual value and 778,801 cents for the continuous value. The gap between the printed whole-cent values is \(781{,}198 - 778{,}801 = 2{,}397\) cents, which is 23.97 CU. From unrounded values the gap is 2,397.62 cents.

E2 · The annual and semiannual factors at half a year

Read the \(t = 0.5\) row of Lab 1. By how much does the annual factor exceed the semiannual factor?

Solution

The row prints 0.9759000729 for the annual factor and 0.9756097561 for the semiannual factor. The difference of the printed ten-place values is \(0.9759000729 - 0.9756097561 = 0.0002903168\). From unrounded values it is 0.00029031685, which is 0.0002903169 to ten places. This agrees with Proposition 2.4, because the semiannual factor is the smaller of the two.

E3 · A ten-year present value in cents

Read the \(t = 10\) row of Lab 1 and find the present value of 1,000,000 cents due in ten years under annual compounding at 5%, in whole cents.

Solution

The row prints 0.6139132535 for the annual factor. Then \(1{,}000{,}000 \times 0.6139132535 = 613{,}913.25\) cents, which is 613,913 cents to the nearest cent. Lab 1 prints the annual present value only at \(t = 5\), so this value is derived from the row and is not printed by the lab.

Research

R1 · Discounting at a negative rate

Let \(-1 < r < 0\). Show that \((1 + r)^{-t} > 1\) for \(t > 0\), and that the factor increases with \(t\).

Solution

Since \(0 < 1 + r < 1\), \(\ln(1 + r) < 0\) by (iii) and \(\ln 1 = 0\). So \((1 + r)^{-t} = e^{-t\ln(1+r)}\) by the convention, and the exponent \(-t\ln(1+r)\) is strictly positive for \(t > 0\). Hence \((1 + r)^{-t} > 1\) by (i). For \(t_{2} > t_{1}\), the exponent at \(t_{2}\) is larger, because \(-\ln(1+r) > 0\), so by (ii) the factor at \(t_{2}\) is larger. This reverses both claims of Proposition 1.4, as Remark 1.5 states.

R2 · The annual rate that gives a discount factor of 0.6 in ten years

Find the annual effective rate \(r\) for which the discount factor at \(t = 10\) is 0.6, to six decimal places.

Solution

The condition is \((1 + r)^{-10} = 0.6\), so \(1 + r = (1/0.6)^{1/10}\). Now \(1/0.6 = 1.6666\ldots\), and \((1.6666\ldots)^{0.1} = e^{0.1\ln(1.6666\ldots)} = e^{0.0510826}\). So \(1 + r = 1.052409779149\) (twelve decimal places), and \(r = 0.052410\) to six decimal places. Check: \(1.052409779149^{10} = 1.6666666667\) to ten decimal places, and \(1/1.6666666667 = 0.6\) to ten decimal places. Using the shorter value \(1.0524097791\) instead gives \(1.6666666659\): the error of \(4.9 \times 10^{-11}\) in \(1 + r\) is magnified about 15.8 times in the tenth power, since \(10(1+r)^{9} = 15.8\) at this value.

R3 · The annual rate with the continuous present value of Lab 1

Lab 1 uses a continuous rate of 0.05. Find the annual effective rate \(r\) whose discount factor at \(t = 5\) equals \(e^{-0.25}\), the continuous factor at 0.05 for five years.

Solution

The condition is \((1 + r)^{-5} = e^{-0.25}\). Taking the fifth root of the positive numbers on both sides, \((1 + r)^{-1} = e^{-0.05}\), so \(1 + r = e^{0.05}\) and \(r = e^{0.05} - 1 = 0.0512710964\). So a continuous rate of 0.05 gives the same five-year factor as an annual effective rate of 5.1271%, which is the effective annual rate of continuous compounding at 0.05 (L04, Definition 1.2).

    Options

    Example 3.1 and Lab 1 print the three values. The annual factor is the largest of the three, by Propositions 2.2 and 2.4. Lab 1 prints the continuous value below the semiannual value at \(t = 5\).

  6. What is the present value, in whole cents, of 1,000,000 cents due in five years at an annual effective rate of 5%?

    \(1{,}000{,}000 \times 1.05^{-5} = 783{,}526.17\) cents, which is 783,526 cents (Example 3.1).

  7. What is the discount factor at \(t = 0\)?

    Options

    At \(t = 0\) the exponent is zero in each convention, so the annual factor is \((1+r)^{0} = 1\), the continuous factor is \(e^{0} = 1\), and the semiannual factor is \((1+r/2)^{0} = 1\).

  8. What is the continuous discount factor at 5% for ten years, to six decimal places?

    \(e^{-0.5} = 0.6065306597\), which is 0.606531 to six places (Lab 1 prints 0.6065306597 at \(t = 10\)).

  9. Does a continuous rate of 5% discount a payment at \(t > 0\) by the same factor as an annual effective rate of 5%?

    Options

    The same number used as a continuous rate gives the smaller factor, because \(\ln(1.05) = 0.0487901642 < 0.05\) (Proposition 2.2).
