Lagrange Multipliers for Equality Constraints

  • Uses: the gradient and the implicit function theorem (M2); positive semidefinite matrices (M3); Lesson 1, Proposition 3.2 for the sign of a quadratic form
  • Introduces: Lagrange multiplier
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore

The minimum-variance problem asks for the weights, which must add up to one, that minimize the variance of a portfolio. Adding a constraint to a minimization changes the first-order condition: the gradient of the objective need not vanish, but at a minimizer where the constraint gradient is non-zero it must line up with the gradient of the constraint. This lesson states that condition, proves it under that regularity hypothesis and one stated result from M2 (Assumption 1), and shows that it is not enough on its own. For the budget constraint it gives an exact closed form, which Lab 1 checks in exact arithmetic.

1Constrained minimization

Let \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) be open, and let \(f : U \to \mathbb{R}\) and \(h : U \to \mathbb{R}\) be continuously differentiable. The set \(\{x \in U : h(x) = 0\}\) is the feasible set of the equality constraint \(h(x) = 0\), the set of points that satisfy it. A point \(x^*\) of the feasible set is a local minimizer of \(f\) on the feasible set when \(f(x^*) \le f(x)\) for every feasible \(x\) in some open ball around \(x^*\). Local and global minimizers are different objects, and Example 2.6 shows a stationary point that is not even a local minimizer.

The gradient of \(f\) at \(x\) is the column vector \(\nabla f(x)\) of its partial derivatives, and \(\nabla h(x)\) is the gradient of the constraint function. The geometric picture behind the next definition is a motivation only: no result on this page uses it. Its content is that \(\nabla h(x)\), when non-zero, is the normal vector of the level set of \(h\) at \(x\).

2The Lagrange condition

Definition 2.1 (Lagrange multiplier)

Let \(f\) and \(h\) be as above, and let \(x^*\) be a feasible point. A real number \(\lambda\) is a Lagrange multiplier of \(f\) at \(x^*\) for the constraint \(h(x) = 0\) when \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda \nabla h(x^*)\).

The sign convention is a choice: with \(\nabla f + \lambda \nabla h = 0\) the multiplier would change sign. This lesson uses the form \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla h\), so every multiplier in this lesson is read with that sign.

Assumption 1 (implicit function theorem, from M2)

Let \(h\) be continuously differentiable on an open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\), let \(x^*\) be a point of \(U\) with \(h(x^*) = 0\), and let \(\partial_k h(x^*) \ne 0\). Then there are a neighbourhood \(N\) of \(x^*_{-k}\) and a continuously differentiable \(\varphi : N \to \mathbb{R}\) with \(\varphi(x^*_{-k}) = x^*_k\), \(h(x_{-k}, \varphi(x_{-k})) = 0\) for \(x_{-k} \in N\), and \(\nabla \varphi(x^*_{-k}) = -\nabla_{-k} h(x^*) / \partial_k h(x^*)\). This is the implicit function theorem, which M2 states. It is assumed here and not proved on this page.

Theorem 2.2 (necessity of the Lagrange condition)

Assuming Assumption 1, let \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) be open, let \(f, h : U \to \mathbb{R}\) be continuously differentiable, and let \(x^*\) be a point of \(U\) with \(h(x^*) = 0\) and \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). Suppose \(x^*\) is a local minimizer of \(f\) on the feasible set \(\{x \in U : h(x) = 0\}\). Then there is a real \(\lambda\) with \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda \nabla h(x^*)\).

The hypotheses are: \(U\) open, both functions continuously differentiable, the point feasible, the regularity condition \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\), and minimality on the feasible set. The regularity condition is stated as a hypothesis, not assumed silently; Example 2.5 shows that the conclusion can fail without it.

Proof

Write \(p = \nabla f(x^*)\) and \(g = \nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). Suppose, for contradiction, that \(p\) is not a real multiple of \(g\). Put \(c = (p \cdot g)/(g \cdot g)\) and \(r = p - c g\). Then \(r \cdot g = p \cdot g - c\, g \cdot g = 0\), and \(r \ne 0\) because \(p\) is not a multiple of \(g\). Let \(d = -r\). Then \(g \cdot d = 0\), and

\[p \cdot d = -p \cdot r = -\bigl(|r|^2 + c\, g \cdot r\bigr) = -|r|^2 < 0,\]

where the middle step uses \(p = r + c g\), and the last step uses \(g \cdot r = 0\).

Because \(g \ne 0\), some coordinate \(k\) has \(\partial_k h(x^*) \ne 0\). Write \(x = (x_{-k}, x_k)\). Assumption 1 gives a neighbourhood of \(x^*_{-k}\) and a continuously differentiable function \(\varphi\) on it with \(\varphi(x^*_{-k}) = x^*_k\), \(h(x_{-k}, \varphi(x_{-k})) = 0\) and \(\nabla\varphi(x^*_{-k}) = -\nabla_{-k} h(x^*) / \partial_k h(x^*)\). Define \(\gamma(t) = (x^*_{-k} + t d_{-k},\ \varphi(x^*_{-k} + t d_{-k}))\) for small \(|t|\). Then \(\gamma\) is continuously differentiable, \(h(\gamma(t)) = 0\) (so \(\gamma(t)\) is feasible), \(\gamma(0) = x^*\), and \(\gamma'(0) = d\). The last equality holds in the \(k\)-th coordinate because \(g \cdot d = 0\) gives \(d_k = -\sum_{j \ne k} g_j d_j / g_k = \nabla\varphi(x^*_{-k}) \cdot d_{-k}\).

Since \(f\) is differentiable at \(x^*\), \(f(\gamma(t)) = f(x^*) + t\, p \cdot d + o(t)\) as \(t \to 0\). Because \(p \cdot d < 0\), there is a \(t_0 > 0\) with \(f(\gamma(t)) < f(x^*)\) for \(0 < t < t_0\). Those \(\gamma(t)\) are feasible points arbitrarily close to \(x^*\), which contradicts the minimality of \(x^*\). So \(p\) is a multiple of \(g\): \(p = \lambda g\) for some real \(\lambda\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.3 (the proof uses only the local curve)

The proof builds one feasible curve through \(x^*\) with a prescribed tangent \(d\) in the tangent space \(\{v : \nabla h(x^*) \cdot v = 0\}\). Minimality rules out every such curve along which \(f\) decreases to first order, and that leaves only the multiples of \(\nabla h(x^*)\).

Example 2.5 (the regularity hypothesis is needed)

Let \(U = \mathbb{R}^2\), \(h(x, y) = y^2 - x^3\) and \(f(x, y) = x\). At the origin \(\nabla h = (-3x^2, 2y) = (0, 0)\), so the regularity condition fails. The feasible set \(\{y^2 = x^3\}\) forces \(x = (y^2)^{1/3} \ge 0\), so \(f = x \ge 0 = f(0, 0)\) on it, and the origin is a minimizer. But \(\nabla f(0, 0) = (1, 0)\) is not a multiple of \(\nabla h(0, 0) = (0, 0)\), because \(\lambda (0, 0) = (0, 0)\) for every \(\lambda\). So the minimizer has no Lagrange multiplier, and Theorem 2.2 does not apply.

Example 2.6 (a stationary point that is not a minimizer)

Let \(f(x, y) = y^3\) and \(h(x, y) = x\) on \(\mathbb{R}^2\). Here \(\nabla h = (1, 0) \ne 0\), and \(\nabla f(0, 0) = (0, 3y^2)\) at \(y = 0\) is \((0, 0) = 0 \cdot \nabla h\), so the origin satisfies the Lagrange condition with \(\lambda = 0\). The feasible set is the \(y\)-axis. For every \(y > 0\) the point \((0, -y)\) is feasible and \(f(0, -y) = -y^3 < 0 = f(0, 0)\). These points lie arbitrarily close to the origin, so the origin is not a local minimizer, and therefore not a global one. The witness \((0, -1)\) alone already refutes global minimality.

Proposition 2.4 (uniqueness of the multiplier)

Let \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). If \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are both Lagrange multipliers of \(f\) at \(x^*\) for the constraint \(h = 0\), then \(\lambda = \mu\).

Proof

Subtracting the two conditions gives \((\lambda - \mu) \nabla h(x^*) = 0\). Since \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\), its coordinates are not all zero, so \(\lambda - \mu = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

3A closed form under a budget constraint

A symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix \(\Sigma\) is positive definite when \(x^{\top} \Sigma x > 0\) for every \(x \ne 0\). Positive definiteness is stronger than the non-negativity of Lesson 1, Proposition 3.2, because it excludes a zero value at a non-zero \(x\). Let \(\mathbf{1}\) be the vector of \(n\) ones, and consider

\[\min_{w \in \mathbb{R}^n}\; f(w) = \tfrac12\, w^{\top} \Sigma w \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1.\]

The constraint \(h(w) = \mathbf{1}^{\top} w - 1\) has gradient \(\nabla h = \mathbf{1} \ne 0\), so Theorem 2.2 applies to it under Assumption 1. The next proposition does not rely on Theorem 2.2, because it proves the minimum directly.

Proposition 3.2 (the minimum-variance weights)

Let \(\Sigma\) be a positive definite \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(\Sigma\) is invertible. Put \(u = \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{1}\), \(s = \mathbf{1}^{\top} u\) and \(\lambda = 1/s\), and let \(w^* = \lambda u\). Then:

(a) \(s > 0\), so \(\lambda\) is well defined; (b) \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w^* = 1\); (c) \(\nabla f(w^*) = \Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\), so \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier at \(w^*\); (d) \(w^*\) is the unique minimizer of \(f\) on \(\{w : \mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1\}\).

The hypotheses are that \(\Sigma\) is symmetric and positive definite, and that the constraint is the budget \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1\). No condition on the sign of \(w\) is imposed.

Proof

Invertibility. If \(\Sigma x = 0\) for some \(x\), then \(x^{\top} \Sigma x = 0\), which forces \(x = 0\) by positive definiteness. So the only solution of \(\Sigma x = 0\) is zero, and \(\Sigma\) is invertible.

(a). The vector \(u\) satisfies \(\Sigma u = \mathbf{1} \ne 0\), so \(u \ne 0\). Then \(s = \mathbf{1}^{\top} u = (\Sigma u)^{\top} u = u^{\top} \Sigma u > 0\), using symmetry of \(\Sigma\) in \((\Sigma u)^{\top} = u^{\top} \Sigma\).

(b). \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}^{\top} u = \lambda s = 1\).

(c). \(\nabla f(w) = \Sigma w\), because \(\nabla (\tfrac12 w^{\top} \Sigma w) = \tfrac12 (\Sigma + \Sigma^{\top}) w = \Sigma w\). Then \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \Sigma u = \lambda \mathbf{1}\).

(d). Let \(w\) satisfy \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1\), and put \(d = w - w^*\), so \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\). Expanding,

\[f(w) = f(w^*) + w^{*\top} \Sigma d + \tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d.\]

By (c) and symmetry, \(w^{*\top} \Sigma d = (\Sigma w^*)^{\top} d = \lambda \mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\). So \(f(w) - f(w^*) = \tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d\). This is non-negative, and it is zero only when \(d = 0\), because \(\Sigma\) is positive definite. Hence \(w^*\) is the unique minimizer. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.3 (the matrix of Lab 1 is positive definite)

Let \(A\) be any real \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A^{\top} A + I\) is symmetric and positive definite.

Proof

The matrix \((A^{\top} A)^{\top} = A^{\top} A\), so \(A^{\top} A + I\) is symmetric. For any \(x\), \(x^{\top}(A^{\top} A + I) x = (Ax)^{\top}(Ax) + x^{\top} x = \|Ax\|^2 + \|x\|^2\). If \(x \ne 0\), the second term is positive, and the first is non-negative, so the sum is positive. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · Minimum-variance weights in exact arithmetic

Predicted from Proposition 3.2 and Proposition 3.3 before running. The matrix \(\Sigma = A^{\top} A + I\) is symmetric and positive definite, so the closed form applies. Proposition 3.2 predicts three things. The budget \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w\) equals 1 exactly. The gradient \(\Sigma w\) equals \(\lambda \mathbf{1}\) exactly, so every residual \(\Sigma w - \lambda \mathbf{1}\) is zero. For every direction \(d\) with \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\) and every step \(t\), the increase \(f(w + t d) - f(w)\) equals \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\), which is non-negative. The entries of \(A\), \(\Sigma\), \(\lambda\) and \(w\) are computed from a fixed seed and printed; the lab does not predict them.

"""M7 lab L02: minimum-variance weights under a budget constraint, solved in exact arithmetic.

Standard library only. No randomness and no network. The inputs come from a fixed linear congruential generator,
so every run reads the same numbers. Every computation uses fractions.Fraction, so the residuals are exact zeros
or exact non-zero values, not rounding errors.

SIMULATED: the symmetric positive definite matrix Sigma is generated by this lab from a fixed seed.
It is an illustrative input, not an estimate from data. The lab does not estimate Sigma and does not
claim that it describes any market.

The problem is: minimize f(w) = (1/2) w' Sigma w over w in R^3, subject to 1' w = 1. The lab
1. builds Sigma = A' A + I from a 3 x 3 integer matrix A (so Sigma is symmetric and positive definite);
2. solves Sigma u = 1 by exact Gaussian elimination, sets lambda = 1 / (1' u) and w = lambda u;
3. checks that the budget holds exactly, that the gradient Sigma w equals lambda times the constraint normal 1
   exactly, and that f(w + t d) - f(w) equals (1/2) t^2 d' Sigma d for six directions d with 1' d = 0 and six
   values of t, so the increase is non-negative in every tested case.

Checks on six directions and six step sizes are checks on those cases only. The claim for all directions is the
proof on the page: Proposition 3.2 of the lesson gives the minimizer under the assumption that Sigma is positive
definite, and Proposition 3.3 shows that A' A + I is positive definite for every real square A.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

SEED = 1
SIZE = 3


def lcg_integers(seed: int, count: int) -> list[int]:
    """Deterministic integers in -3 to 3 from a linear congruential generator with fixed constants."""
    state = seed
    values: list[int] = []
    for _ in range(count):
        state = (1103515245 * state + 12345) % 2**31
        values.append(state % 7 - 3)
    return values


def gram_plus_identity(entries: list[int], size: int) -> list[list[Fraction]]:
    """Sigma = A' A + I for the size x size matrix A given row by row in entries."""
    a = [[entries[i * size + j] for j in range(size)] for i in range(size)]
    sigma: list[list[Fraction]] = []
    for i in range(size):
        row: list[Fraction] = []
        for j in range(size):
            value = sum(a[k][i] * a[k][j] for k in range(size)) + (1 if i == j else 0)
            row.append(Fraction(value))
        sigma.append(row)
    return sigma


def solve(matrix: list[list[Fraction]], rhs: list[Fraction]) -> list[Fraction]:
    """Solve matrix x = rhs by Gaussian elimination with exact fractions and partial pivoting."""
    n = len(rhs)
    rows = [[*matrix[i], rhs[i]] for i in range(n)]
    for col in range(n):
        pivot = next((r for r in range(col, n) if rows[r][col] != 0), None)
        if pivot is None:
            raise ValueError("the matrix is singular, so the system has no unique solution")
        rows[col], rows[pivot] = rows[pivot], rows[col]
        for r in range(n):
            if r != col and rows[r][col] != 0:
                factor = rows[r][col] / rows[col][col]
                rows[r] = [a - factor * b for a, b in zip(rows[r], rows[col], strict=True)]
    return [rows[i][n] / rows[i][i] for i in range(n)]


def matvec(matrix: list[list[Fraction]], x: list[Fraction]) -> list[Fraction]:
    """The product matrix times x, exact."""
    return [sum((a * b for a, b in zip(row, x, strict=True)), Fraction(0)) for row in matrix]


def quadratic(sigma: list[list[Fraction]], x: list[Fraction]) -> Fraction:
    """The value x' Sigma x, exact."""
    return sum((a * b for a, b in zip(x, matvec(sigma, x), strict=True)), Fraction(0))


def directions() -> list[list[Fraction]]:
    """Six directions d with 1' d = 0 (components sum to zero), exact."""
    raw = [(1, -1, 0), (0, 1, -1), (1, 0, -1), (2, -1, -1), (-1, 2, -1), (1, 1, -2)]
    return [[Fraction(c) for c in d] for d in raw]


def main() -> None:
    entries = lcg_integers(SEED, SIZE * SIZE)
    print(f"A entries (row by row, seed {SEED}): {entries}")
    sigma = gram_plus_identity(entries, SIZE)
    print("Sigma = A' A + I:")
    for row in sigma:
        print("  " + " ".join(str(x) for x in row))

    ones = [Fraction(1)] * SIZE
    u = solve(sigma, ones)
    total = sum(u, Fraction(0))
    lam = 1 / total
    w = [lam * x for x in u]
    print(f"1' Sigma^-1 1 = {total}, multiplier lambda = {lam}")
    print("minimum-variance weights w = lambda Sigma^-1 1:")
    print("  " + " ".join(str(x) for x in w))

    budget = sum(w, Fraction(0))
    print(f"budget 1' w = {budget}, equal to 1 exactly: {budget == 1}")
    gradient = matvec(sigma, w)
    residual = [g - lam * one for g, one in zip(gradient, ones, strict=True)]
    exact_zero = all(r == 0 for r in residual)
    print(f"gradient Sigma w minus lambda 1: {[str(r) for r in residual]}, all exactly zero: {exact_zero}")

    fw = quadratic(sigma, w) / 2
    print(f"objective f(w) = (1/2) w' Sigma w = {fw}")
    steps = [Fraction(-1, 2), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1), Fraction(-1), Fraction(2), Fraction(3, 2)]
    identity_ok = 0
    increases_ok = 0
    tested = 0
    for d in directions():
        dsd = quadratic(sigma, d)
        for t in steps:
            moved = [wi + t * di for wi, di in zip(w, d, strict=True)]
            gap = quadratic(sigma, moved) / 2 - fw
            tested += 1
            identity_ok += gap == t * t * dsd / 2
            increases_ok += gap >= 0
    print(f"f(w + t d) - f(w) equals (1/2) t^2 d' Sigma d in {identity_ok} of {tested} tested cases")
    print(f"f(w + t d) - f(w) is non-negative in {increases_ok} of {tested} tested cases")


main()

The computations use exact fractions, so "exactly zero" and "equal to 1 exactly" are exact statements about the printed inputs, with no rounding. The printed values are \(\Sigma\), \(\lambda = 177/55\) and \(w = (47/55, 17/55, -9/55)\), with \(f(w) = 177/110\). The third weight is negative. The lab imposes no sign constraint, so this solution is a short position in the third asset, and the sign constraint is the subject of Lesson 3. The matrix is simulated: the entries come from a generator with fixed constants and describe no asset or market.

4Where the canon breaks

The proposition above is proved on the page, and Theorem 2.2 is proved on the page from Assumption 1. The hypotheses carry the weight, and four points need care.

  • Stationarity is necessary, not sufficient. Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.2 gives a necessary condition. Example 2.6 shows a stationary point that is not a minimizer, so a solution of \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla h\) must still be checked.
  • The regularity hypothesis cannot be dropped. Example 2.5 gives a minimizer at which \(\nabla h = 0\) and no multiplier exists. Theorem 2.2 is silent there, and the reader should not read it as a claim about such points.
  • A local minimizer is not a global one. Theorem 2.2 is a statement about local minimizers. Proposition 3.2 proves global minimality, and only for the budget problem with positive definite \(\Sigma\). This lesson gives no global argument for other problems.
  • The budget constraint is an equality. The closed form \(w^*\) can have negative entries, as Lab 1 shows. The solution of the problem with \(w \ge 0\) is a different problem, which Lesson 3 treats with the KKT conditions.

5Misconceptions

  1. “At a constrained minimum the gradient of the objective is zero.”

    Theorem 2.2, under Assumption 1, says the gradient is a multiple of the constraint gradient. For \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) on the line \(x + y = 1\), the minimizer is \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) and \(\nabla f = (1, 1) = 1 \cdot \nabla h\), which is not zero. Lab 1 shows the same for the budget problem: its residual line reports \(\Sigma w^* - \lambda \mathbf{1} = 0\) with \(\lambda = 177/55\), so \(\Sigma w^* = \tfrac{177}{55} \mathbf{1}\) is not zero because the multiplier is not zero.

  2. “A point that satisfies the Lagrange condition is a minimizer.”

    Example 2.6 shows that it need not be. The condition is necessary, and the sufficiency for the budget problem comes from the quadratic structure and positive definiteness in Proposition 3.2, not from the condition alone.

  3. “The Lagrange condition holds at every minimizer, whatever the constraint.”

    Theorem 2.2 requires \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). Example 2.5 is a minimizer where \(\nabla h\) vanishes and no multiplier exists, so the hypothesis is needed for the conclusion.

Mechanics

M1 · The point of the line nearest the origin

Minimize \(x^2 + y^2\) subject to \(x + y = 1\). Find the minimizer and the multiplier.

Solution

Here \(f = x^2 + y^2\) and \(h = x + y - 1\), so \(\nabla f = (2x, 2y)\) and \(\nabla h = (1, 1) \ne 0\). The Lagrange condition gives \((2x, 2y) = \lambda (1, 1)\), so \(x = y = \lambda / 2\). The constraint gives \(\lambda = 1\), so \(x = y = \tfrac12\). The multiplier is \(\lambda = 1\), and the minimizer is \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). The point is the foot of the perpendicular from the origin to the line, and it is the closest point of the line to the origin, since \(x^2 + y^2\) is the squared distance.

M2 · The closed form for a diagonal matrix

Let \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)\). Apply Proposition 3.2: compute \(u\), \(s\), \(\lambda\), \(w^*\) and \(f(w^*)\).

Solution

Solving \(\Sigma u = \mathbf{1}\) gives \(u = (1, \tfrac12)\). Then \(s = 1 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac32\), so \(\lambda = \tfrac23\) and \(w^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\). The budget is \(\tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = 1\). The gradient is \(\Sigma w^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac23) = \tfrac23 \mathbf{1}\), as Proposition 3.2 (c) requires. The objective is \(f(w^*) = \tfrac12 (\tfrac49 \cdot 1 + \tfrac19 \cdot 2) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac13\).

M3 · Checking the increase formula by hand

With \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)\) and \(w^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\) from Problem M2, let \(d = (1, -1)\) and \(t = 1\). Compute \(f(w^* + t d) - f(w^*)\) directly and compare it with \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\).

Solution

The point \(w^* + d = (\tfrac53, -\tfrac23)\) gives \(f = \tfrac12 (\tfrac{25}{9} + 2 \cdot \tfrac49) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{33}{9} = \tfrac{11}{6}\). From Problem M2, \(f(w^*) = \tfrac13 = \tfrac26\). The increase is \(\tfrac{11}{6} - \tfrac{2}{6} = \tfrac{9}{6} = \tfrac32\). The formula gives \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1 \cdot d^{\top} \Sigma d = \tfrac12 (1 + 2) = \tfrac32\), so the two agree, as the proof of Proposition 3.2 (d) predicts.

P1 · The multiplier of a regular constraint is unique

Prove Proposition 2.4 from the definition of a Lagrange multiplier, and state where the regularity hypothesis is used.

Solution

If \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) both satisfy \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda \nabla h(x^*)\) and \(\nabla f(x^*) = \mu \nabla h(x^*)\), then subtracting gives \((\lambda - \mu) \nabla h(x^*) = 0\). The regularity hypothesis \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\) says that this vector has a non-zero coordinate, so the scalar \(\lambda - \mu\) must be zero. The hypothesis is used only in the last step, and without it any \(\lambda\) would satisfy the condition when \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\).

P2 · Positive definiteness is needed for uniqueness

Let \(\Sigma = 0\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), with the budget \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\). Show that every feasible point is a minimizer, and identify the step of Proposition 3.2 that fails.

Solution

With \(\Sigma = 0\), \(f(w) = 0\) for every \(w\), so every point of the budget line attains the minimum value 0. The minimizer is not unique. In the proof of Proposition 3.2, step (invertibility) fails, because \(\Sigma\) has no inverse and \(u\) is undefined. Step (d) also fails at its last claim: the increase \(\tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d\) is zero for every \(d\), not zero only when \(d = 0\). Positive definiteness is the hypothesis that prevents both failures.

P3 · The minimum value of the budget problem

Under the hypotheses of Proposition 3.2, show that \(f(w^*) = \tfrac{1}{2s}\), and that \(f(w^*) = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\).

Solution

By Proposition 3.2 (c), \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\), so \(w^{*\top} \Sigma w^* = \lambda\, \mathbf{1}^{\top} w^* = \lambda\) by the budget (b). Therefore \(f(w^*) = \tfrac12 \lambda\). Since \(\lambda = 1/s\), this is \(\tfrac{1}{2s}\). The value is the reciprocal of \(s = \mathbf{1}^{\top} \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{1}\), halved.

Empirical

E1 · Checking the first row of Sigma times w (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints \(\Sigma\) with first row \(4\ 2\ 5\) and \(w = (47/55, 17/55, -9/55)\). Compute the first coordinate of \(\Sigma w\) from these printed values and compare it with \(\lambda\).

Solution

The first coordinate is \(4 \cdot \tfrac{47}{55} + 2 \cdot \tfrac{17}{55} + 5 \cdot \left(-\tfrac{9}{55}\right) = \tfrac{188 + 34 - 45}{55} = \tfrac{177}{55}\). This equals the printed multiplier \(\lambda = 177/55\), and the lab's residual line reports that every coordinate of \(\Sigma w - \lambda \mathbf{1}\) is exactly zero. The check uses only printed values, and the arithmetic is exact.

E2 · What the 36 of 36 lines do and do not show (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints that the increase equals \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\) in 36 of 36 tested cases and is non-negative in 36 of 36. State what these counts establish and what they do not.

Solution

The counts establish the identity and the non-negativity for the six directions and six step sizes in the lab, because the arithmetic is exact. They do not establish optimality over all directions, which is the work of Proposition 3.2 (d). The proof there covers every feasible direction, and the lab is a numerical instance of it. The identity itself holds for every positive definite \(\Sigma\) by the proof of Proposition 3.2 (d). The count does not extend the claim to other matrices; the proof does.

E3 · Reading the objective value (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints \(f(w) = 177/110\). Recover this value from the printed multiplier, without recomputing \(\Sigma w\) entry by entry.

Solution

By Problem P3, \(f(w^*) = \lambda / 2 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{177}{55} = \tfrac{177}{110}\). This matches the printed value, so the objective line is consistent with the multiplier line.

Research

R1 · The sign constraint changes the problem

The Lab 1 solution has \(w_3 = -9/55 < 0\). Explain why this solution does not answer the question of the minimum-variance problem with \(w \ge 0\), and say which lesson treats that question.

Solution

Proposition 3.2 minimizes over the budget set with no sign restriction, so its unique minimizer is the minimizer of the problem without the constraint \(w \ge 0\). The minimizer has a negative entry, so it violates \(w \ge 0\). A minimizer of the problem with \(w \ge 0\) must lie in the feasible set of that problem, and the lab's point is not in it. The problem with a sign constraint is an inequality-constrained problem, treated in Lesson 3.

R2 · Why the budget constraint satisfies the regularity hypothesis

Show that the budget constraint \(h(w) = \mathbf{1}^{\top} w - 1\) satisfies the regularity hypothesis of Theorem 2.2 at every point, and explain why Proposition 3.2 does not need that theorem.

Solution

The gradient of \(h\) is the constant vector \(\mathbf{1}\), which is non-zero at every point, so the hypothesis \(\nabla h \ne 0\) holds everywhere. Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.2 then gives the necessary condition \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\). Proposition 3.2 does not need the theorem, because part (d) proves minimality directly: \(f(w) - f(w^*) = \tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d \ge 0\) for every feasible \(w\). That is a sufficiency argument, which the necessity theorem cannot provide.

R3 · The multiplier as a rate of change of the minimum value

Let \(c\) be a real number and consider the budget constraint \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = c\) with \(c\) in place of 1. Show that the minimum value is \(c^2 / (2s)\), and that its derivative with respect to \(c\) is \(c/s\). Check that at \(c = 1\) the derivative is the multiplier \(\lambda = 1/s\).

Solution

The proof of Proposition 3.2 with the budget \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = c\) in place of 1 gives the unique minimizer \(c w^*\), since every step is linear in the budget. Its objective is \(\tfrac12 c^2 w^{*\top} \Sigma w^* = \tfrac12 c^2 \lambda \cdot 1 = c^2 / (2s)\), using Problem P3. The derivative of \(c^2/(2s)\) with respect to \(c\) is \(c/s\), which equals \(\lambda\) at \(c = 1\). At \(c = 1\) the derivative of the minimum value equals the multiplier. This is one case computed on the page. The general statement for perturbations of the constraint is not proved on this page.

8Retrieval quiz

Answer from memory, then check. Finishing the quiz adds this lesson's cards to your review deck.

  1. For \(f(w) = \tfrac12 w^{\top} \Sigma w\) with symmetric \(\Sigma\), what is \(\nabla f(w)\)?

    Options

    The factor \(\tfrac12\) cancels the factor 2 that the product rule produces, so the gradient is \(\Sigma w\) (proof of Proposition 3.2 (c)).

  2. At a minimizer of \(f\) on the budget set, with \(\Sigma\) positive definite, the gradient \(\Sigma w^*\) is:

    Options

    Proposition 3.2 (c): \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\). The gradient is not zero unless \(\lambda = 0\).

  3. For \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)\) and the budget \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\), what is the multiplier \(\lambda\)?

    \(u = (1, \tfrac12)\), \(s = \tfrac32\), so \(\lambda = \tfrac23\), about 0.6667 (Problem M2).

  4. For the same problem, what is the second coordinate of the minimizer \(w^*\)?

    \(w^* = \lambda u = \tfrac23 (1, \tfrac12) = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\), so the second coordinate is \(\tfrac13\), about 0.3333.

  5. Which hypothesis of Theorem 2.2 rules out the case in which no Lagrange multiplier exists?

    Options

    Example 2.5 is a minimizer with \(\nabla h = 0\) at which no multiplier exists. The regularity condition excludes that case.

  6. Is a point satisfying \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla h\) always a minimizer on the feasible set?

    Options

    The stationary point \((0, 0)\) of \(f = y^3\) on the line \(x = 0\) is not a minimizer, and \(h = x\) is linear, so linearity of the constraint does not give sufficiency.

  7. For the \(1 \times 1\) matrix \(A = (2)\), what is the value of \(A^{\top} A + I\)?

    \(2 \cdot 2 + 1 = 5\), a positive number, as Proposition 3.3 requires.

  8. For \(d\) with \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\), what is \(f(w^* + t d) - f(w^*)\) when \(\Sigma\) is positive definite?

    Options

    Proposition 3.2 (d): the linear term vanishes because \(w^{*\top} \Sigma d = \lambda \mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\), leaving \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\).

  9. What is the multiplier \(\lambda\) that Lab 1 prints, as a decimal to four places?

    Lab 1 prints \(\lambda = 177/55\), which is \(3.218181\ldots\), so to four places it is 3.2182, for the 3 by 3 matrix \(\Sigma\) printed in the lab.

  10. Which property of \(\Sigma\) guarantees that \(\Sigma\) is invertible and the minimizer unique?

    Options

    Positive definiteness gives invertibility and the strict increase in Proposition 3.2 (d). Symmetry does not: \(\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) is symmetric and not positive definite. A positive trace does not either: \(\operatorname{diag}(5, -1)\) has trace 4 and is not positive definite.

  11. For \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)\) and the budget \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\), what is the minimum value of \(f\)?

    Problem M2 gives \(f(w^*) = \tfrac13\). Problem P3 gives \(\lambda/2 = \tfrac13\) as well.
