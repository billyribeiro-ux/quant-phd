The KKT Conditions

  • Uses: convex function (Lesson 1), Lagrange multiplier (Lesson 2), gradient and positive semidefinite matrix (M2 and M3)
  • Introduces: Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A minimizer of a function subject to constraints can sit on the boundary of the feasible set, where some constraints hold with equality and others do not. The KKT conditions are four checkable tests that a candidate point and a set of multipliers must pass. This lesson states them, proves that they are sufficient for optimality when the problem is convex, shows by example that convexity cannot be dropped, and applies them to a two-asset allocation problem. Lab 1 checks each condition with exact arithmetic.

1The problem and the conditions

Definition 1.1 (the problem (P))

Let \(f\), \(g_1, \dots, g_m\) and \(h_1, \dots, h_p\) be real functions on \(\R^n\), each differentiable. The problem (P) is to minimize \(f(x)\) over \(x \in \R^n\) subject to \(g_i(x) \le 0\) for \(i = 1, \dots, m\) and \(h_j(x) = 0\) for \(j = 1, \dots, p\). The feasible set \(F\) is the set of points that satisfy all \(m + p\) constraints. A point \(x^\ast\) is optimal if \(x^\ast \in F\) and \(f(x^\ast) \le f(x)\) for every \(x \in F\). The value \(p^\ast\) is the infimum of \(f\) over \(F\), when \(F\) is non-empty. A constraint \(g_i(x) \le 0\) is active at \(x\) when \(g_i(x) = 0\), and slack when \(g_i(x) < 0\).

Definition 1.2 (the KKT conditions)

The Lagrangian of (P) is \(L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x)\), for \(\lambda \in \R^m\) and \(\nu \in \R^p\). A pair \((x^\ast, (\lambda, \nu))\) is a Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) point of (P) when it satisfies four conditions:

  • (K1) stationarity. \(\nabla f(x^\ast) + \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i(x^\ast) + \sum_j \nu_j \nabla h_j(x^\ast) = 0\). This says that \(\nabla_x L(x^\ast, \lambda, \nu) = 0\).
  • (K2) primal feasibility. \(x^\ast \in F\).
  • (K3) dual feasibility. \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) for every \(i\).
  • (K4) complementary slackness. \(\lambda_i \, g_i(x^\ast) = 0\) for every \(i\).

A KKT residual is the amount by which a candidate fails one of these four conditions. A residual of zero means the condition holds. The numbers \(\lambda_i\) and \(\nu_j\) are the multipliers of the candidate.

Remark 1.3 (what K4 says)

Condition (K4) says that for each \(i\), at least one of \(\lambda_i\) and \(g_i(x^\ast)\) is zero. It does not say which one. A constraint can be active with a zero multiplier, and a slack constraint must have a zero multiplier. Section 4 uses both cases.

Lemma 1.4 (gradient inequality)

Let \(\varphi : \R^n \to \R\) be convex and differentiable. Then for all \(x, y \in \R^n\), \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(y) + \nabla \varphi(y)^{\top}(x - y)\). In particular, if \(\nabla \varphi(y) = 0\), then \(y\) minimizes \(\varphi\) over \(\R^n\).

Proof

Convexity means \(\varphi(\theta a + (1 - \theta) b) \le \theta \varphi(a) + (1 - \theta) \varphi(b)\) for \(\theta \in [0, 1]\). Fix \(y\) and \(x\). For \(t \in (0, 1]\), take \(a = x\), \(b = y\), \(\theta = t\), which gives \(\varphi(y + t(x - y)) \le t\varphi(x) + (1 - t)\varphi(y)\). Subtract \(\varphi(y)\) and divide by \(t > 0\): \[\frac{\varphi(y + t(x - y)) - \varphi(y)}{t} \le \varphi(x) - \varphi(y).\] As \(t \downarrow 0\), the left side tends to \(\nabla \varphi(y)^{\top}(x - y)\), by the definition of the derivative in the direction \(x - y\). The inequality is preserved in the limit, so \(\nabla \varphi(y)^{\top}(x - y) \le \varphi(x) - \varphi(y)\). If \(\nabla \varphi(y) = 0\), this gives \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(y)\) for every \(x\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 1.5 (a quadratic with a positive semidefinite matrix is convex)

Let \(\Sigma\) be a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix with \(x^{\top}\Sigma x \ge 0\) for all \(x\), and let \(c \in \R^n\). Then \(q(w) = \tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w - c^{\top}w\) is convex on \(\R^n\), and its gradient is \(\nabla q(w) = \Sigma w - c\).

Proof

Let \(x, y \in \R^n\) and \(\theta \in [0, 1]\), and put \(z = \theta x + (1 - \theta) y\). Expand the quadratic part using the symmetry of \(\Sigma\). The linear part \(-c^{\top}\) is the same on both sides, because \(c^{\top}z = \theta c^{\top}x + (1 - \theta) c^{\top}y\), so it cancels. For the quadratic part, \[\begin{aligned} &\theta \tfrac12 x^{\top}\Sigma x + (1 - \theta) \tfrac12 y^{\top}\Sigma y - \tfrac12 z^{\top}\Sigma z \\ &\quad= \tfrac12 \theta (1 - \theta)\,(x - y)^{\top}\Sigma (x - y). \end{aligned}\] The check: the coefficient of \(x^{\top}\Sigma x\) on the left is \(\theta/2 - \theta^2/2 = \theta(1-\theta)/2\), the same for \(y^{\top}\Sigma y\), and the cross term \(-\theta(1-\theta)\,x^{\top}\Sigma y\) matches \(-\theta(1-\theta)\,x^{\top}\Sigma y\) on the right. Since \(\Sigma\) is positive semidefinite, the right side is at least zero, so \(q(z) \le \theta q(x) + (1 - \theta) q(y)\). The gradient formula follows by differentiating \(\tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w\) with \(\Sigma\) symmetric. \(\blacksquare\)

2Sufficiency under convexity

Theorem 2.1 (KKT conditions are sufficient)

Assume that \(f\) and each \(g_i\) are convex and differentiable on \(\R^n\), and that each \(h_j\) is affine. Let \((x^\ast, (\lambda, \nu))\) be a KKT point of (P). Then \(x^\ast\) is optimal for (P), and \(f(x^\ast) = p^\ast\).

Proof

Let \(\varphi(x) = L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x)\). Each term is convex: \(f\) by hypothesis; \(\lambda_i g_i\) because \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) by (K3) and \(g_i\) is convex; \(\nu_j h_j\) because \(h_j\) is affine. A sum of convex functions is convex, so \(\varphi\) is convex and differentiable. Its gradient at \(x^\ast\) is \(\nabla f(x^\ast) + \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i(x^\ast) + \sum_j \nu_j \nabla h_j(x^\ast)\), which is zero by (K1). By Lemma 1.4, \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(x^\ast)\) for every \(x \in \R^n\).

Now evaluate the two ends. At \(x^\ast\), (K4) gives \(\lambda_i g_i(x^\ast) = 0\) and (K2) gives \(h_j(x^\ast) = 0\), so \(\varphi(x^\ast) = f(x^\ast)\). Let \(x\) be any point of \(F\). Then \(g_i(x) \le 0\) and \(\lambda_i \ge 0\), so \(\lambda_i g_i(x) \le 0\), and \(h_j(x) = 0\). Hence \(\varphi(x) \le f(x)\). Combining, \[f(x) \ge \varphi(x) \ge \varphi(x^\ast) = f(x^\ast).\] This holds for every \(x \in F\), and \(x^\ast \in F\) by (K2), so \(x^\ast\) is optimal and \(f(x^\ast) = p^\ast\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.2 (what the proof uses)

The proof uses the four conditions, the convexity of \(f\) and of the \(g_i\), and the affineness of the \(h_j\). It does not use any constraint qualification, and it does not need the multipliers to be unique. Sufficiency is a statement about a candidate that is already known to pass the four tests. Whether such a candidate exists is a separate question, taken up in Section 3.

Example 2.3 (a KKT point that is not optimal)

Take \(n = 1\), \(f(x) = x^3\), and no constraints. Then \(f'(0) = 0\), so \(x^\ast = 0\) with no multipliers satisfies (K1) to (K4) trivially. But \(f(-1) = -1 < 0 = f(0)\), so \(0\) is not optimal. In fact \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(x \to -\infty\), so this problem has no minimizer at all. The function is not convex: at \(x = -1\) and \(y = 0\), with \(\theta = \tfrac12\), the midpoint value is \(f(-\tfrac12) = -\tfrac18\), while the average of the endpoint values is \(\tfrac12 f(-1) + \tfrac12 f(0) = -\tfrac12\). Since \(-\tfrac18 > -\tfrac12\), the convexity inequality fails. This is the only hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 that the example breaks.

3Necessity and the constraint qualification

Example 3.1 (an optimum with no multipliers)

Take \(n = 1\), \(f(x) = x\), \(m = 1\), \(g_1(x) = x^2\), and no equality constraints. The feasible set is \(F = \{x : x^2 \le 0\} = \{0\}\), so \(x^\ast = 0\) is the only feasible point and therefore optimal. Here \(f\) is affine and \(g_1\) is convex, by Lemma 1.5 with \(\Sigma = 2\) and \(c = 0\). The hypotheses of Theorem 2.1 hold. Yet no KKT point exists at \(x^\ast\). The gradient of \(g_1\) at \(0\) is \(2 \cdot 0 = 0\), so (K1) reads \(1 + \lambda_1 \cdot 0 = 0\), which is false for every \(\lambda_1\). The reason is that no point satisfies \(x^2 < 0\), so Slater's condition, defined in Lesson 4, Definition 3.1, fails.

Proposition 3.2 (KKT necessity under Slater's condition, stated without a complete proof)

Assume that \(f\) and each \(g_i\) are convex and differentiable on \(\R^n\), that each \(h_j\) is affine, and that Slater's condition holds: there is a point \(\bar x\) with \(g_i(\bar x) < 0\) for every \(i\) and \(h_j(\bar x) = 0\) for every \(j\). If \(x^\ast\) is optimal for (P), then there exist \(\lambda\) and \(\nu\) such that \((x^\ast, (\lambda, \nu))\) is a KKT point.

Proof sketch (not a complete proof)

The standard argument separates a convex set built from the objective and constraint values, at the optimum, from a point, using a separating hyperplane. The normal of the hyperplane supplies the multipliers. This page uses the separation theorem without proof. It also does not carry out the step that uses \(\bar x\) to show that the objective coefficient of the normal is strictly positive, which is what allows the multipliers to be normalized. The page cites the result to boyd2004convex as the general statement that the curriculum lists for this unit. No passage of that source has been read for this page, so no locator is given, and the grade is C.

4A two-asset problem

Consider the allocation problem

\[\begin{aligned} \text{(P}_{\mathrm{A}}\text{)}\quad &\min_{w \in \R^2}\ f(w) = \tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w - \mu^{\top} w \\ &\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1,\quad w_1 \ge 0,\quad w_2 \ge 0. \end{aligned}\]

with \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and a vector \(\mu \in \R^2\). The matrix \(\Sigma\) and the vector \(\mu\) are given inputs of this unit, chosen by hand as illustrative numbers. They are SIMULATED and are not market data. The objective has the form \(\tfrac{\gamma}{2} w^{\top}\Sigma w - \mu^{\top} w\) with \(\gamma = 1\), and returns are in one period with no annualization. The matrix is symmetric, and for any \(a, b\), \((a, b)\Sigma(a, b)^{\top} = a^2 + b^2 + (a + b)^2 \ge 0\), so Lemma 1.5 applies. The constraints are affine, so \(g_i(w) = -w_i\) are convex, and \(h(w) = \mathbf{1}^{\top}w - 1\) is affine. Theorem 2.1 therefore applies to any KKT point of (PA). In the conditions below, \(\nabla g_1 = (-1, 0)^{\top}\), \(\nabla g_2 = (0, -1)^{\top}\), and \(\nabla h = (1, 1)^{\top}\).

Example 4.1 (the active instance, \(\mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac15)\))

Let \(w^\ast = (1, 0)\). Then \(\nabla f(w^\ast) = \Sigma w^\ast - \mu = (2, 1) - (\tfrac32, \tfrac15) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac45)\). Feasibility (K2) holds: \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}w^\ast = 1\), \(g_1(w^\ast) = -1 \le 0\), and \(g_2(w^\ast) = 0 \le 0\). Complementary slackness (K4) for the first constraint has \(g_1(w^\ast) = -1 \neq 0\), so \(\lambda_1 = 0\). Stationarity (K1) reads, componentwise, \(\tfrac12 + \nu - \lambda_1 = 0\) and \(\tfrac45 + \nu - \lambda_2 = 0\). With \(\lambda_1 = 0\), the first gives \(\nu = -\tfrac12\), and the second gives \(\lambda_2 = \tfrac45 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac{3}{10}\). Then \(\lambda_2 \ge 0\) (K3), and \(\lambda_2 g_2(w^\ast) = \tfrac{3}{10} \cdot 0 = 0\) (K4). So \((w^\ast, (\lambda, \nu))\) with \(\lambda = (0, \tfrac{3}{10})\) and \(\nu = -\tfrac12\) is a KKT point. By Theorem 2.1, \(w^\ast\) is optimal, and \(p^\ast = f(w^\ast) = \tfrac12 \cdot 2 - \tfrac32 = -\tfrac12\). The binding constraint is \(w_2 \ge 0\), and it carries the positive multiplier.

Example 4.2 (the inactive instance, \(\mu = (1, 1)\))

Let \(w^\ast = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). Then \(\Sigma w^\ast = (\tfrac32, \tfrac32)\), so \(\nabla f(w^\ast) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). Both weights are positive, so \(g_1(w^\ast) = g_2(w^\ast) = -\tfrac12 \neq 0\), and (K4) forces \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0\). Stationarity then reads \(\tfrac12 + \nu = 0\) in both components, so \(\nu = -\tfrac12\). Feasibility and dual feasibility hold, so this is a KKT point, and Theorem 2.1 gives optimality. The optimal value is \(f(w^\ast) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac32 - 1 = -\tfrac14\), where \(w^{\ast\top}\Sigma w^\ast = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac32 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac32 = \tfrac32\) and \(\mu^{\top}w^\ast = 1\).

"""M7 lab L03: KKT residuals for a two-asset problem with a budget and a non-negativity constraint.

The problem is minimize f(w) = (1/2) w'Sigma w - mu'w over w in R^2, subject to 1'w = 1 and w >= 0.
Sigma is a given symmetric positive-definite matrix and mu a given vector. Both are chosen by hand
for this lab as illustrative inputs, SIMULATED and not market data. The returns are in one period,
with no annualization.

Every number is an exact fractions.Fraction, so each residual is exact and nothing is rounded.
The lab checks the four KKT conditions at a candidate point and its multipliers, and it compares
the candidate with a grid of 101 feasible points. The grid is numerical evidence and not a proof:
the proof that a KKT point is optimal is Theorem 2.1 on the page.

Standard library only. No randomness and no network.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

Vector = tuple[Fraction, Fraction]

SIGMA: tuple[tuple[Fraction, Fraction], tuple[Fraction, Fraction]] = (
    (Fraction(2), Fraction(1)),
    (Fraction(1), Fraction(2)),
)
GRID_STEPS = 100


def matvec(m: tuple[tuple[Fraction, Fraction], tuple[Fraction, Fraction]], v: Vector) -> Vector:
    """The product m v for a 2 by 2 matrix m and a vector v."""
    return (m[0][0] * v[0] + m[0][1] * v[1], m[1][0] * v[0] + m[1][1] * v[1])


def dot(u: Vector, v: Vector) -> Fraction:
    """The inner product u'v of two vectors in R^2."""
    return u[0] * v[0] + u[1] * v[1]


def objective(w: Vector, mu: Vector) -> Fraction:
    """f(w) = (1/2) w'Sigma w - mu'w, with Sigma from SIGMA."""
    return Fraction(1, 2) * dot(w, matvec(SIGMA, w)) - dot(mu, w)


def kkt_residuals(w: Vector, nu: Fraction, lam: Vector, mu: Vector) -> dict[str, object]:
    """The four KKT residuals at (w, nu, lam) for the budget h(w) = 1'w - 1 and constraints g_i(w) = -w_i.

    Stationarity: grad f + nu * 1 - lam (the gradient of lam_i g_i is -lam_i e_i), a vector.
    Primal: the largest violation of 1'w = 1 and of w_i >= 0, which is 0 when both hold.
    Dual: the largest violation of lam_i >= 0, which is 0 when all multipliers are non-negative.
    Complementary slackness: the largest of |lam_i * w_i|, since g_i(w) = -w_i.
    """
    grad = (matvec(SIGMA, w)[0] - mu[0], matvec(SIGMA, w)[1] - mu[1])
    stationarity = (grad[0] + nu - lam[0], grad[1] + nu - lam[1])
    primal = max(abs(w[0] + w[1] - 1), max(-w[0], Fraction(0)), max(-w[1], Fraction(0)))
    dual = max(-lam[0], -lam[1], Fraction(0))
    slack = max(abs(lam[0] * w[0]), abs(lam[1] * w[1]))
    exact = stationarity == (Fraction(0), Fraction(0)) and primal == 0 and dual == 0 and slack == 0
    return {
        "stationarity": stationarity,
        "primal": primal,
        "dual": dual,
        "slack": slack,
        "holds exactly": exact,
    }


def grid_minimum(mu: Vector) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """The smallest objective over w = (k/N, 1 - k/N) for k = 0..N, and the w1 that attains it."""
    best_w1 = Fraction(0)
    best = objective((Fraction(0), Fraction(1)), mu)
    for k in range(GRID_STEPS + 1):
        w1 = Fraction(k, GRID_STEPS)
        value = objective((w1, 1 - w1), mu)
        if value < best:
            best, best_w1 = value, w1
    return best, best_w1


def report(label: str, mu: Vector, w: Vector, nu: Fraction, lam: Vector) -> None:
    print(f"{label}: mu = ({mu[0]}, {mu[1]})")
    print(f"  candidate w = ({w[0]}, {w[1]}), nu = {nu}, lambda = ({lam[0]}, {lam[1]})")
    res = kkt_residuals(w, nu, lam, mu)
    s = res["stationarity"]
    assert isinstance(s, tuple)
    print(f"  stationarity residual: ({s[0]}, {s[1]})")
    print(f"  primal residual: {res['primal']}")
    print(f"  dual residual: {res['dual']}")
    print(f"  complementary slackness max |lambda_i w_i|: {res['slack']}")
    print(f"  all four KKT conditions hold exactly: {res['holds exactly']}")
    print(f"  objective at the candidate: {objective(w, mu)}")
    best, best_w1 = grid_minimum(mu)
    print(f"  smallest objective on the {GRID_STEPS + 1} grid points: {best} at w1 = {best_w1}")
    print(f"  the grid minimum equals the candidate objective: {best == objective(w, mu)}")


def main() -> None:
    half = Fraction(1, 2)
    # Active instance: the candidate puts all weight on asset 1, so the constraint w2 >= 0 binds.
    # Stationarity in row 1 gives nu = mu1 - 2 = -1/2; row 2 gives lambda2 = 1 - mu2 + nu = 3/10.
    report(
        "active instance",
        (Fraction(3, 2), Fraction(1, 5)),
        (Fraction(1), Fraction(0)),
        Fraction(-1, 2),
        (Fraction(0), Fraction(3, 10)),
    )
    # Inactive instance: both weights are positive, so the non-negativity multipliers are zero.
    report(
        "inactive instance",
        (Fraction(1), Fraction(1)),
        (half, half),
        Fraction(-1, 2),
        (Fraction(0), Fraction(0)),
    )
    # Two deliberate failures on the active instance, to show what each condition detects.
    active_mu = (Fraction(3, 2), Fraction(1, 5))
    off = kkt_residuals((half, half), Fraction(-1, 2), (Fraction(0), Fraction(3, 10)), active_mu)
    s = off["stationarity"]
    assert isinstance(s, tuple)
    print(f"off-solution point w = (1/2, 1/2) with the active multipliers: stationarity residual ({s[0]}, {s[1]})")
    flipped = kkt_residuals((Fraction(1), Fraction(0)), Fraction(-1, 2), (Fraction(0), Fraction(-3, 10)), active_mu)
    print(f"flipped sign lambda2 = -3/10 at the active candidate: dual residual {flipped['dual']}")

Every value above is an exact fraction, so the zero residuals are exact zeros and not values below a tolerance. Each KKT condition is computed for the candidate, and the printed lines show which condition a deliberate error breaks. The grid has 101 feasible points, the points \((k/100, 1 - k/100)\), and the lab reports the smallest objective among them. On these points the candidate's objective is the smallest. That is a finite check, and it does not prove optimality for all feasible points. Theorem 2.1 gives the proof, because the data of (PA) are convex. The lab does not show that the multipliers are unique, and the page does not claim it.

Remark 4.3 (the stationary point of the objective alone)

Solving \(\nabla f(w) = 0\) with no constraints gives \(w = \Sigma^{-1}\mu\). For \(\mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac15)\) and \(\Sigma^{-1} = \tfrac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), this is \(w = (\tfrac{14}{15}, -\tfrac{11}{30})\). It has a negative second weight, and its weights sum to \(\tfrac{17}{30} \neq 1\), so it is not feasible. Feasibility and the multipliers are part of the test, and stationarity of \(f\) alone decides nothing.

5Where the canon breaks

The conditions of this lesson carry four limits, and each one is shown by an example on the page.

  • A KKT point is not automatically a minimizer. Example 2.3 gives a KKT point of a non-convex function that is not a minimizer, and that has no minimizer at all. Theorem 2.1 needs the convexity hypotheses.
  • Without a constraint qualification, a minimizer can have no multipliers. Example 3.1 has a convex problem whose optimum satisfies no KKT conditions. Proposition 3.2 gives the necessity direction under Slater's condition, and its grade is C because the page does not prove it.
  • Lab 1 checks a finite set. The residuals are exact at the printed candidates, and the grid comparison covers 101 points. Neither one is the proof of optimality, which comes from Theorem 2.1.
  • The lesson does not solve a KKT system in general. The two instances are solved by hand, with the active set chosen by inspection. Finding the active set for a larger problem is the subject of Lesson 5.

6Misconceptions

  1. “Any point that satisfies the KKT conditions is the optimum.”

    That holds only under convexity. Theorem 2.1 requires \(f\) and the \(g_i\) to be convex, and Example 2.3 shows a KKT point of \(x^3\) that is not a minimizer. Without convexity, a KKT point can be a minimizer, a maximizer or neither, and the four conditions do not tell these apart.

  2. “Complementary slackness means every constraint is binding at the optimum.”

    Condition (K4) says \(\lambda_i g_i(x^\ast) = 0\), which allows either factor to be zero. In Example 4.1 the constraint \(w_1 \ge 0\) is slack, with \(g_1(w^\ast) = -1\), and its multiplier is zero. In Example 4.2 both constraints are slack and both multipliers are zero. The binding constraint in Example 4.1 has the positive multiplier \(\tfrac{3}{10}\). A positive multiplier forces its constraint to bind, by (K4). A zero multiplier does not force its constraint to be slack, so the two cases must be read off the point itself.

  3. “The point where the gradient of the objective vanishes is the answer.”

    Remark 4.3 gives the unconstrained stationary point of the two-asset objective, \((\tfrac{14}{15}, -\tfrac{11}{30})\). Its second weight is negative and its weights sum to \(\tfrac{17}{30}\), so it violates two constraints. Stationarity must be combined with feasibility and with the multipliers, which is what (K1) to (K4) do together.

M1 · KKT for a circle-and-line problem

Minimize \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) subject to \(g(x, y) = 1 - x - y \le 0\). Find a KKT point and its optimal value.

Solution

Here \(\nabla f = (2x, 2y)\) and \(\nabla g = (-1, -1)\). Stationarity (K1) gives \(2x - \lambda = 0\) and \(2y - \lambda = 0\), so \(x = y = \lambda/2\). Complementary slackness (K4) with \(\lambda\) possibly positive forces \(g(x, y) = 0\), since a zero multiplier would give \(x = y = 0\), which violates \(x + y \ge 1\). So \(x + y = 1\), which with \(x = y\) gives \(x = y = \tfrac12\) and \(\lambda = 1\). Then \(\lambda \ge 0\) (K3). The point is optimal by Theorem 2.1, since \(f\) and \(g\) are convex, and \(p^\ast = f(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) = \tfrac12\).

M2 · Comparing two feasible points in Example 4.2

For the inactive instance (\(\mu = (1, 1)\)), compute \(f(\tfrac35, \tfrac25)\) exactly and compare it with the optimal value.

Solution

We have \(\Sigma w = (2 \cdot \tfrac35 + \tfrac25, \tfrac35 + 2 \cdot \tfrac25) = (\tfrac85, \tfrac75)\). Then \(w^{\top}\Sigma w = \tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac85 + \tfrac25 \cdot \tfrac75 = \tfrac{24}{25} + \tfrac{14}{25} = \tfrac{38}{25}\), so \(\tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w = \tfrac{19}{25}\). Also \(\mu^{\top}w = 1\). Hence \(f(\tfrac35, \tfrac25) = \tfrac{19}{25} - 1 = -\tfrac{6}{25}\). Since \(-\tfrac{6}{25} = -0.24 > -0.25 = -\tfrac14\), the point is feasible and worse than the optimum, as Example 4.2 requires.

M3 · No multipliers at a non-optimal candidate

For the active instance (\(\mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac15)\)), show that \(w = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) is not a KKT point for any multipliers.

Solution

Both weights are positive, so \(g_1(w) = g_2(w) = -\tfrac12 \ne 0\), and (K4) forces \(\lambda = 0\). Then (K1) requires \(\nabla f(w) + \nu \mathbf{1} = 0\). Here \(\nabla f(w) = \Sigma w - \mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac32) - (\tfrac32, \tfrac15) = (0, \tfrac{13}{10})\). The first component forces \(\nu = 0\), and then the second component is \(\tfrac{13}{10} \ne 0\). So no \(\nu\) works, and no KKT point exists at this candidate. Its objective value is \(\tfrac34 - \tfrac{17}{20} = -\tfrac{1}{10}\), which is above the optimum \(-\tfrac12\) of Example 4.1, so Theorem 2.1 gives no certificate here.

P1 · Convexity of a multiplier term

Let \(g\) be convex and \(\lambda \ge 0\). Prove that \(\lambda g\) is convex, and state where \(\lambda \ge 0\) is used.

Solution

For \(\theta \in [0, 1]\), convexity of \(g\) gives \(g(\theta a + (1 - \theta)b) \le \theta g(a) + (1 - \theta) g(b)\). Multiply by \(\lambda \ge 0\), which preserves the inequality: \(\lambda g(\theta a + (1 - \theta) b) \le \theta \lambda g(a) + (1 - \theta) \lambda g(b)\). This is the convexity inequality for \(\lambda g\). The sign condition \(\lambda \ge 0\) is used at the multiplication step; for \(\lambda < 0\) the inequality reverses, and \(\lambda g\) is concave, not convex.

P2 · The gradient inequality for a square

Verify Lemma 1.4 directly for \(\varphi(x) = x^2\) on \(\R\), for all \(x, y\).

Solution

Here \(\varphi'(y) = 2y\), and the claim is \(x^2 \ge y^2 + 2y(x - y)\). Expanding the right side gives \(y^2 + 2xy - 2y^2 = 2xy - y^2\). The difference is \(x^2 - 2xy + y^2 = (x - y)^2 \ge 0\). This is the lemma for this function, with equality exactly at \(x = y\).

P3 · A lower bound on the active instance

For the active instance (Example 4.1), prove that \(f(w) \ge -\tfrac12\) for every feasible \(w\), using the KKT point and not the full optimality argument.

Solution

Let \(w\) be feasible and put \(d = w - w^\ast\). For the quadratic \(q(w) = \tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w - \mu^{\top}w\), expanding \(q(w^\ast + d)\) gives \(q(w^\ast) + (\Sigma w^\ast - \mu)^{\top} d + \tfrac12 d^{\top}\Sigma d\). The last term is nonnegative by Lemma 1.5. Feasibility gives \(w_1 = 1 - w_2\), so \(d = (-w_2, w_2)\), and \((\Sigma w^\ast - \mu)^{\top} d = (\tfrac12)(-w_2) + (\tfrac45)(w_2) = \tfrac{3}{10} w_2\). Hence \(f(w) \ge -\tfrac12 + \tfrac{3}{10} w_2\), and \(w_2 \ge 0\) gives \(f(w) \ge -\tfrac12\). The coefficient \(\tfrac{3}{10}\) is the multiplier \(\lambda_2\) of Example 4.1.

Empirical

E1 · The size of the stationarity residual (formula input)

Lab 1 prints the stationarity residual of \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) at the active multipliers as \((-\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). Compute its Euclidean norm to four decimals.

Solution

The norm is \(\sqrt{(-\tfrac12)^2 + (\tfrac12)^2} = \sqrt{\tfrac12} = \tfrac{\sqrt 2}{2}\), which is 0.7071 to four decimals. The lab prints the residual as exact fractions, so the norm uses no rounded input.

E2 · What the grid can and cannot show (formula input)

Lab 1 reports the minimum over the grid \(w_1 = k/100\), \(k = 0, \dots, 100\). State the number of grid points, and say what the grid minimum does not establish.

Solution

The values \(k = 0, \dots, 100\) give \(101\) grid points, matching the lab's count. The grid minimum is the smallest value among these points, so it is not smaller than the true minimum over the whole segment. It does not establish that the true minimum lies on the grid, and only Theorem 2.1 establishes optimality.

E3 · The objective at the inactive candidate (formula input)

Lab 1 prints an objective of \(-\tfrac14\) at the inactive candidate. Recompute it by hand from \(\tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w - \mu^{\top}w\).

Solution

With \(w = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(\Sigma w = (\tfrac32, \tfrac32)\), so \(w^{\top}\Sigma w = \tfrac32\) and \(\tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w = \tfrac34\). With \(\mu = (1, 1)\), \(\mu^{\top}w = 1\). The objective is \(\tfrac34 - 1 = -\tfrac14\), which matches the printed value.

Research

R1 · A KKT point that is a maximum

Show that \(x^\ast = 0\) satisfies (K1) to (K4) for the problem of maximizing \(x^2\) on \(\R\), written as minimizing \(-x^2\). Say whether it is a minimizer, and identify the hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 that fails.

Solution

Here \(f(x) = -x^2\) with no constraints. Its derivative at \(0\) is \(0\), so (K1) holds with no multipliers, and (K2) to (K4) hold trivially. But \(f(x) = -x^2 < 0\) for \(x \ne 0\), and \(f(0) = 0\), so \(0\) is the global maximum of \(f\), and \(f\) has no minimizer, because \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(|x| \to \infty\). The hypothesis that fails is the convexity of \(f\): \(-x^2\) is concave, not convex, and the proof of Theorem 2.1 uses convexity at the step \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(x^\ast)\).

R2 · Which hypothesis of Proposition 3.2 fails

In Example 3.1, verify that the objective and constraint are convex and that the conclusion of Proposition 3.2 fails. Identify the hypothesis that fails.

Solution

The objective \(x\) is affine, hence convex, and \(g_1(x) = x^2\) is convex by Lemma 1.5. The point \(x^\ast = 0\) is optimal, as shown in Example 3.1. The conclusion of Proposition 3.2 asserts that a KKT point exists, and none does. The hypothesis that fails is Slater's condition, since no \(\bar x\) satisfies \(\bar x^2 < 0\). So in this example the conclusion fails exactly where the hypothesis fails.

R3 · Why sufficiency needs no Slater point

Explain why Theorem 2.1 holds with no Slater point, while Proposition 3.2 needs one.

Solution

Theorem 2.1 starts from a point and multipliers that already satisfy (K1) to (K4), and it checks that the point is optimal. The proof uses the convexity of the functions and the four conditions, and it never has to produce a multiplier. Proposition 3.2 goes the other way: it starts from an optimal point and must produce multipliers. Producing them is where a constraint qualification is needed, and Example 3.1 shows that the qualification cannot be dropped in that direction.

  1. Which condition is dual feasibility?

    Options

    (K3) requires the multipliers of the inequality constraints to be non-negative. The other two options are not conditions of the KKT system.

  2. Which statement is complementary slackness?

    Options

    (K4) is the product condition. It does not say that constraints bind; Remark 1.3 and Misconception 2 explain why.

  3. In Example 4.1, what is the multiplier \(\lambda_2\) of the binding constraint \(w_2 \ge 0\) at the KKT point?

    Stationarity in the second component gives \(\tfrac45 + \nu - \lambda_2 = 0\), with \(\nu = -\tfrac12\). So \(\lambda_2 = \tfrac{3}{10}\).

  4. In Example 4.1, what is the equality multiplier \(\nu\) of the budget constraint at the KKT point?

    The first stationarity component is \(\tfrac12 + \nu - \lambda_1 = 0\), and \(\lambda_1 = 0\) because \(w_1 = 1 > 0\). So \(\nu = -\tfrac12\).

  5. Which hypotheses make a KKT point globally optimal?

    Options

    Theorem 2.1 needs the convexity of \(f\) and the \(g_i\) and the affineness of the \(h_j\). Slater's condition is the hypothesis of the necessity direction, Proposition 3.2.

  6. In Example 2.3, the point \(x^\ast = 0\) satisfies the stationarity condition for \(f(x) = x^3\). Why is it not a minimizer?

    Options

    There are no constraints, so feasibility and multipliers are not the issue. The value \(f(-1) = -1\) is below \(f(0) = 0\), and the convexity inequality fails.

  7. In Example 3.1, the problem minimizes \(x\) subject to \(x^2 \le 0\). Which hypothesis of Proposition 3.2 fails?

    Options

    The objective and constraint are convex and differentiable. No strictly feasible point exists, so Slater's condition fails, and the optimum has no KKT multipliers.

  8. In Example 4.2, what is the optimal value of the inactive instance?

    At \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(\tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w = \tfrac34\) and \(\mu^{\top}w = 1\), so the value is \(-\tfrac14\).

  9. In Example 4.2, what are the multipliers of the two non-negativity constraints?

    Options

    Both weights are \(\tfrac12 > 0\), so \(g_i(w^\ast) = -\tfrac12 \ne 0\), and (K4) forces both multipliers to be zero.
