Lagrangian Duality

  • Uses: KKT conditions and the Lagrangian (Lesson 3), convex function (Lesson 1), Lagrange multiplier (Lesson 2)
  • Introduces: Lagrangian duality
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Each multiplier pair with nonnegative inequality multipliers and a finite dual value gives a lower bound on the optimal value of a constrained minimization, and the dual problem searches for the best such bound. When the best bound equals the primal optimal value, the gap between them is zero. This lesson proves the bound (weak duality), proves the zero gap at a KKT point of a problem with convex, differentiable objective and inequality constraints and affine equality constraints, and checks the dual of a two-asset problem in exact arithmetic in Lab 1. A zero gap under the further condition of Section 3 is not proved here.

1The dual function and weak duality

Use the problem (P), its feasible set \(F\), its optimal value \(p^\ast\) and its Lagrangian \(L\), as defined in Lesson 3, Definition 1.1 and Definition 1.2. Assume throughout that \(F\) is non-empty.

Definition 1.1 (the dual function and the dual problem)

For \(\lambda \in \R^m\) and \(\nu \in \R^p\), the dual function is \(g(\lambda, \nu) = \inf_{x \in \R^n} L(x, \lambda, \nu)\), a number or \(-\infty\). A pair \((\lambda, \nu)\) is dual feasible when \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) for every \(i\) and \(g(\lambda, \nu)\) is finite. The Lagrangian duality of (P) is the problem of maximizing \(g(\lambda, \nu)\) over dual feasible pairs. Its optimal value is \(d^\ast\). The problem (P) is the primal problem, and \(p^\ast\) is its optimal value.

Proposition 1.2 (the dual function is concave)

For fixed \(x\), the map \((\lambda, \nu) \mapsto L(x, \lambda, \nu)\) is affine. Hence \(g\) is concave on the set of \((\lambda, \nu)\) where it is finite.

Proof

Affineness holds because \(L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x)\) is a constant plus a linear function of \((\lambda, \nu)\) for fixed \(x\). Let \(a = (\lambda_a, \nu_a)\) and \(b = (\lambda_b, \nu_b)\) be points where \(g\) is finite, and let \(\theta \in [0, 1]\). For every \(x\), affineness gives \(L(x, \theta a + (1 - \theta) b) = \theta L(x, a) + (1 - \theta) L(x, b)\). The infimum of a sum is at least the sum of the infima, so \[\begin{aligned} g(\theta a + (1 - \theta) b) &= \inf_x \big[\theta L(x, a) + (1 - \theta) L(x, b)\big] \\ &\ge \theta g(a) + (1 - \theta) g(b). \end{aligned}\] This is the concavity inequality. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.3 (weak duality)

For every dual feasible \((\lambda, \nu)\), \(g(\lambda, \nu) \le p^\ast\). Hence \(d^\ast \le p^\ast\). This needs no convexity assumption.

Proof

Let \(x \in F\). Since \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) and \(g_i(x) \le 0\), each term \(\lambda_i g_i(x)\) is at most zero. Since \(h_j(x) = 0\), the equality terms vanish. Hence \[\begin{aligned} g(\lambda, \nu) &\le L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x) \\ &\le f(x). \end{aligned}\] The first inequality is the definition of the infimum. Since this holds for every \(x \in F\), \(g(\lambda, \nu) \le p^\ast\). Taking the supremum over dual feasible \((\lambda, \nu)\) gives \(d^\ast \le p^\ast\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.4 (a grid bounds the duality gap)

Let \(\tilde F \subseteq F\) and let \(\tilde D\) be a set of dual feasible pairs, both finite and non-empty. Then \[\begin{aligned} \min_{x \in \tilde F} f(x) - \max_{(\lambda, \nu) \in \tilde D} g(\lambda, \nu) &\ge p^\ast - d^\ast \\ &\ge 0. \end{aligned}\] In particular, if the left side is zero, then \(p^\ast = d^\ast\).

Proof

The minimum over \(\tilde F\) is at least \(p^\ast\), because \(\tilde F \subseteq F\). The maximum over \(\tilde D\) is at most \(d^\ast\), because each point of \(\tilde D\) is dual feasible. Subtracting, the left side is at least \(p^\ast - d^\ast\). The last inequality is Proposition 1.3. If the left side is zero, then \(0 \ge p^\ast - d^\ast \ge 0\), so \(p^\ast = d^\ast\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.5 (the grid is an inner set on both sides)

A grid of primal points gives an upper bound on \(p^\ast\), and a grid of dual points gives a lower bound on \(d^\ast\). The grid gap is the smallest value of \(f\) on the primal grid minus the largest value of \(g\) on the dual grid. It is therefore an upper bound on the true gap. A grid can miss the dual optimum, and then its gap can be positive even when the true gap is zero. Problem R3 gives a case. The printed grid gap in Lab 1 is zero, so Proposition 1.4 settles the true gap there.

2A zero gap from a KKT point

Proposition 2.1 (a KKT point gives zero gap)

Assume that \(f\) and each \(g_i\) are convex and differentiable on \(\R^n\), and that each \(h_j\) is affine. Let \((\hat{x}, (\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast))\) be a KKT point of (P). Then \(g(\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast) = f(\hat{x})\), and \(p^\ast = d^\ast = f(\hat{x})\). The dual optimum is attained at \((\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast)\), and the primal optimum at \(\hat{x}\).

Proof

Let \(\varphi(x) = L(x, \lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast)\). It is convex and differentiable: \(f\) is convex by hypothesis, each \(\lambda_i^\ast g_i\) is convex because \(\lambda_i^\ast \ge 0\) by (K3) and \(g_i\) is convex, and each \(\nu_j^\ast h_j\) is affine. This is the argument of the proof of Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 3. By (K1), \(\nabla \varphi(\hat{x}) = 0\). Lemma 1.4 of Lesson 3 then gives that \(\hat{x}\) minimizes \(\varphi\) over \(\R^n\). So \[\begin{aligned} g(\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast) &= \inf_x \varphi(x) = \varphi(\hat{x}) \\ &= f(\hat{x}) + \sum_i \lambda_i^\ast g_i(\hat{x}) + \sum_j \nu_j^\ast h_j(\hat{x}) = f(\hat{x}). \end{aligned}\] where the last equality uses (K4) and (K2). Next, \((\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast)\) is dual feasible, so \(d^\ast \ge g(\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast) = f(\hat{x})\). By Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 3, \(\hat{x}\) is optimal, so \(p^\ast = f(\hat{x})\). Thus \(d^\ast \ge p^\ast\). Weak duality, Proposition 1.3, gives \(d^\ast \le p^\ast\). Hence \(d^\ast = p^\ast = f(\hat{x})\), and both optima are attained. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (the dual of the active instance)

Take the active instance of Lesson 3, Example 4.1: \(\mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac15)\), \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), and the constraints \(g_i(w) = -w_i\), \(h(w) = \mathbf{1}^{\top} w - 1\). Expanding the Lagrangian gives \[\begin{aligned} L(w, \lambda, \nu) &= \tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w - r^{\top} w - \nu, \\ r &= \mu + \lambda - \nu \mathbf{1}. \end{aligned}\] The matrix \(\Sigma\) is positive definite, since \((a, b)\Sigma(a, b)^{\top} = a^2 + b^2 + (a + b)^2\) is zero only at \(a = b = 0\). So the infimum over \(w\) is attained where \(\Sigma w = r\), that is at \(w = \Sigma^{-1} r\). Substituting gives \(g(\lambda, \nu) = -\tfrac12 r^{\top}\Sigma^{-1} r - \nu\). With \(\Sigma^{-1} = \tfrac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), this is a closed form. At the KKT point \(\lambda^\ast = (0, \tfrac{3}{10})\), \(\nu^\ast = -\tfrac12\), we get \(r = (2, 1)\), \(\Sigma^{-1} r = (1, 0)\), and \(r^{\top}\Sigma^{-1} r = 2\). So \(g(\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast) = -1 + \tfrac12 = -\tfrac12\), which equals \(f(1, 0) = -\tfrac12\). Proposition 2.1 gives the zero gap for this instance with no tolerance.

Lab 1 · The dual function and the duality gap of the active instance, in exact arithmetic

Predicted before running. The dual grid has \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\) in steps of \(1/10\) on \([0, 1]\) and \(\nu\) in steps of \(1/10\) on \([-1, 0]\), which gives \(11^3 = 1331\) points. The primal grid \(w = (k/20, 1 - k/20)\) has 21 points. Weak duality should hold for all \(1331 \cdot 21 = 27951\) pairs. The largest dual value on the grid should be \(-\tfrac12\), at \(\lambda = (0, \tfrac{3}{10})\) and \(\nu = -\tfrac12\), and only that grid point should attain it. The smallest primal value on the grid should be \(-\tfrac12\), at \(w = (1, 0)\). So the grid gap is zero. The point \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) should satisfy both inequality constraints strictly and the budget exactly. The dual value at \(\lambda = 0\), \(\nu = 0\) should be \(-\tfrac{199}{300}\), below \(-\tfrac12\). Finally, the dual value at the KKT point should equal \(f(1, 0) = -\tfrac12\).

"""M7 lab L04: the dual function, weak duality and the duality gap for the two-asset problem of Lesson 3's lab.

The primal problem is minimize f(w) = (1/2) w'Sigma w - mu'w over w in R^2, subject to 1'w = 1 and
w >= 0, with the active instance mu = (3/2, 1/5) and Sigma = [[2, 1], [1, 2]] from Lesson 3's lab. These are
illustrative inputs, SIMULATED and not market data, in one period with no annualization.

For lambda >= 0 and any nu, the Lagrangian is L(w, lambda, nu) = (1/2) w'Sigma w - r'w - nu with
r = mu + lambda - nu 1. Minimizing over w gives the dual function
g(lambda, nu) = -(1/2) r'Sigma^{-1} r - nu, which this lab evaluates in closed form.

Every value is an exact fractions.Fraction. The lab checks weak duality, g <= f, on a finite grid of
dual points and feasible primal points, and it reports the grid's duality gap. Because the arithmetic is
exact, the grid results are exact statements about the grid. The grid gap bounds the true gap from above
(Proposition 1.4 on the page), and weak duality bounds it from below by zero, so the printed zero grid gap
settles the true gap for this instance. The zero gap at the KKT point is Proposition 2.1 on the page.

Standard library only. No randomness and no network.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

Vector = tuple[Fraction, Fraction]

SIGMA: tuple[tuple[Fraction, Fraction], tuple[Fraction, Fraction]] = (
    (Fraction(2), Fraction(1)),
    (Fraction(1), Fraction(2)),
)
MU: Vector = (Fraction(3, 2), Fraction(1, 5))
DET = SIGMA[0][0] * SIGMA[1][1] - SIGMA[0][1] * SIGMA[1][0]
SIGMA_INV: tuple[tuple[Fraction, Fraction], tuple[Fraction, Fraction]] = (
    (SIGMA[1][1] / DET, -SIGMA[0][1] / DET),
    (-SIGMA[1][0] / DET, SIGMA[0][0] / DET),
)
DUAL_STEPS = 10
PRIMAL_STEPS = 20


def quad(m: tuple[tuple[Fraction, Fraction], tuple[Fraction, Fraction]], v: Vector) -> Fraction:
    """The quadratic form v'm v for a 2 by 2 matrix m."""
    return v[0] * (m[0][0] * v[0] + m[0][1] * v[1]) + v[1] * (m[1][0] * v[0] + m[1][1] * v[1])


def primal(w: Vector) -> Fraction:
    """The primal objective f(w) = (1/2) w'Sigma w - mu'w."""
    return Fraction(1, 2) * quad(SIGMA, w) - (MU[0] * w[0] + MU[1] * w[1])


def dual(lam: Vector, nu: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The dual function g(lambda, nu) = -(1/2) r'Sigma^{-1} r - nu, with r = mu + lambda - nu 1."""
    r = (MU[0] + lam[0] - nu, MU[1] + lam[1] - nu)
    return -Fraction(1, 2) * quad(SIGMA_INV, r) - nu


def dual_grid() -> list[tuple[Vector, Fraction]]:
    """Points (lambda, nu) with lambda_i in {0, 1/10, ..., 1} and nu in {-1, -9/10, ..., 0}."""
    lams = [Fraction(j, DUAL_STEPS) for j in range(DUAL_STEPS + 1)]
    nus = [Fraction(j - DUAL_STEPS, DUAL_STEPS) for j in range(DUAL_STEPS + 1)]
    return [((a, b), nu) for a in lams for b in lams for nu in nus]


def primal_grid() -> list[Vector]:
    """Feasible points (k/20, 1 - k/20) for k = 0..20, which satisfy 1'w = 1 and w >= 0."""
    return [(Fraction(k, PRIMAL_STEPS), Fraction(PRIMAL_STEPS - k, PRIMAL_STEPS)) for k in range(PRIMAL_STEPS + 1)]


def main() -> None:
    print("primal: minimize (1/2) w'Sigma w - mu'w, with Sigma = [[2, 1], [1, 2]], mu = (3/2, 1/5)")
    print("subject to 1'w = 1 and w >= 0 (exact fractions)")
    duals = dual_grid()
    primals = primal_grid()
    print(
        f"dual grid: lambda_1, lambda_2 in steps of 1/{DUAL_STEPS} on [0, 1] and nu in steps of 1/{DUAL_STEPS} "
        f"on [-1, 0]: {len(duals)} dual points"
    )
    print(f"primal grid: w = (k/{PRIMAL_STEPS}, 1 - k/{PRIMAL_STEPS}) for k = 0..{PRIMAL_STEPS}: {len(primals)} points")

    values = [(lam, nu, dual(lam, nu)) for lam, nu in duals]
    prim = [(w, primal(w)) for w in primals]
    holds = sum(g <= f for _, _, g in values for _, f in prim)
    total = len(values) * len(prim)
    print(f"weak duality g(lambda, nu) <= f(w) on the grid: {holds} of {total} pairs")

    best_lam, best_nu, best_g = max(values, key=lambda t: t[2])
    attaining = sum(1 for _, _, g in values if g == best_g)
    print(f"largest dual value on the grid: {best_g} at lambda = ({best_lam[0]}, {best_lam[1]}), nu = {best_nu}")
    print(f"dual grid points attaining that value: {attaining}")

    best_w, best_f = min(prim, key=lambda t: t[1])
    print(f"smallest primal objective on the grid: {best_f} at w = ({best_w[0]}, {best_w[1]})")
    print(f"grid duality gap (smallest primal minus largest dual): {best_f - best_g}")

    test_point = (Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1, 2))
    print(
        "point (1/2, 1/2), with both inequality constraints strict and the budget exact: "
        f"{test_point[0] > 0 and test_point[1] > 0 and test_point[0] + test_point[1] == 1}"
    )
    print(f"dual value at lambda = (0, 0), nu = 0 (no multipliers): {dual((Fraction(0), Fraction(0)), Fraction(0))}")

    kkt_lam: Vector = (Fraction(0), Fraction(3, 10))
    kkt_nu = Fraction(-1, 2)
    kkt_w: Vector = (Fraction(1), Fraction(0))
    print(
        "KKT point: dual value at (lambda*, nu*) equals the primal value at w* = (1, 0): "
        f"{dual(kkt_lam, kkt_nu) == primal(kkt_w) == Fraction(-1, 2)}"
    )

3Slater's condition and strong duality

Definition 3.1 (Slater's condition and a Slater point)

A Slater point of (P) is a point \(\bar x\) that satisfies each inequality strictly, \(g_i(\bar x) < 0\), and each equality exactly, \(h_j(\bar x) = 0\). Slater's condition is the statement that a Slater point exists. It is the hypothesis of Open item 3.2 in Lesson 3, the necessity statement, which this lesson does not use.

Strong duality under Slater's condition is not stated as a numbered result on this page, and no result here depends on it. The zero gap for the active instance comes from Proposition 2.1, which does not use Slater's condition.

Remark 3.3 (the instance satisfies Slater's condition)

For the active instance, \(\bar w = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) is a Slater point. It satisfies \(\bar w_1 > 0\) and \(\bar w_2 > 0\), so \(g_1(\bar w) = -\tfrac12 < 0\) and \(g_2(\bar w) = -\tfrac12 < 0\), and \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bar w = 1\). Proposition 2.1 gives the zero gap with a complete proof, and it does not need this Slater point.

Example 3.4 (zero gap, with no Slater point and no dual maximum)

Take \(n = 1\), \(f(x) = x\), and \(g_1(x) = x^2\), with no equality constraints. As in Lesson 3, Example 3.1, \(F = \{0\}\), so \(p^\ast = 0\). Slater's condition fails. The Lagrangian is \(L(x, \lambda) = x + \lambda x^2\). For \(\lambda = 0\), \(L\) is linear in \(x\) and unbounded below, so \(g(0) = -\infty\). For \(\lambda > 0\), \(L\) is minimized at \(x = -1/(2\lambda)\), where it equals \(-\tfrac{1}{2\lambda} + \tfrac{1}{4\lambda} = -\tfrac{1}{4\lambda}\). So \(g(\lambda) = -\tfrac{1}{4\lambda}\), which is negative for every finite \(\lambda > 0\) and tends to \(0\) as \(\lambda \to \infty\). Hence \(d^\ast = \sup_{\lambda \ge 0} g(\lambda) = 0 = p^\ast\). The gap is zero. The supremum is not attained, so the dual maximum does not exist. The example shows that a zero gap can hold when Slater's condition fails, and that the dual maximum need not be attained in that case.

4Where the canon breaks

The lesson has four limits, and each is shown on the page.

  • Weak duality is a bound, not an equality. Proposition 1.3 holds with no convexity. Equality needs a separate argument, such as Proposition 2.1.
  • The grid can mislead in one direction. A grid of dual points can miss the dual optimum, and then its gap can be positive although the true gap is zero. Problem R3 gives the numbers. A zero grid gap, in contrast, settles the gap by Proposition 1.4.
  • Strong duality under Slater's condition is not proved here. The page proves zero gap at a KKT point (Proposition 2.1) and in Example 3.4, and it makes no general claim about when strong duality holds.
  • The closed form for the dual is special to this instance. The formula \(g(\lambda, \nu) = -\tfrac12 r^{\top}\Sigma^{-1} r - \nu\) holds because the Lagrangian is a quadratic in \(w\) with positive definite \(\Sigma\), so its infimum is at \(\Sigma^{-1} r\). The page makes no claim about the dual of a general problem.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Weak duality shows that the primal and dual optimal values are equal.”

    Weak duality gives only \(d^\ast \le p^\ast\). The dual value at \(\lambda = 0\), \(\nu = 0\) in Lab 1 is \(-\tfrac{199}{300}\), which is below \(p^\ast = -\tfrac12\) by \(\tfrac{49}{300}\). A dual value strictly below the primal optimum is allowed by Proposition 1.3, and it is not a statement that the bound is tight. Equality needs an argument such as Proposition 2.1.

  2. “The dual problem minimizes the dual function.”

    Each dual feasible value \(g(\lambda, \nu)\) is a lower bound on \(p^\ast\), by Proposition 1.3. The best lower bound is the largest one, so the dual problem maximizes \(g\). Lab 1 shows the largest dual value on its grid, \(-\tfrac12\), and the smaller value \(-\tfrac{199}{300}\) at the origin.

  3. “A grid gap is only a tolerance check, so a zero grid gap says nothing about the true gap.”

    Proposition 1.4 says the grid gap is at least \(p^\ast - d^\ast\), and weak duality says \(p^\ast - d^\ast\) is at least zero. A grid gap of zero therefore forces the true gap to be zero. The implication uses only Propositions 1.3 and 1.4. Exact arithmetic makes the printed zero exact, so no tolerance is needed. Lab 1 prints a grid gap of 0.

Mechanics

M1 · The dual of a one-variable problem

Find the dual function, the dual optimum and the primal optimum for minimizing \(x^2\) subject to \(1 - x \le 0\).

Solution

The Lagrangian is \(L(x, \lambda) = x^2 + \lambda(1 - x)\). Minimizing over \(x\), \(2x - \lambda = 0\) gives \(x = \lambda/2\). Substituting, \(g(\lambda) = \tfrac{\lambda^2}{4} + \lambda - \tfrac{\lambda^2}{2} = \lambda - \tfrac{\lambda^2}{4}\). Maximizing over \(\lambda \ge 0\), \(1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{2} = 0\) gives \(\lambda = 2\), with \(g(2) = 1\). The primal optimum is \(x = 1\), with value \(1\), so the gap is zero. The multiplier \(\lambda = 2\) and \(x = 1\) form a KKT point, as Proposition 2.1 predicts.

M2 · The dual value at an intermediate multiplier

For the problem of M1, compute \(g(1)\) and the gap that \(g(1)\) leaves against \(p^\ast = 1\).

Solution

From M1, \(g(1) = 1 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac34\). The gap that this multiplier leaves is \(1 - \tfrac34 = \tfrac14\), which is positive because \(\lambda = 1\) is not the dual optimum. The value \(\tfrac34\) is a lower bound on \(p^\ast\), as Proposition 1.3 requires.

M3 · The dual value at the origin for the two-asset instance

Show by hand that \(g(0, 0) = -\tfrac{199}{300}\) for the active instance.

Solution

At \(\lambda = 0\) and \(\nu = 0\), \(r = \mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac15)\). We have \(\Sigma^{-1}\mu = \tfrac13 (2 \cdot \tfrac32 - \tfrac15, -\tfrac32 + 2 \cdot \tfrac15) = \tfrac13(\tfrac{14}{5}, -\tfrac{11}{10}) = (\tfrac{14}{15}, -\tfrac{11}{30})\). Then \(\mu^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mu = \tfrac32 \cdot \tfrac{14}{15} - \tfrac15 \cdot \tfrac{11}{30} = \tfrac{210}{150} - \tfrac{11}{150} = \tfrac{199}{150}\). So \(g(0, 0) = -\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{199}{150} = -\tfrac{199}{300}\), which is the value Lab 1 prints.

P1 · Weak duality for the one-variable problem

For the problem of M1, prove \(g(\lambda) \le 1\) for every \(\lambda \ge 0\) directly from the formula for \(g\), without using the zero gap.

Solution

We have \(1 - g(\lambda) = 1 - \lambda + \tfrac{\lambda^2}{4} = \left(1 - \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\right)^2 \ge 0\), by expanding the square. So \(g(\lambda) \le 1 = p^\ast\), which is the weak duality bound, with equality exactly at \(\lambda = 2\).

P2 · Proof of the grid bound

Prove Proposition 1.4, and say which step uses finiteness of \(\tilde F\) and \(\tilde D\).

Solution

Since \(\tilde F\) is finite and non-empty, its minimum of \(f\) exists and is attained at some point \(\tilde x\) of \(\tilde F\). Since \(\tilde F \subseteq F\), \(f(\tilde x) \ge p^\ast\). Since \(\tilde D\) is finite and non-empty, its maximum of \(g\) is attained at some dual feasible pair, and that value is at most \(d^\ast\). Subtracting gives the inequality. Finiteness is used to say that the minimum and maximum exist, so the left side is a number. Without finiteness the same bounds hold for the infimum and supremum.

P3 · The dual value at the KKT point

Let \((\hat{x}, (\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast))\) be a KKT point with convex data. Prove \(g(\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast) = f(\hat{x})\), using Lemma 1.4 of Lesson 3.

Solution

The function \(\varphi(x) = L(x, \lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast)\) is convex and differentiable, because \(\lambda^\ast \ge 0\) and the \(g_i\) are convex, while the \(h_j\) are affine. Its gradient at \(\hat{x}\) is zero by (K1). By Lemma 1.4 of Lesson 3, \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(\hat{x})\) for every \(x\), so \(g(\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast) = \varphi(\hat{x})\). By (K4) and (K2), \(\varphi(\hat{x}) = f(\hat{x})\). Hence \(g(\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast) = f(\hat{x})\).

Empirical

E1 · Counting the grid pairs (formula input)

Lab 1 prints 1331 dual points, 21 primal points and 27951 pairs. Confirm each count from the grid definitions.

Solution

Each of \(\lambda_1\), \(\lambda_2\) and \(\nu\) takes 11 values, \(0, \tfrac{1}{10}, \dots, 1\) for the \(\lambda_i\) and \(-1, -\tfrac{9}{10}, \dots, 0\) for \(\nu\). So there are \(11^3 = 1331\) dual points. The primal grid has \(k = 0, \dots, 20\), which is 21 points. The pairs number \(1331 \cdot 21 = 27951\), and the lab reports all 27951 as satisfying \(g \le f\).

E2 · The gap to the true optimum (formula input)

Lab 1 prints \(-\tfrac{199}{300}\) at the origin. Give its value to four decimals, and the difference from \(p^\ast = -\tfrac12\) as an exact fraction.

Solution

The value is \(-\tfrac{199}{300} = -0.66333\ldots\), which is \(-0.6633\) to four decimals. The difference is \(-\tfrac{199}{300} + \tfrac{150}{300} = -\tfrac{49}{300}\), so the origin's dual value lies \(\tfrac{49}{300}\) below \(p^\ast\). The fraction is exact, and the four-decimal value is only for display.

E3 · Why the printed zero grid gap is a proof (formula input)

Lab 1 prints a grid duality gap of 0. Explain in one paragraph why this settles the true gap, citing the proposition that gives the argument.

Solution

The printed gap is the smallest primal objective on the grid minus the largest dual value on the grid. Proposition 1.4 says this quantity is at least \(p^\ast - d^\ast\). Weak duality, Proposition 1.3, says \(p^\ast - d^\ast \ge 0\). The printed value is exactly zero, so \(0 \ge p^\ast - d^\ast \ge 0\), and the true gap is zero. The lab's arithmetic is exact, so no rounding enters this argument.

R1 · Slater's condition for the instance

Verify Slater's condition for the active instance, and say which result on this page gives the zero gap without using Slater's condition.

Solution

The point \(\bar w = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) satisfies \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}\bar w = 1\), and \(g_1(\bar w) = -\tfrac12 < 0\), \(g_2(\bar w) = -\tfrac12 < 0\). So it is a Slater point. Proposition 2.1 gives the zero gap from the KKT point \((\lambda^\ast, \nu^\ast)\) of Example 2.2, and its hypotheses do not include Slater's condition.

R2 · Attainment in Example 3.4

In Example 3.4, the dual supremum is zero and not attained. Explain why Proposition 2.1 cannot be applied to that example to produce a dual optimum.

Solution

Proposition 2.1 needs a KKT point. In Example 3.4, \(x = 0\) is the only feasible point. Its stationarity condition reads \(1 + 2\lambda \cdot 0 = 1 \neq 0\) for every \(\lambda\), so no KKT point exists and Proposition 2.1 has no point to use. The page does not claim a general attainment result.

R3 · A grid that misses the dual optimum

Restrict the dual grid of Lab 1 to \(\lambda = (0, 0)\) and \(\nu\) in steps of \(\tfrac{1}{10}\) on \([-1, 0]\), and keep the primal grid. Show that the grid gap is \(\tfrac{7}{300}\), although the true gap is zero.

Solution

With \(\lambda = 0\), \(r = (\tfrac32 - \nu, \tfrac15 - \nu)\). The value is \(g(0, \nu) = -\tfrac13(a^2 - ab + b^2) - \nu\), where \(a = \tfrac32 - \nu\) and \(b = \tfrac15 - \nu\). Evaluating at the grid points \(\nu = -\tfrac35\) and \(\nu = -\tfrac{7}{10}\), each gives \(-\tfrac{157}{300}\). The continuous maximum over \(\nu\) is at \(\nu = -\tfrac{13}{20}\), with value \(-\tfrac{209}{400}\), but \(-\tfrac{13}{20}\) is not on the grid. Since \(g(0, \nu)\) is concave in \(\nu\) by Proposition 1.2, the two neighbours of \(-\tfrac{13}{20}\) on the grid give the largest grid value, which is \(-\tfrac{157}{300}\), and the smallest primal value is \(-\tfrac12\), at \(w = (1, 0)\), which is on the primal grid. The grid gap is \(-\tfrac12 + \tfrac{157}{300} = \tfrac{7}{300}\), which is positive, while the true gap is zero by Proposition 2.1. This is the case Remark 1.5 describes.
