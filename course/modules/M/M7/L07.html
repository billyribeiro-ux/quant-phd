The principle of optimality and finite-horizon dynamic programming

A plan of several choices can be found working backward one period at a time. This lesson defines a finite-horizon decision problem, its plans and its value function, and proves the principle of optimality: a plan that is optimal from a state has optimal later parts of the plan from the states it reaches. The proof gives the backward recursion. Lesson L08 calls that recursion the Bellman equation (its Definition 1.2). Lab 1 computes the recursion for a small problem and checks it against an enumeration of its plans. The lab shows where the principle stops: a greedy plan, which takes the largest period reward at each step, can have optimal later parts and still not be optimal.

1Finite-horizon problems, plans and value

Time runs over \(t = 0, 1, \ldots, H\), where the horizon \(H\) is a whole number with \(H \ge 1\). Rewards are real numbers, written as elements of \(\R\). The sets and maps below are fixed before any decision is made. Lab 1 uses an integer problem, and every amount in it is in whole cents or in utils, a label for an integer reward.

Definition 1.1 (finite-horizon decision problem)

A finite-horizon decision problem has the following data. A finite set \(S\) of states. For each \(s \in S\), a nonempty finite set \(A(s)\) of feasible actions. A transition map \(f\) with \(f(s,a) \in S\) for every \(s \in S\) and \(a \in A(s)\), so the next state is a known function of the current state and action; the transitions are deterministic. A period reward \(r(s,a) \in \R\) for each \(s\) and \(a \in A(s)\). A terminal reward \(B(s) \in \R\) for each \(s \in S\), paid at time \(H\).

Definition 1.2 (plans, total reward and value)

Let \(0 \le t \le H\) and \(s \in S\). A plan from \((t,s)\) is a sequence \(p = (a_t, \ldots, a_{H-1})\) for which the states \(s_t = s\) and \(s_{k+1} = f(s_k, a_k)\) satisfy \(a_k \in A(s_k)\) for each \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\). Its total reward is \[ J_t(p) = \sum_{k=t}^{H-1} r(s_k, a_k) + B(s_H). \] The value function is \(V_t(s) = \max_p J_t(p)\), the maximum over the plans from \((t,s)\). A plan from \((t,s)\) is optimal if \(J_t(p) = V_t(s)\). Because each \(A(s)\) is finite and nonempty and \(H\) is finite, the set of plans from \((t,s)\) is finite and nonempty, so the maximum exists.

Remark 1.3 (where finiteness is used)

Finiteness is used for the existence of the maxima in Definition 1.2 and Theorem 2.1. Over an infinite action set the largest value may not be attained. Take \(H = 1\), one state \(s\), \(A(s) = \{a : 0 < a < 1\}\), \(r(s,a) = a\), \(f(s,a) = s\) and \(B(s) = 0\). The supremum of the totals from \((0,s)\) is 1, and no plan attains it, because each \(a\) with \(0 < a < 1\) gives the total \(a < 1\).

Definition 1.4 (continuation and the principle of optimality)

Let \(p = (a_t, \ldots, a_{H-1})\) be a plan from \((t,s)\), with states \(s_k\) along it. For \(t \le k \le H-1\), the continuation of \(p\) from time \(k\) is \((a_k, \ldots, a_{H-1})\), a plan from \((k, s_k)\). The principle of optimality is the statement that, if \(p\) is optimal from \((t,s)\), then for each \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\) the continuation of \(p\) from time \(k\) is optimal from \((k, s_k)\). Theorem 2.2 proves it for the problem of Definition 1.1. Remark 2.5 shows that the converse does not hold.

2The backward recursion and the principle of optimality

The next theorem is the backward recursion. Its proof runs downward in time, which is the form of mathematical induction from M1 that this lesson uses. Lesson L08 calls the equation in Theorem 2.1 the Bellman equation (Definition 1.2 of L08).

Theorem 2.1 (backward recursion)

Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, \(V_H(s) = B(s)\) for every \(s \in S\). For \(0 \le t \le H-1\) and every \(s \in S\), \[ V_t(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \bigl\{ r(s,a) + V_{t+1}\bigl(f(s,a)\bigr) \bigr\}. \]

Proof

The proof is by downward induction on \(t\). At \(t = H\) the only plan from \((H,s)\) is the empty plan, whose total reward is \(J_H = B(s)\), so \(V_H(s) = B(s)\).

Suppose the formula holds at time \(t+1\), and let \(s \in S\). A plan from \((t,s)\) is a pair \((a, q)\), where \(a \in A(s)\) is the first action and \(q\) is a plan from \((t+1, f(s,a))\). Conversely, every such pair is a plan from \((t,s)\), because the state at time \(t+1\) is \(f(s,a)\). For such a pair the total reward splits as \[ J_t(a,q) = r(s,a) + J_{t+1}(q), \] since the first period contributes \(r(s,a)\) and the remaining periods and the terminal reward contribute \(J_{t+1}(q)\). Taking the maximum over pairs means taking the maximum over \(a \in A(s)\) of the maximum over \(q\): \[ V_t(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \Bigl\{ r(s,a) + \max_{q} J_{t+1}(q) \Bigr\} = \max_{a \in A(s)} \bigl\{ r(s,a) + V_{t+1}\bigl(f(s,a)\bigr) \bigr\}. \] Both maxima range over finite nonempty sets, so they exist.

Theorem 2.2 (principle of optimality)

Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, let \(0 \le t \le H-1\), let \(s \in S\), and let \(p^{*} = (a^{*}_t, \ldots, a^{*}_{H-1})\) be an optimal plan from \((t,s)\), with states \(s^{*}_k\) along it. Then for every \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\), the continuation of \(p^{*}\) from time \(k\) is optimal from \((k, s^{*}_k)\).

Proof

Fix \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\). Let \(g = (a^{*}_k, \ldots, a^{*}_{H-1})\) be the continuation of \(p^{*}\) from time \(k\), and let \(q\) be any plan from \((k, s^{*}_k)\). Form the plan \(p'\) that follows \(p^{*}\) up to time \(k\) and then follows \(q\). Its states up to time \(k\) are \(s^{*}_t, \ldots, s^{*}_k\), so the actions in the first part are feasible, and \(q\) starts from \(s^{*}_k\). So \(p'\) is a plan from \((t,s)\).

Write \(\Sigma = \sum_{j=t}^{k-1} r(s^{*}_j, a^{*}_j)\). The total reward splits at time \(k\) for both plans: \[ J_t(p') = \Sigma + J_k(q), \qquad J_t(p^{*}) = \Sigma + J_k(g). \] Since \(p^{*}\) is optimal, \(J_t(p') \le J_t(p^{*})\), so \(J_k(q) \le J_k(g)\). This holds for every plan \(q\) from \((k, s^{*}_k)\). Since \(g\) is itself such a plan, \(J_k(g)\) is the maximum over these plans, so \(J_k(g) = V_k(s^{*}_k)\).

Theorem 2.3 (first-step characterization)

Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, let \(0 \le t \le H-1\), \(s \in S\), \(a \in A(s)\), and let \(q\) be a plan from \((t+1, f(s,a))\). The plan \((a, q)\) from \((t,s)\) is optimal if and only if both of the following hold: (i) \(r(s,a) + V_{t+1}(f(s,a)) = V_t(s)\), so \(a\) attains the maximum in Theorem 2.1; and (ii) \(q\) is optimal from \((t+1, f(s,a))\).

Proof

By the split in the proof of Theorem 2.1, \(J_t(a,q) = r(s,a) + J_{t+1}(q)\). Since \(J_{t+1}(q) \le V_{t+1}(f(s,a))\) by the definition of \(V_{t+1}\), and \(r(s,a) + V_{t+1}(f(s,a))\) is at most the maximum in Theorem 2.1, the chain \[ J_t(a,q) \le r(s,a) + V_{t+1}\bigl(f(s,a)\bigr) \le V_t(s) \] holds. The plan is optimal, meaning \(J_t(a,q) = V_t(s)\), exactly when both inequalities are equalities. The first is an equality exactly when (ii) holds, and the second exactly when (i) holds.

Corollary 2.4 (an optimal plan from the recursion)

Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, let \(0 \le t \le H\) and \(s \in S\). Form the plan from \((t,s)\) forward: at each time \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\) and state \(s_k\), choose an action that attains the maximum in Theorem 2.1 at \((k, s_k)\). The resulting plan is optimal from \((t,s)\).

Proof

Induction on the number of remaining periods \(H - t\). If \(H - t = 0\), the empty plan is the only plan and is optimal. Otherwise the plan has the form \((a, q)\), where \(a\) attains the maximum at \((t,s)\), and \(q\) is the plan built by the same rule from \((t+1, f(s,a))\). By the induction hypothesis \(q\) is optimal from \((t+1, f(s,a))\), and condition (i) of Theorem 2.3 holds because \(a\) attains the maximum. By Theorem 2.3 the plan \((a, q)\) is optimal.

Lab 1 · Backward recursion checked against brute force

SIMULATED: a fixed toy problem with integer inputs, not market data. Four periods, five wealth states in cents (0, 100, 200, 300, 400), consumption in cents, and integer rewards in utils. Stated before running: Part A should give \(V_0(400) = 240\) utils, attained by consumption of 100 cents in each of the four periods. Part B should print recursion_matches_brute_force True, which compares the recursion with brute force at each of the 20 pairs of time and wealth (t = 0 to 3, five states each), and should print 70 for the feasible sequences from (0, 400). Part C should show the greedy plan 400, 0, 0, 0 earning 130 utils, not optimal, while each of its continuations from t = 1, 2 and 3 is optimal. The code draws no random numbers, so the output is the same on every run.

"""M7 L07 lab 1: backward recursion for a finite-horizon toy problem, checked against brute-force enumeration.

SIMULATED: a fixed toy problem with integer inputs. It is not market data and it uses no random draws. Wealth and
consumption are whole cents. Period rewards and the bequest are integers in utils, so every value is
exact and no floating-point error enters.

The problem has H = 4 periods, t = 0 to 3. The state at time t is wealth W in {0, 100, 200, 300, 400} cents. The
action is consumption c, a multiple of 100 cents with c <= W. Next wealth is W - c. The period reward is u(c) from the
table below. The reward at time H = 4 is the bequest W // 100 utils.

Part A prints the value table V_t(W) from the backward recursion, for t = 0 to 4.
Part B enumerates every consumption sequence of length H - t from each state, takes the best total, and compares it
with the recursion. It prints the number of feasible sequences checked from (0, 400).
Part C prints the optimal plan from (0, 400), checks that each continuation of it (the printed names say tail)
is optimal from the state it reaches, and compares the greedy plan (the largest period reward at each step)
with the optimum.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from itertools import product

HORIZON = 4
GRID = (0, 100, 200, 300, 400)
REWARD = {0: 0, 100: 60, 200: 100, 300: 120, 400: 130}


def period_reward(c: int) -> int:
    """Utility in utils of consuming c cents in one period."""
    return REWARD[c]


def bequest(w: int) -> int:
    """Reward in utils for the wealth w cents left at time H, counted in whole hundreds."""
    return w // 100


def feasible(w: int) -> tuple[int, ...]:
    """Consumption choices from wealth w cents: every grid amount c with c <= w."""
    return tuple(c for c in GRID if c <= w)


def backward_recursion() -> list[dict[int, int]]:
    """values[t][w] is V_t(w) in utils. V_H is the bequest; each earlier value is the maximum of the recursion."""
    values: list[dict[int, int]] = [{} for _ in range(HORIZON + 1)]
    values[HORIZON] = {w: bequest(w) for w in GRID}
    for t in range(HORIZON - 1, -1, -1):
        for w in GRID:
            values[t][w] = max(period_reward(c) + values[t + 1][w - c] for c in feasible(w))
    return values


def brute_force(t: int, w: int) -> tuple[int, int]:
    """Best total reward from (t, w) over every consumption sequence, and how many feasible sequences were checked."""
    best: int | None = None
    checked = 0
    for plan in product(GRID, repeat=HORIZON - t):
        wealth = w
        total = 0
        feasible_plan = True
        for c in plan:
            if c > wealth:
                feasible_plan = False
                break
            total += period_reward(c)
            wealth -= c
        if not feasible_plan:
            continue
        total += bequest(wealth)
        checked += 1
        if best is None or total > best:
            best = total
    if best is None:
        raise RuntimeError("no feasible plan")
    return best, checked


def optimal_plan(values: list[dict[int, int]], w0: int) -> tuple[list[int], list[int]]:
    """Follow the recursion's maximising consumption from (0, w0). Returns the plan and the wealth path."""
    plan: list[int] = []
    path = [w0]
    w = w0
    for t in range(HORIZON):
        c = max(feasible(w), key=lambda x: period_reward(x) + values[t + 1][w - x])
        plan.append(c)
        w -= c
        path.append(w)
    return plan, path


def greedy_plan(w0: int) -> tuple[list[int], list[int]]:
    """Take the largest period reward available at each step, ignoring the future. Returns the plan and wealth path."""
    plan: list[int] = []
    path = [w0]
    w = w0
    for _ in range(HORIZON):
        c = max(feasible(w), key=period_reward)
        plan.append(c)
        w -= c
        path.append(w)
    return plan, path


def plan_total(consumptions: list[int], w0: int) -> int:
    """Total reward in utils of a consumption sequence started at wealth w0, including the bequest."""
    total = 0
    w = w0
    for c in consumptions:
        total += period_reward(c)
        w -= c
    return total + bequest(w)


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A, B and C. Every value is an integer, so every line of the output is exact."""
    values = backward_recursion()
    print("Part A: value table V_t(W) in utils, from the backward recursion")
    for t in range(HORIZON + 1):
        cells = " ".join(f"W{w}={values[t][w]}" for w in GRID)
        print(f"t {t} {cells}")

    print("Part B: brute force over every consumption sequence")
    all_match = True
    for t in range(HORIZON):
        for w in GRID:
            best, _ = brute_force(t, w)
            all_match = all_match and best == values[t][w]
    _, checked = brute_force(0, 400)
    print(f"plans_checked_from_0_400 {checked}")
    print(f"recursion_matches_brute_force {all_match}")

    print("Part C: optimal plan from (0, 400), its continuations, and the greedy plan")
    plan, path = optimal_plan(values, 400)
    print("optimal_consumption " + " ".join(str(c) for c in plan))
    print("optimal_wealth_path " + " ".join(str(w) for w in path))
    print(f"optimal_total {plan_total(plan, 400)}")
    tails_optimal = all(plan_total(plan[k:], path[k]) == values[k][path[k]] for k in range(HORIZON))
    print(f"optimal_plan_every_tail_optimal {tails_optimal}")

    greedy, greedy_path = greedy_plan(400)
    print("greedy_consumption " + " ".join(str(c) for c in greedy))
    print(f"greedy_total {plan_total(greedy, 400)}")
    for k in range(1, HORIZON):
        tail_optimal = plan_total(greedy[k:], greedy_path[k]) == values[k][greedy_path[k]]
        print(f"greedy_tail_from_t {k} wealth {greedy_path[k]} optimal {tail_optimal}")
    print(f"greedy_plan_optimal {plan_total(greedy, 400) == values[0][400]}")


main()

Remark 2.5 (the converse of the principle fails)

Theorem 2.2 says that the continuations of an optimal plan are optimal. The converse does not hold: a plan whose continuations from each later time \(k > t\) are all optimal need not be optimal. Take \(H = 2\), \(S = \{x, y\}\), \(A(x) = \{a, b\}\), \(A(y) = \{c\}\), \(f(x,a) = f(x,b) = y\), \(f(y,c) = y\), \(r(x,a) = 0\), \(r(x,b) = 1\), \(r(y,c) = 0\), and \(B(x) = B(y) = 0\). Then \(V_1(y) = 0\), and \(V_0(x) = \max\{0 + 0,\, 1 + 0\} = 1\), attained only by \(b\). The plan \(p = (a, c)\) from \((0,x)\) has \(J_0(p) = 0 < 1\), so it is not optimal. Its continuation from time 1 is \((c)\) from \((1,y)\), the only plan from that state, so it is optimal. The principle gives a necessary condition for optimality, not a sufficient one.

3Where the canon breaks

  • Additive rewards. The proof of Theorem 2.2 uses that the total reward splits into a prefix and a continuation, \(J_t(p') = \Sigma + J_k(q)\). Under a different objective the split can fail, and the principle with it. Problem R2 gives an objective for which Theorem 2.2 is false.
  • Finite sets and deterministic transitions. Remark 1.3 shows that a maximum over an infinite action set may fail to exist. This lesson also assumes that the next state is a known function of the current state and action. Problems with random transitions need a weighted average over the next states, which this lesson does not treat.
  • A fixed horizon. Each result in §2 holds for one finite \(H\). This lesson does not treat an infinite horizon.
  • Enumeration does not scale. Problem R3 shows that with \(m\) actions in every state, there are \(m^{H-t}\) plans from \((t,s)\). The recursion computes each \(V_t(s)\) from the \(|A(s)|\) candidate sums at \((t,s)\), so the whole table takes at most \(H\) times \(|S|\) times \(m\) candidate sums. Lab 1 uses the enumeration only as a check.

4Misconceptions

  1. “The best first step is the one with the largest period reward.”

    At wealth 400 cents, the largest period reward is at consumption 400, worth 130 utils. The greedy plan takes it and earns a total of 130 utils. The optimal plan takes 100 cents in each period and earns 240 utils (greedy_total 130 and optimal_total 240 in Part C of Lab 1). The first step must be judged by its effect on the value of the state it leads to, which the recursion of Theorem 2.1 includes.

  2. “If every continuation of a plan from a later time is optimal, the plan is optimal.”

    Remark 2.5 gives a two-period problem in which the plan \((a, c)\) has an optimal continuation and is not optimal. Part C of Lab 1 shows the same effect: the greedy plan 400, 0, 0, 0 has its three continuations from t = 1, 2 and 3 printed as optimal, and greedy_plan_optimal False. The first action belongs to no continuation, so the continuations cannot judge it. Theorem 2.3 adds the missing condition, which is the optimal first action.

  3. “A state has the same value whatever the time left.”

    The recursion in Theorem 2.1 uses \(V_{t+1}\), so the value of a state depends on how many periods remain. Part A of Lab 1 shows the wealth-400 values 240, 220, 200, 130 and 4 utils at t = 0 to 4. A state's value at time \(t\) reflects the periods that are still to come.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems M1 to M3 use the two-period problem below. Its states are \(x\) and \(y\), with \(A(x) = \{u, v\}\), \(A(y) = \{w\}\), \(f(x,u) = y\), \(f(x,v) = x\), \(f(y,w) = y\), \(r(x,u) = 3\), \(r(x,v) = 5\), \(r(y,w) = 2\), \(B(x) = 0\) and \(B(y) = 1\), with \(H = 2\).

Mechanics

M1 · The value function of the two-period problem

Compute \(V_1(x)\), \(V_1(y)\) and \(V_0(x)\) for the problem above.

Solution

At time 1 there is one period left. From \(x\), action \(u\) gives \(3 + B(y) = 4\) and action \(v\) gives \(5 + B(x) = 5\), so \(V_1(x) = 5\). From \(y\), the only action \(w\) gives \(2 + B(y) = 3\), so \(V_1(y) = 3\). At time 0, from \(x\), action \(u\) gives \(3 + V_1(y) = 6\) and action \(v\) gives \(5 + V_1(x) = 10\), so \(V_0(x) = 10\), attained by \(v\).

M2 · The optimal plan from \((0,x)\)

For the problem above, state the optimal plan from \((0,x)\) and its total reward.

Solution

Corollary 2.4 says to take a maximizing action at each step. At time 0 from \(x\), the maximizing action is \(v\), as M1 shows. The state stays \(x\), and at time 1 from \(x\) the maximizing action is again \(v\), since \(V_1(x) = 5\) comes from \(v\). The optimal plan is \((v, v)\), with total reward \(5 + 5 = 10\), which equals \(V_0(x)\).

M3 · Every plan from \((0,x)\)

List every plan from \((0,x)\) for the problem above, with its total reward, and confirm that the maximum is 10.

Solution

A plan from \((0,x)\) is a pair of actions. The plan \((u, w)\) moves to \(y\) and then stays there, with total \(3 + (2 + B(y)) = 3 + 3 = 6\). The plan \((v, u)\) stays at \(x\) and then moves to \(y\), with total \(5 + (3 + B(y)) = 5 + 4 = 9\). The plan \((v, v)\) stays at \(x\) twice, with total \(5 + 5 = 10\). The plan \((u, v)\) is not feasible, because after \(u\) the state is \(y\) and \(v\) is not in \(A(y)\). So there are three plans, with totals 6, 9 and 10, and the maximum is 10.

Proof

P1 · The last step of the recursion

Prove the case \(t = H - 1\) of Theorem 2.1 directly from Definition 1.2, without downward induction.

Solution

A plan from \((H-1,s)\) is a single action \(a \in A(s)\), with states \(s\) and then \(f(s,a)\). Its total reward is \(r(s,a) + B(f(s,a))\), because the sum has one term and the terminal reward is paid at time \(H\) from state \(f(s,a)\). So the plans from \((H-1,s)\) are indexed by \(a \in A(s)\), and the maximum of their totals is \(\max_{a \in A(s)} \{r(s,a) + B(f(s,a))\}\). This is the formula of Theorem 2.1 at \(t = H-1\), because \(V_H = B\).

P2 · The principle at time one

Let \(H = 2\) and let \((a, b)\) be an optimal plan from \((0,s)\), where \(b\) is the action at time 1. Show directly that \(b\) is optimal from \((1, f(s,a))\).

Solution

Write \(s_1 = f(s,a)\). Suppose some \(b' \in A(s_1)\) gives a larger total from \((1, s_1)\) than \(b\) does. Then \((a, b')\) is a plan from \((0,s)\), because \(b' \in A(s_1)\). Its total is \(r(s,a) + \bigl[ r(s_1,b') + B(f(s_1,b')) \bigr]\), and the total of \((a,b)\) is \(r(s,a) + \bigl[ r(s_1,b) + B(f(s_1,b)) \bigr]\). The shared term \(r(s,a)\) cancels, so \((a,b')\) has the larger total. That contradicts the optimality of \((a,b)\). Hence \(b\) is optimal from \((1, s_1)\). This is Theorem 2.2 with \(k = 1\) and \(H = 2\).

P3 · Corollary 2.4 for two periods

For \(H = 2\), use Theorem 2.3 to show that choosing a maximizing first action and then a maximizing second action gives an optimal plan from \((0,s)\).

Solution

Let \(a\) attain the maximum in Theorem 2.1 at \((0,s)\), and let \(b\) attain the maximum at \((1, f(s,a))\). The one-period plan \((b)\) from \((1, f(s,a))\) is optimal, because the maximum at time 1 is \(V_1(s_1) = r(s_1,b) + B(f(s_1,b))\) with \(s_1 = f(s,a)\), which \((b)\) attains. Condition (i) of Theorem 2.3 holds because \(a\) is maximizing, and condition (ii) holds by the optimality of \((b)\). So \((a, b)\) is optimal from \((0,s)\).

Solution

Part C prints greedy_total 130 and optimal_total 240. The ratio is \(130/240 = 13/24 = 0.541666\ldots\), which is 0.5417 to four decimal places. The totals are exact integers, so no approximation enters before the division.

E3 · Count the feasible consumption sequences

Part B of Lab 1 prints plans_checked_from_0_400 70. Explain the number 70 from the grid and the horizon, without rerunning the code.

Solution

Write each consumption as \(100 c'_j\) with \(c'_j\) a whole number, \(j = 1, \ldots, 4\). Feasibility from 400 cents means \(c'_1 + \cdots + c'_4 \le 4\), with each \(c'_j \ge 0\). Adding a slack variable \(c'_5 \ge 0\) turns this into five nonnegative whole numbers that sum to 4. There are \(\binom{8}{4} = 70\) such solutions. This matches the printed count, which is the number of feasible sequences the enumeration checked.

Research

R1 · Where the proof of Theorem 2.1 needs a maximum

Remark 1.3 says that a maximum may fail to exist over an infinite action set. Explain how the existence of a value can fail in Definition 1.2 and what Theorem 2.1 then says.

Solution

The proof of Theorem 2.1 writes \(V_t(s)\) as a maximum over \(a\) and over plans \(q\). With \(A(s) = \{a : 0 < a < 1\}\), \(r(s,a) = a\), \(H = 1\) and \(B = 0\), the set of totals from \((0,s)\) is the open interval from 0 to 1, whose supremum is 1 with no attaining plan. A value defined as this supremum would be 1 here. The page proves nothing about such a definition, because the proofs in §2 use finiteness. No plan attains the supremum, so no optimal plan exists, and Theorem 2.2 has no optimal plan to start from.

R2 · An objective for which the principle fails

Replace the total reward of Definition 1.2 by \(J(p) = \max_k r(s_k, a_k)\), the largest period reward along the plan, with \(B = 0\). Take \(H = 2\), \(S = \{s, x\}\), \(A(s) = \{a_0\}\), \(A(x) = \{b, c\}\), \(f(s,a_0) = x\), \(f(x,b) = f(x,c) = x\), \(r(s,a_0) = 10\), \(r(x,b) = 5\) and \(r(x,c) = 3\). Show that the plan \((a_0, c)\) is optimal from \((0,s)\) but its continuation \((c)\) is not optimal from \((1,x)\).

Solution

From \((1,x)\) the two one-period plans have objectives 5 and 3, so \(V_1(x) = 5\) under this objective, and \((c)\), with objective 3, is not optimal. From \((0,s)\) the only action is \(a_0\), so the plans are \((a_0, b)\) and \((a_0, c)\). Their objectives are \(\max\{10, 5\} = 10\) and \(\max\{10, 3\} = 10\). Both equal 10, which is \(V_0(s)\), so \((a_0, c)\) is optimal. The continuation of an optimal plan is not optimal here. The step that fails is the additive split of Theorem 2.2's proof, because \(\max\{10, \cdot\}\) does not add the tail's value to a fixed prefix.

R3 · Counting plans against the recursion

Suppose \(|A(s)| = m\) for every \(s \in S\). Show that there are \(m^{H-t}\) plans from \((t,s)\), and compare that count with the number of candidate sums in the recursion for the same problem.

Solution

A plan from \((t,s)\) is a sequence of \(H - t\) actions, where each action is chosen from the set of feasible actions at the state reached. Each state has \(m\) feasible actions, so there are \(m\) choices at each of the \(H - t\) times, and \(m^{H-t}\) plans in all. The recursion computes \(V_k(s')\) from the \(m\) candidate sums at each state \(s'\) and each time \(k\) from \(t\) to \(H-1\). That is at most \(H \cdot |S| \cdot m\) candidate sums for the whole table. The plan count grows exponentially in \(H - t\), and the candidate count grows linearly in \(H\).
