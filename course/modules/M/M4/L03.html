Skewness and Kurtosis

  • Uses: random variable (Lesson 1); expectation and variance (Lesson 2)
  • Introduces: skewness and kurtosis
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore

A variance is one number. It does not say whether the distances from the mean lie more on one side than the other, or how the probability is spread across the distances. This lesson defines two shape numbers, each an expectation of a power of the distance from the mean divided by the standard deviation (§2), and each summarizes one of these shape features in a single number. It derives their values for a two-point law in closed form, proves two facts that limit how they can be read, and checks the closed forms with exact arithmetic.

1Moments and central moments

A moment of a random variable is an expectation of a power of it. For a positive integer \(k\), the \(k\)-th moment is \(E[X^k]\), and the \(k\)-th central moment is \(m_k = E[(X - \mu)^k]\), where \(\mu = E[X]\). Every moment of a finite law is defined, because it is a finite sum. The variance of Lesson 2 is \(m_2\).

Proposition 1.1 (the first two central moments)

For a random variable \(X\) on a finite probability space with positive weights, \(m_1 = 0\) and \(m_2 = \operatorname{Var}(X)\).

Proof

By linearity of expectation (Lesson 2, Proposition 1.3), \(m_1 = E[X] - \mu = \mu - \mu = 0\). The second claim is Definition 2.1 of Lesson 2 with \(k = 2\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Standardized moments: skewness and kurtosis

Definition 2.1 (standardized variable)

Let \(X\) take at least two values on a finite probability space with positive weights, so that \(\sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)} > 0\) by Lesson 2, Proposition 2.4. The standardized variable is \(Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma\). It has mean 0 and variance 1, which Proposition 2.3 checks.

Definition 2.2 (skewness and kurtosis)

The skewness of \(X\) is \(\gamma_3 = E[Z^3]\), the third standardized moment. The kurtosis of \(X\) is \(\gamma_4 = E[Z^4]\), the fourth standardized moment. This lesson uses the kurtosis without subtracting 3. Excess kurtosis, the kurtosis minus 3, is a different quantity and is not used on this page. Every kurtosis computed on this page is \(E[Z^4]\) in this sense.

Proposition 2.3 (standardized moments in central moments)

Let \(X\) take at least two values on a finite probability space with positive weights. Then \(E[Z] = 0\), \(E[Z^2] = 1\), \(\gamma_3 = m_3 / \sigma^3\), and \(\gamma_4 = m_4 / \sigma^4 = m_4 / m_2^2\).

Proof

Write \(Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma\), where \(\sigma\) is a positive constant. By linearity (Lesson 2, Proposition 1.3), \(E[Z^j] = E[(X - \mu)^j]/\sigma^j\) for \(j = 1, 2, 3, 4\), because \((X - \mu)^j / \sigma^j\) is the \(j\)-th power of \(Z\) and dividing by the constant \(\sigma^j\) pulls out of the sum. So \(E[Z] = m_1/\sigma = 0\) and \(E[Z^2] = m_2/\sigma^2 = 1\). Also \(\gamma_3 = E[Z^3] = m_3/\sigma^3\) and \(\gamma_4 = E[Z^4] = m_4/\sigma^4\). Since \(\sigma^4 = m_2^2\), the last form follows. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.4 (affine maps)

Let \(X\) take at least two values on a finite probability space with positive weights, and let \(Y = aX + b\) with constants \(a \ne 0\) and \(b\). Then \(\gamma_{4,Y} = \gamma_{4,X}\), and \(\gamma_{3,Y} = \operatorname{sign}(a)\, \gamma_{3,X}\), where \(\operatorname{sign}(a)\) is 1 for \(a > 0\) and \(-1\) for \(a < 0\).

Proof

By Lesson 2, Proposition 2.3, \(\sigma_Y = |a| \sigma_X\), since \(\sigma_Y^2 = a^2 \sigma_X^2\). Also \(E[Y] = a\mu_X + b\), so \(Y - E[Y] = a(X - \mu_X)\). Therefore \(Z_Y = a(X - \mu_X) / (|a| \sigma_X) = \operatorname{sign}(a)\, Z_X\). Raising to the third power gives \(Z_Y^3 = \operatorname{sign}(a)^3 Z_X^3 = \operatorname{sign}(a)\, Z_X^3\), and to the fourth power gives \(Z_Y^4 = Z_X^4\), since \(\operatorname{sign}(a)^4 = 1\). Taking expectations gives the two claims. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.5 (kurtosis is at least 1)

Let \(X\) take at least two values on a finite probability space with positive weights. Then \(\gamma_4 \ge 1\), and \(\gamma_4 = 1\) if and only if \(X\) takes exactly two values, each with probability \(\tfrac12\).

Proof

Let \(W = Z^2\). By Proposition 2.3, \(E[W] = 1\) and \(E[W^2] = E[Z^4] = \gamma_4\). The shortcut formula of Lesson 2, Proposition 2.2, applied to \(W\), gives \(\operatorname{Var}(W) = E[W^2] - (E[W])^2 = \gamma_4 - 1\). A variance is nonnegative, so \(\gamma_4 \ge 1\). For the equality case, \(\gamma_4 = 1\) means \(\operatorname{Var}(W) = 0\), so by Lesson 2, Proposition 2.4, \(W = Z^2\) takes a single value, which is \(E[W] = 1\). Then \(Z\) takes the values \(\pm 1\). Since \(E[Z] = 0\), write \(P(Z = 1) = r\), so \(P(Z = -1) = 1 - r\), and \(E[Z] = r - (1 - r) = 2r - 1 = 0\), which gives \(r = \tfrac12\). Conversely, if \(X\) takes two values \(a < b\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, then \(\mu = (a + b)/2\) and \(\sigma = (b - a)/2\), so \(Z = \pm 1\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, and \(\gamma_4 = E[Z^4] = 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.6 (the two-point Bernoulli law)

Let \(X\) take the value 1 with probability \(p\) and 0 with probability \(q = 1 - p\), where \(0 < p < 1\). Then \(\mu = p\) and \(\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}(X) = p - p^2 = pq\), by Lesson 2, Proposition 2.2. The centred values are \(X - p = q\) when \(X = 1\), and \(X - p = -p\) when \(X = 0\). So \(m_3 = p\,q^3 - q\,p^3 = pq(q^2 - p^2) = pq(q - p)\), and \(m_4 = p\,q^4 + q\,p^4 = pq(q^3 + p^3) = pq(1 - 3pq)\), using \(q^3 + p^3 = (q + p)(q^2 - pq + p^2)\), \(q + p = 1\), and \(q^2 - pq + p^2 = (q + p)^2 - 3pq = 1 - 3pq\). By Proposition 2.3,

\[\gamma_3 = \frac{pq(q - p)}{(pq)^{3/2}} = \frac{1 - 2p}{\sqrt{pq}}, \qquad \gamma_4 = \frac{pq(1 - 3pq)}{(pq)^2} = \frac{1 - 3pq}{pq}.\]

For \(p = \tfrac1{10}\), \(pq = \tfrac{9}{100}\) and \(\sqrt{pq} = \tfrac{3}{10}\), so \(\gamma_3 = \tfrac{4/5}{3/10} = \tfrac83\) and \(\gamma_4 = \tfrac{73/100}{9/100} = \tfrac{73}{9}\), which is about 8.11. Lab 1 checks the squared skewness \(\gamma_3^2 = \tfrac{64}{9}\) and the kurtosis \(\gamma_4 = \tfrac{73}{9}\) exactly. For \(p = \tfrac14\), \(\gamma_4 = \tfrac73\), which is less than the \(\tfrac{73}{9}\) of the law with \(p = \tfrac1{10}\).

Assumption 2.7 (the Gaussian integral, used without proof)

Let \(f(z) = e^{-z^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\) for real \(z\), the density of the standard normal law. The page uses without proof that \(f\) is even, that \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(z)\,dz = 1\), and that \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |z|^{k} f(z)\,dz\) is finite for \(k = 1, 2, 3, 4\). It also uses the derivative \(f'(z) = -zf(z)\), which follows by direct differentiation of that formula, and integration by parts over the real line, whose boundary terms vanish because \(z^{k}f(z) \to 0\) as \(|z| \to \infty\). Each result that uses Assumption 2.7 says so in its statement.

Proposition 2.8 (standard normal moments and kurtosis)

Assuming Assumption 2.7, a random variable \(Z\) with density \(f\) (the standard normal law of Assumption 2.7) has \(E[Z] = 0\), \(E[Z^2] = 1\) and \(E[Z^4] = 3\). Its kurtosis is therefore \(\gamma_4 = 3\).

Proof

Since \(f(-z) = f(z)\), the function \(zf(z)\) is odd and integrable, so \(E[Z] = \int zf(z)\,dz = 0\). Because \(f'(z) = -zf(z)\), we have \(zf(z) = -f'(z)\). Integrate by parts with \(u = z\) and \(v = -f\): \(E[Z^2] = \int z\,(-f'(z))\,dz = \big[-zf(z)\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int f(z)\,dz = 0 + 1 = 1\). The boundary term is zero because \(zf(z) \to 0\) as \(|z| \to \infty\), and the last integral is 1 by Assumption 2.7. For the fourth moment, take \(u = z^{3}\) and \(v = -f\): \(E[Z^4] = \int z^{3}\,(-f'(z))\,dz = \big[-z^{3}f(z)\big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int 3z^{2}f(z)\,dz = 0 + 3E[Z^2] = 3\), with the boundary term zero for the same reason. Since \(E[Z] = 0\) and \(E[Z^2] = 1\), \(Z\) is already standardized (\(\mu = 0\), \(\sigma = 1\)), so the standardization formula of Definition 2.1 leaves \(Z\) unchanged, and the kurtosis of Definition 2.2 is \(\gamma_4 = E[Z^4] = 3\). The finite-space hypothesis of Definition 2.1 is not used in this proof. \(\blacksquare\)

3A skewed discrete law and an exact lab

Take the law with values 0, 1, 2 and 10 and probabilities \(\tfrac12\), \(\tfrac14\), \(\tfrac18\) and \(\tfrac18\). Its mean is \(\mu = \tfrac74\), and its central moments are \(m_2 = \tfrac{163}{16}\), \(m_3 = \tfrac{2157}{32}\) and \(m_4 = \tfrac{149461}{256}\). The third moment is a sum of four terms: \((-\tfrac74)^3 \cdot \tfrac12 + (-\tfrac34)^3 \cdot \tfrac14 + (\tfrac14)^3 \cdot \tfrac18 + (\tfrac{33}{4})^3 \cdot \tfrac18 = -\tfrac{1372}{512} - \tfrac{54}{512} + \tfrac{1}{512} + \tfrac{35937}{512} = \tfrac{34512}{512} = \tfrac{2157}{32}\). The term from the value 10 alone is \(\tfrac{35937}{512}\), which is larger than the total \(m_3 = \tfrac{34512}{512}\), so the other three terms, two negative and one positive (\(+\tfrac{1}{512}\)), together reduce it by \(\tfrac{1425}{512}\). By Proposition 2.3, \(\gamma_3^2 = m_3^2 / m_2^3\) and \(\gamma_4 = m_4 / m_2^2\) are exact fractions, and Lab 1 prints them.

"""M4 lab L03: standardized third and fourth moments, computed exactly, then a seeded sample check.

Standard library only (fractions, math, random, collections.abc). The skewed law is a stated table: values 0, 1, 2 and
10 with probabilities 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/8. Its central moments m_k = E[(X - mu)^k] are exact rationals. The
standardized skewness is E[Z^3] with Z = (X - mu)/sigma, which equals m3 / m2^(3/2); its square is the exact rational
m3^2 / m2^3, and the kurtosis E[Z^4] equals m4 / m2^2 exactly. The program checks the moment formulas against a
two-point Bernoulli law with p = 1/10, where the closed forms (1 - 2p)^2 / (pq) for the squared skewness and (1 - 3pq)
/ (pq) for the kurtosis are derived on the lesson page, with q = 1 - p. Last, it draws 20000 seeded values (seed 2026)
from the skewed law and prints the sample skewness and kurtosis, using the population form of each moment with divisor
n. A sample is one random run. Its values are estimates that depend on the seed, and the exact values printed above
them do not.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
from collections.abc import Sequence
from fractions import Fraction

Law = Sequence[tuple[int, Fraction]]

SKEWED: tuple[tuple[int, Fraction], ...] = (
    (0, Fraction(1, 2)),
    (1, Fraction(1, 4)),
    (2, Fraction(1, 8)),
    (10, Fraction(1, 8)),
)
WEIGHTS = (4, 2, 1, 1)  # integer weights proportional to 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8
SEED = 2026
N_DRAWS = 20000


def check_law(law: Law) -> None:
    """Raise ValueError unless every probability lies in [0, 1] and the probabilities sum to exactly 1."""
    if any(p < 0 or p > 1 for _, p in law):
        raise ValueError("each probability must lie in [0, 1]")
    if sum((p for _, p in law), Fraction(0)) != 1:
        raise ValueError("probabilities must sum to exactly 1")


def mean(law: Law) -> Fraction:
    """E[X] for a finite law."""
    return sum((v * p for v, p in law), Fraction(0))


def central_moment(law: Law, k: int) -> Fraction:
    """m_k = E[(X - mu)^k] for an integer k >= 2, exact."""
    mu = mean(law)
    return sum(((v - mu) ** k * p for v, p in law), Fraction(0))


def bernoulli(p: Fraction) -> tuple[tuple[int, Fraction], ...]:
    """The law with P(X = 1) = p and P(X = 0) = 1 - p."""
    return ((0, 1 - p), (1, p))


def sample_central_moments(xs: Sequence[int]) -> tuple[float, float, float]:
    """Sample m2, m3 and m4 about the sample mean, each with divisor n."""
    n = len(xs)
    mu = sum(xs) / n
    m2 = sum((x - mu) ** 2 for x in xs) / n
    m3 = sum((x - mu) ** 3 for x in xs) / n
    m4 = sum((x - mu) ** 4 for x in xs) / n
    return m2, m3, m4


def main() -> None:
    check_law(SKEWED)
    mu = mean(SKEWED)
    m2 = central_moment(SKEWED, 2)
    m3 = central_moment(SKEWED, 3)
    m4 = central_moment(SKEWED, 4)
    print(f"skewed law: E[X] = {mu}, Var(X) = m2 = {m2}")
    print(f"third central moment m3 = {m3}, fourth central moment m4 = {m4}")

    skew_sq = m3 * m3 / (m2 * m2 * m2)
    kurt = m4 / (m2 * m2)
    skew = float(m3) / float(m2) ** 1.5
    print(f"squared skewness m3^2 / m2^3 = {skew_sq} (exact), skewness = {skew:.4f}")
    print(f"kurtosis m4 / m2^2 = {kurt} (exact), {float(kurt):.4f}")

    p = Fraction(1, 10)
    q = 1 - p
    bern = bernoulli(p)
    bern_skew_sq = central_moment(bern, 3) ** 2 / central_moment(bern, 2) ** 3
    bern_kurt = central_moment(bern, 4) / central_moment(bern, 2) ** 2
    closed_skew_sq = (1 - 2 * p) ** 2 / (p * q)
    closed_kurt = (1 - 3 * p * q) / (p * q)
    print(
        f"Bernoulli(1/10) squared skewness {bern_skew_sq} equals (1 - 2p)^2 / (pq) = {closed_skew_sq}: "
        f"{bern_skew_sq == closed_skew_sq}"
    )
    print(f"Bernoulli(1/10) kurtosis {bern_kurt} equals (1 - 3pq) / (pq) = {closed_kurt}: {bern_kurt == closed_kurt}")

    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    values = [v for v, _ in SKEWED]
    draws = rng.choices(values, weights=WEIGHTS, k=N_DRAWS)
    s2, s3, s4 = sample_central_moments(draws)
    print(
        f"skewed law sample, seed {SEED}, n = {N_DRAWS}: skewness {s3 / math.pow(s2, 1.5):.3f}, "
        f"kurtosis {s4 / (s2 * s2):.3f}"
    )
    print(f"exact skewness {skew:.3f} and exact kurtosis {float(kurt):.3f} for the same law")

4Where the canon breaks

The two numbers are summaries, and each has a specific reading that the definitions do not extend. A law is symmetric about its mean when \(X - \mu\) and \(-(X - \mu)\) have the same distribution. This is a statement about the whole law, not about one moment.

  • Zero skewness does not mean symmetry. Take the values \(-2\), 1 and 3 with probabilities \(\tfrac25\), \(\tfrac12\) and \(\tfrac1{10}\). The mean is \(0\), since \(-\tfrac45 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac{3}{10} = 0\). The third moment is \((-2)^3 \cdot \tfrac25 + 1 \cdot \tfrac12 + 27 \cdot \tfrac{1}{10} = -\tfrac{16}{5} + \tfrac12 + \tfrac{27}{10} = 0\), so \(\gamma_3 = 0\). The law is not symmetric about its mean, because the value \(-2\) has probability \(\tfrac25\) while the value 2 has probability 0. Its kurtosis is \(\gamma_4 = m_4/m_2^2 = 15/9 = \tfrac53\), since \(m_2 = 3\) and \(m_4 = 15\), a computation the reader can check from the same three values.
  • Kurtosis is not a peakedness measure by itself. Proposition 2.5 gives \(\gamma_4 \ge 1\), with equality only for the two-point symmetric law. A value of \(\gamma_4\) above 1 says that the standardized fourth moment is larger, not where the probability sits. The Bernoulli law with \(p = \tfrac{1}{10}\) has \(\gamma_4 = \tfrac{73}{9}\) and two values only.
  • The moments must be finite. Every law the computations in §1 to §3 use is finite, so every value computed there is defined. The one exception is the standard normal law of Proposition 2.8, whose moments are integrals and are finite only under Assumption 2.7. For a law whose fourth moment is infinite, \(\gamma_4\) has no value. This lesson constructs no such law.
  • A sample value is an estimate. Lab 1 prints sample values for one seed. They approximate the exact values for the same law and are not the exact values themselves.

5Misconceptions

  1. “A skewness of zero means the law is symmetric.”

    The law on \(-2\), 1 and 3 in §4 has \(\gamma_3 = 0\), and it is not symmetric about its mean. The third moment is a single sum, and cancellation of its terms can give zero without any symmetry. A symmetry claim needs the distribution itself, not one moment.

  2. “The kurtosis of every law is 3.”

    Example 2.6 gives \(\gamma_4 = \tfrac{73}{9}\) for the Bernoulli law with \(p = \tfrac1{10}\), and \(\gamma_4 = \tfrac73\) for \(p = \tfrac14\). Neither is 3, so the belief is false for these two laws, which the page computes. The value 3 is the kurtosis of the standard normal law (Proposition 2.8, under Assumption 2.7), but it is not special to that law: the Bernoulli law with \(p = \tfrac{1 - 1/\sqrt{3}}{2}\) has \(pq = \tfrac16\), so \(\gamma_4 = (1 - 3pq)/(pq) = 3\) by Example 2.6.

  3. “Reversing the sign of a variable leaves its skewness unchanged.”

    Proposition 2.4 gives \(\gamma_{3,Y} = \operatorname{sign}(a)\,\gamma_{3,X}\), so \(Y = -X\) has the opposite skewness. Kurtosis does not change under the same map, because \(\operatorname{sign}(a)^4 = 1\).

Mechanics

M1 · Bernoulli with \(p = \tfrac14\)

For the Bernoulli law with \(p = \tfrac14\), find \(\sigma\), \(\gamma_3\) and \(\gamma_4\) in exact form.

Solution

Here \(q = \tfrac34\), so \(pq = \tfrac{3}{16}\) and \(\sigma = \sqrt{3}/4\). By Example 2.6, \(\gamma_3 = (1 - 2p)/\sqrt{pq} = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{4}{\sqrt 3} = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt 3}\), which is about 1.155. The kurtosis is \(\gamma_4 = (1 - 3pq)/(pq) = (1 - \tfrac{9}{16}) \cdot \tfrac{16}{3} = \tfrac{7}{16} \cdot \tfrac{16}{3} = \tfrac73\).

M2 · A symmetric law on plus and minus one

Let \(X\) take the values 0 and 2 with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. Find \(\mu\), \(\gamma_3\) and \(\gamma_4\).

Solution

The mean is \(\mu = 1\). Then \(X - \mu\) is \(\pm 1\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, so \(m_2 = 1\), \(m_3 = \tfrac12(1) + \tfrac12(-1) = 0\), and \(m_4 = 1\). So \(\sigma = 1\), \(\gamma_3 = 0\), and \(\gamma_4 = m_4/m_2^2 = 1\). This is the equality case of Proposition 2.5.

M3 · The third moment of the skewed law

For the law with values 0, 1, 2, 10 and probabilities \(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18\), verify that \(m_3 = \tfrac{2157}{32}\) and state the sign of \(\gamma_3\).

Solution

The mean is \(\mu = \tfrac74\). The four terms of \(m_3 = \sum (x - \mu)^3 p(x)\) are \(-\tfrac{343}{64} \cdot \tfrac12 = -\tfrac{1372}{512}\), \(-\tfrac{27}{64} \cdot \tfrac14 = -\tfrac{54}{512}\), \(\tfrac{1}{64} \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac{1}{512}\), and \(\tfrac{35937}{64} \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac{35937}{512}\). Their sum is \(\tfrac{34512}{512} = \tfrac{2157}{32}\). Since \(m_3 > 0\) and \(\sigma > 0\), \(\gamma_3 = m_3/\sigma^3 > 0\) by Definition 2.2 and Proposition 2.3.

P1 · Affine invariance of kurtosis

Prove Proposition 2.4 for \(X\) taking at least two values on a finite probability space with positive weights, and \(Y = aX + b\) with \(a \ne 0\), using only Definition 2.1 and the properties of expectation.

Solution

\(E[Y] = a\mu + b\), so \(Y - E[Y] = a(X - \mu)\). The variance of \(Y\) is \(E[a^2 (X - \mu)^2] = a^2 \sigma^2\), so \(\sigma_Y = |a|\sigma\). Then \(Z_Y = a(X - \mu)/(|a|\sigma) = \operatorname{sign}(a) Z_X\). Fourth powers remove the sign, so \(E[Z_Y^4] = E[Z_X^4]\), which gives \(\gamma_{4,Y} = \gamma_{4,X}\). The third power keeps the sign, so \(\gamma_{3,Y} = \operatorname{sign}(a) \gamma_{3,X}\).

P2 · The second standardized moment is one

Prove that \(E[Z^2] = 1\) for the standardized variable of Definition 2.1, without using Proposition 2.3 as a black box.

Solution

\(Z^2 = (X - \mu)^2 / \sigma^2\). Since \(\sigma^2 = E[(X - \mu)^2]\) by Lesson 2, Definition 2.1, the expectation of \(Z^2\) is \(E[(X - \mu)^2]/\sigma^2 = \sigma^2/\sigma^2 = 1\). The step pulls the constant \(\sigma^{-2}\) out of the expectation, which is linearity (Lesson 2, Proposition 1.3).

P3 · The fourth moment of the Bernoulli law

Verify the identity \(q^3 + p^3 = 1 - 3pq\) for \(q = 1 - p\), and use it to confirm \(m_4 = pq(1 - 3pq)\) in Example 2.6.

Solution

Since \(q + p = 1\), \(q^3 + p^3 = (q + p)(q^2 - qp + p^2) = q^2 - qp + p^2\). Also \(q^2 + 2qp + p^2 = (q + p)^2 = 1\), so \(q^2 + p^2 = 1 - 2qp\), and \(q^2 - qp + p^2 = 1 - 2qp - qp = 1 - 3pq\). With \(q^3 + p^3 = 1 - 3pq\), the expression \(m_4 = p\,q^4 + q\,p^4 = pq(q^3 + p^3) = pq(1 - 3pq)\) follows.

Empirical

E1 · The seeded sample against the exact values (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the seed 2026 sample skewness and kurtosis for 20000 draws, and then the exact values. State the gap in each case and say what the printed figures do not establish.

Solution

Compare the printed sample skewness with the exact skewness 2.0730, and the printed sample kurtosis with the exact 5.6254; the lab prints the exact values as 2.073 and 5.625 in its last line, at three decimals. The gaps are 0.008 for skewness and 0.045 for kurtosis, for this one seed, computed from the printed three-decimal values. The figures do not establish a bound on the error of a sample of this size, and they say nothing about another seed.

E2 · Checking the Bernoulli closed forms (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints two Bernoulli lines, one for the squared skewness and one for the kurtosis, and each ends in True. Verify both printed values from Example 2.6.

Solution

For \(p = \tfrac1{10}\), \(pq = \tfrac{9}{100}\). The squared skewness is \((1 - 2p)^2/(pq) = (\tfrac{4}{5})^2 / \tfrac{9}{100} = \tfrac{16}{25} \cdot \tfrac{100}{9} = \tfrac{64}{9}\). The kurtosis is \((1 - 3pq)/(pq) = \tfrac{73}{100} \cdot \tfrac{100}{9} = \tfrac{73}{9}\). Both match the printed values, and the True lines show that the moment definitions give the same fractions.

E3 · Why the sampler uses integer weights (Lab 1)

Lab 1 draws with weights 4, 2, 1 and 1. Explain why these weights give the probabilities \(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18\), and say whether the sample proportions must equal these probabilities exactly.

Solution

The weights total 8, and \(4/8 = \tfrac12\), \(2/8 = \tfrac14\), \(1/8 = \tfrac18\). So the weights are the probabilities up to the common factor 8, and the sampler selects each value with its probability. The sample proportions are random and need not equal the probabilities exactly. The printed sample moments are computed from the draws, so they inherit that randomness.

Research

R1 · When is kurtosis exactly one

Using Proposition 2.5, find every finite law with \(\gamma_4 = 1\), and show that a law with the same mean and variance but a different two-point structure has \(\gamma_4 > 1\) unless it is the symmetric two-point law. Identify the law in the equality case.

Solution

Proposition 2.5 says \(\gamma_4 = 1\) exactly when \(X\) takes two values with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. Those values are \(\mu - \sigma\) and \(\mu + \sigma\), since \(Z = \pm 1\) gives \(X = \mu \pm \sigma\). Any other finite law, with three or more values or with two values of unequal probability, has \(\operatorname{Var}(Z^2) > 0\) by Lesson 2, Proposition 2.4 applied to \(W = Z^2\). The reason is that \(W\) is constant only when \(Z\) takes two values \(\pm c\), and then \(E[Z] = 0\) forces probability \(\tfrac12\) on each, which is the symmetric two-point law. So its kurtosis is strictly greater than 1. The equality case is therefore the symmetric two-point law at \(\mu \pm \sigma\), and it is the only finite law with at least two values and kurtosis 1.

R2 · Why a single kurtosis is not a tail statement

A report states that the kurtosis of returns is 8, so the returns are fat-tailed. Using Example 2.6, explain why the number 8 alone does not identify the tail, and state what additional quantity the claim would need.

Solution

The Bernoulli law with \(p = \tfrac1{10}\) has kurtosis \(\tfrac{73}{9}\), which is about 8.11, and it has only two values, so it has no tail beyond them. A kurtosis of about 8 is therefore compatible with a law that has no tail beyond its values: the Bernoulli law above has kurtosis about 8.11 and no tail. The word fat-tailed is a statement about how the probability of large values decays, and it needs the tail probabilities themselves, for example \(P(|X - \mu| > t)\) for a range of \(t\). This lesson does not compute those, so it cannot support the word.

R3 · Symmetric about the mean implies zero skewness

Let \(X\) take the values \(\mu - d\) and \(\mu + d\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, with \(d > 0\). Show \(\gamma_3 = 0\), and explain why this does not prove the converse.

Solution

\(X - \mu\) is \(\pm d\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, so \(m_3 = \tfrac12 d^3 + \tfrac12 (-d)^3 = 0\), and \(\gamma_3 = 0\). For the converse, the law of §4 has \(\gamma_3 = 0\) but is not symmetric, so a zero skewness alone does not force symmetry. The implication runs one way: symmetry about the mean gives zero third central moment, and zero third central moment does not give symmetry.

  1. What is \(m_1\), the first central moment, of a random variable on a finite probability space with positive weights?

    Proposition 1.1: \(m_1 = E[X] - \mu = 0\).

  2. Which expression is the skewness of \(X\)?

    Options

    Definition 2.2: skewness is the third standardized moment, \(E[Z^3]\). The fourth is the kurtosis.

  3. Under the symmetric law that takes the values \(\mu \pm \sigma\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, what is the kurtosis?

    Proposition 2.5: the equality case of \(\gamma_4 \ge 1\). Direct check: \(Z = \pm 1\), so \(Z^4 = 1\).

  4. If \(Y = -X\), how does the skewness change?

    Options

    Proposition 2.4 with \(a = -1\): \(\gamma_{3,Y} = \operatorname{sign}(-1)\gamma_{3,X} = -\gamma_{3,X}\).

  5. If \(Y = 5X + 3\), how does the kurtosis change?

    Options

    Proposition 2.4: for \(X\) taking at least two values, \(\gamma_4\) is invariant under every affine map with \(a \ne 0\).

  6. For the law on \(-2\), 1, 3 with probabilities \(\tfrac25\), \(\tfrac12\), \(\tfrac1{10}\), what is the kurtosis, as a decimal to four places?

    The mean is 0 and \(m_2 = 3\), \(m_4 = 15\), so \(\gamma_4 = 15/9 = \tfrac53 \approx 1.6667\) (§4).

  7. Which statement about the law on \(-2\), 1, 3 of §4 is true?

    Options

    §4: \(\gamma_3 = 0\), but the value \(-2\) has probability \(\tfrac25\), while its reflection \(2\) about the mean has probability 0, so the law is not symmetric.

  8. For the Bernoulli law with \(p = \tfrac1{10}\), what is the skewness, as a decimal to four places?

    Example 2.6: \(\gamma_3 = (1 - 2p)/\sqrt{pq} = (0.8)/(0.3) = \tfrac83 \approx 2.6667\).

  9. What is the skewness of a law that is symmetric about its mean?

    Options

    Research problem R3: \(X - \mu\) and \(-(X - \mu)\) have the same law, so \(m_3 = -m_3\) and \(m_3 = 0\).
