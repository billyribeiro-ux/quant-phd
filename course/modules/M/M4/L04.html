Independence and conditional expectation

  • Uses: random variable and its distribution, expectation
  • Introduces: independence, conditional distribution and conditional expectation (elementary)
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Two quantities can move together or apart, and the joint table of their probabilities records which. This lesson defines independence for two discrete random variables by the factorization of that table, and proves that independence makes the expectation of a product equal the product of the expectations. The converse fails. Example 1.9 gives a pair with zero covariance that is dependent, and Lab 1 checks it in exact arithmetic. The lesson also defines the conditional expectation \(E[X \mid Y = y]\) from a finite table and proves the total-expectation identity. Lesson L05 turns the covariance into the correlation and the covariance matrix.

1Independence and the joint table

All random variables here take finitely many values. Write \(p(x,y) = P(X = x,\ Y = y)\) for the joint probability, and let the sums below run over the finitely many values in the table.

Definition 1.1 (joint table and marginals)

A joint table for X and Y is the list of values \(p(x,y)\) over the pairs of values, each \(p(x,y) \ge 0\), with \(\sum_{x,y} p(x,y) = 1\). Its marginal probabilities are \(P(X = x) = \sum_{y} p(x,y)\) and \(P(Y = y) = \sum_{x} p(x,y)\). The marginals are the distributions of X and Y alone, as defined in L01.

Definition 1.2 (independence)

Independence of two events \(A\) and \(B\) means \(P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)\). Discrete random variables X and Y are independent when \(p(x,y) = P(X = x)\,P(Y = y)\) for every pair \((x,y)\) of values. In words, every cell of the joint table is the product of its two marginals.

Remark 1.3 (the test is cell by cell)

Independence is a property of the whole joint table. Each pair \((x,y)\) of values is a cell of the table. One cell that fails the product rule makes the pair dependent. Lab 1 applies the test to every cell of two tables and prints the verdict.

Proposition 1.4 (disjoint events are dependent)

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be events with \(A \cap B = \varnothing\), \(P(A) > 0\) and \(P(B) > 0\). Then \(A\) and \(B\) are not independent.

Proof

Since \(A \cap B = \varnothing\), \(P(A \cap B) = 0\). The hypotheses give \(P(A)\,P(B) > 0\), because a product of two positive numbers is positive. So \(P(A \cap B) \ne P(A)P(B)\).

Proposition 1.5 (the expectation of a product under independence)

Let X and Y be independent random variables with finitely many values. Then \(E[XY] = E[X]\,E[Y]\).

Proof

Group the pairs \((x,y)\) by the value \(z = xy\) of the product. This regrouping does not change the finite sum, so \(E[XY] = \sum_{x,y} xy\,p(x,y)\). Independence replaces each cell, giving \(\sum_{x,y} xy\,P(X = x)\,P(Y = y)\). The sum factors, because the summand is a product of a function of x and a function of y: \[ \sum_{x}\sum_{y} x\,P(X = x)\; y\,P(Y = y) = \Big(\sum_{x} x\,P(X = x)\Big)\Big(\sum_{y} y\,P(Y = y)\Big) = E[X]\,E[Y]. \]

Definition 1.6 (covariance, defined here and studied in L05)

The covariance of X and Y is \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = E[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])]\). Only its formula is used in this lesson. L05 defines the correlation from it and proves its properties.

Proposition 1.7 (the shortcut formula for covariance)

For random variables X and Y with finitely many values, \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = E[XY] - E[X]\,E[Y]\).

Proof

Expand the product inside the expectation: \[ (X - E[X])(Y - E[Y]) = XY - E[Y]\,X - E[X]\,Y + E[X]\,E[Y]. \] Expectation is linear over finite sums, and the expectations of the constants \(E[Y]\) and \(E[X]\) are themselves. So \[ E[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])] = E[XY] - E[Y]\,E[X] - E[X]\,E[Y] + E[X]\,E[Y] = E[XY] - E[X]\,E[Y]. \]

Proposition 1.8 (independence implies zero covariance)

If X and Y are independent with finitely many values, then \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = 0\).

Proof

By Proposition 1.5, \(E[XY] = E[X]\,E[Y]\). By Proposition 1.7, the covariance is \(E[XY] - E[X]\,E[Y] = 0\).

Example 1.9 (zero covariance without independence)

Let X be uniform on \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\), so each value has probability \(1/3\), and let \(Y = X^{2}\). Then \(E[X] = 0\), \(E[Y] = 2/3\) and \(E[XY] = E[X^{3}] = 0\). By Proposition 1.7, \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = 0 - 0 \cdot 2/3 = 0\). The pair is not independent. The cell \((0,0)\) has \(p(0,0) = P(X = 0,\ Y = 0) = 1/3\), while \(P(X = 0)\,P(Y = 0) = (1/3)(1/3) = 1/9\). So the converse of Proposition 1.8 is false. Lab 1 Part B prints these values.

2Conditional expectation from a finite table

Definition 2.1 (conditional distribution and conditional expectation, elementary form)

For a value y with \(P(Y = y) > 0\), the conditional distribution of X given \(Y = y\) is \(P(X = x \mid Y = y) = p(x,y)/P(Y = y)\), a probability over the values x. Its expectation is the conditional expectation \(E[X \mid Y = y] = \sum_{x} x\,P(X = x \mid Y = y)\), which this lesson writes in the same way for every such y.

Remark 2.2 (the scope of this lesson)

Definition 2.1 covers conditioning on one discrete variable with a finite table. It does not define conditioning on a variable with a continuous distribution, or on an event of probability zero. This lesson does not treat either case, and nothing later in this lesson needs them.

Proposition 2.3 (total expectation)

For random variables X and Y with finitely many values, \(E[X] = \sum_{y:\,P(Y=y) > 0} E[X \mid Y = y]\,P(Y = y)\).

Proof

For \(P(Y = y) > 0\), \(E[X \mid Y = y]\,P(Y = y) = \sum_{x} x\,p(x,y)\). Summing over these y, and noting that the values with \(P(Y = y) = 0\) contribute \(p(x,y) = 0\) to the joint table, gives \(\sum_{x}\sum_{y} x\,p(x,y)\). Regrouping the finite sum by x gives \(\sum_{x} x \sum_{y} p(x,y) = \sum_{x} x\,P(X = x) = E[X]\).

Proposition 2.4 (independence fixes the conditional expectation)

If X and Y are independent with finitely many values, then \(E[X \mid Y = y] = E[X]\) for every y with \(P(Y = y) > 0\).

Proof

Independence gives \(p(x,y) = P(X = x)\,P(Y = y)\), so \(P(X = x \mid Y = y) = P(X = x)\,P(Y = y)/P(Y = y) = P(X = x)\) for every x. Substituting into the definition, \(E[X \mid Y = y] = \sum_{x} x\,P(X = x) = E[X]\).

Example 2.5 (the dependent table of Lab 1)

Take X in \(\{0,1,2\}\) and Y in \(\{0,1\}\) with \(p(0,0) = 1/10\), \(p(0,1) = 3/10\), \(p(1,0) = 2/10\), \(p(1,1) = 2/10\), \(p(2,0) = 1/10\) and \(p(2,1) = 1/10\). The marginals are \(P(Y = 0) = 2/5\) and \(P(Y = 1) = 3/5\). Then \(E[X] = 0 \cdot 2/5 + 1 \cdot 2/5 + 2 \cdot 1/5 = 4/5\). The conditional expectations are \(E[X \mid Y = 0] = (0 \cdot 1/10 + 1 \cdot 2/10 + 2 \cdot 1/10)/(2/5) = 1\) and \(E[X \mid Y = 1] = (0 \cdot 3/10 + 1 \cdot 2/10 + 2 \cdot 1/10)/(3/5) = 2/3\). Check Proposition 2.3: \(1 \cdot 2/5 + (2/3)(3/5) = 4/5\), which equals \(E[X]\). The two conditional expectations differ, so Proposition 2.4 does not apply, and the table is dependent. Lab 1 Part C prints these values.

Lab 1 · Independence and conditional expectation on finite tables

The inputs are exact tables written in the code, not data. Stated before running. Part A predicts that the product table passes the cell test in every cell and that the dependent table fails it, at cell (0,0). Part B predicts that X uniform on \(\{-1,0,1\}\) with \(Y = X^{2}\) has covariance 0 and fails the cell test at (0,0). Part C predicts \(E[X \mid Y = 0] = 1\), \(E[X \mid Y = 1] = 2/3\), a total-expectation sum equal to \(E[X] = 4/5\), and \(E[X \mid Y = y] = E[X]\) for the product table. The arithmetic is exact, and the code uses no random draws, so the output is fixed.

"""M4 L04 lab 1: independence and conditional expectation on finite joint tables.

Inputs are exact rational tables written in this file, not data. Every probability is a Fraction, so every
comparison is exact. Decimals are printed only at 6 places, for reading.

Part A: two joint tables on X in {0, 1, 2} and Y in {0, 1}. The first is a product table, so it is independent by
construction. The second is a dependent table. For each, the test P(X=x, Y=y) = P(X=x) P(Y=y) is applied to every
cell, and the verdict is printed with the cell (0, 0).
Part B: X uniform on {-1, 0, 1} and Y = X squared. Prints E[X], E[Y], E[XY], the covariance E[XY] - E[X] E[Y], and
the cell (0, 0) against the product of its marginals.
Part C: conditional expectations E[X | Y = y] for the dependent table of Part A, the unconditional E[X], the
total-expectation sum over y of E[X | Y = y] P(Y = y), and a check that a product table gives E[X | Y = y] = E[X].
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections.abc import Callable
from fractions import Fraction

Table = dict[tuple[int, int], Fraction]

DEPENDENT: Table = {
    (0, 0): Fraction(1, 10),
    (0, 1): Fraction(3, 10),
    (1, 0): Fraction(2, 10),
    (1, 1): Fraction(2, 10),
    (2, 0): Fraction(1, 10),
    (2, 1): Fraction(1, 10),
}
PX: dict[int, Fraction] = {0: Fraction(1, 5), 1: Fraction(2, 5), 2: Fraction(2, 5)}
PY: dict[int, Fraction] = {0: Fraction(3, 10), 1: Fraction(7, 10)}
PRODUCT: Table = {(x, y): PX[x] * PY[y] for x in PX for y in PY}


def marginals(table: Table) -> tuple[dict[int, Fraction], dict[int, Fraction]]:
    """Return the marginal pmfs of X (first coordinate) and Y (second coordinate) of a joint table."""
    px: dict[int, Fraction] = {}
    py: dict[int, Fraction] = {}
    for (x, y), p in table.items():
        px[x] = px.get(x, Fraction(0)) + p
        py[y] = py.get(y, Fraction(0)) + p
    return px, py


def is_independent(table: Table) -> bool:
    """True when every cell equals the product of the two marginals of that cell, compared exactly."""
    px, py = marginals(table)
    return all(p == px[x] * py[y] for (x, y), p in table.items())


def expectation_of(table: Table, g: Callable[[int, int], int]) -> Fraction:
    """E[g(X, Y)] for a finite joint table: the sum of g(x, y) times P(X=x, Y=y), exactly."""
    return sum((g(x, y) * p for (x, y), p in table.items()), Fraction(0))


def conditional_expectation_x(table: Table, y: int) -> Fraction:
    """E[X | Y = y] = sum over x of x P(X=x, Y=y) divided by P(Y=y). Fails loudly when P(Y=y) is zero."""
    _, py = marginals(table)
    if py.get(y, Fraction(0)) == 0:
        raise ValueError(f"P(Y = {y}) is zero, so E[X | Y = {y}] is not defined")
    numerator = sum((x * p for (x, yy), p in table.items() if yy == y), Fraction(0))
    return numerator / py[y]


def show(q: Fraction) -> str:
    """An exact fraction, then its value to 6 places in brackets."""
    return f"{q} [{float(q):.6f}]"


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A, B and C. The tables are fixed, so every run prints the same lines."""
    print("Part A: independence of the joint table, cell by cell")
    for name, table in (("product", PRODUCT), ("dependent", DEPENDENT)):
        px, py = marginals(table)
        print(f"table {name} independent {is_independent(table)}")
        print(f"table {name} cell_0_0 joint {show(table[(0, 0)])} product_of_marginals {show(px[0] * py[0])}")

    print("Part B: X uniform on {-1, 0, 1} and Y = X squared")
    uniform = {(x, x * x): Fraction(1, 3) for x in (-1, 0, 1)}
    px, py = marginals(uniform)
    ex = expectation_of(uniform, lambda x, y: x)
    ey = expectation_of(uniform, lambda x, y: y)
    exy = expectation_of(uniform, lambda x, y: x * y)
    print(f"E[X] {show(ex)}")
    print(f"E[Y] {show(ey)}")
    print(f"E[XY] {show(exy)}")
    print(f"covariance {show(exy - ex * ey)}")
    print(f"cell_0_0 joint {show(uniform[(0, 0)])} product_of_marginals {show(px[0] * py[0])}")
    print(f"independent {is_independent(uniform)}")

    print("Part C: conditional expectations on the dependent table")
    ex_dep = expectation_of(DEPENDENT, lambda x, y: x)
    print(f"E[X] {show(ex_dep)}")
    total = Fraction(0)
    for y in sorted(PY):
        cond = conditional_expectation_x(DEPENDENT, y)
        _, py_dep = marginals(DEPENDENT)
        total += cond * py_dep[y]
        print(f"E[X | Y = {y}] {show(cond)} P(Y = {y}) {show(py_dep[y])}")
    print(f"total_expectation_sum {show(total)} equals_E[X] {total == ex_dep}")
    product_gaps = []
    for y in sorted(PY):
        product_gaps.append(conditional_expectation_x(PRODUCT, y) == expectation_of(PRODUCT, lambda x, yy: x))
    print(f"product_table_conditional_equals_E[X] {all(product_gaps)}")

3Where the canon breaks

  • Zero covariance is not independence. Example 1.9 is a pair with \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = 0\) and \(p(0,0) = 1/3 \ne 1/9\). Lab 1 Part B prints both numbers. Proposition 1.8 holds only in one direction.
  • The marginals do not fix the joint table. Let X and Y each take the values 0 and 1 with probability 1/2. Take \(p(0,0) = p(1,1) = 1/2\) and \(p(0,1) = p(1,0) = 0\). Both marginals are the same as under independence, but \(p(0,0) = 1/2 \ne 1/4 = P(X = 0)\,P(Y = 0)\). So the pair is dependent, and the marginals alone cannot decide independence.
  • The product rule for a mean is stated under independence. Proposition 1.5 assumes it. If X takes \(\pm 1\) with probability 1/2 each and \(Y = X\), then \(E[XY] = E[X^{2}] = 1\), while \(E[X]\,E[Y] = 0\). The product rule fails for this dependent pair.
  • Conditional expectation is defined here for finite tables only. Definition 2.1 and Remark 2.2 exclude continuous conditioning. The results of §2 do not extend to it.

4Misconceptions

  1. “Zero covariance means the two variables are independent.”

    Proposition 1.8 gives only the forward direction. Example 1.9 has \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = 0\) and a cell that fails the product rule, \(p(0,0) = 1/3\) against \(1/9\). Lab 1 Part B prints covariance 0 and independent False for this table.

  2. “Two events with no outcome in common are independent.”

    Disjoint events are the opposite. Proposition 1.4 shows that if both have positive probability, \(P(A \cap B) = 0\) differs from \(P(A)P(B) > 0\), so they are dependent. Knowing that A occurred tells you that B did not.

  3. “The conditional expectation \(E[X \mid Y = y]\) is always the same as \(E[X]\).”

    This holds under independence (Proposition 2.4). For the dependent table of Example 2.5, \(E[X \mid Y = 0] = 1\) and \(E[X \mid Y = 1] = 2/3\), while \(E[X] = 4/5\). Lab 1 Part C prints these values. Observing Y changes the expected value of X in this table.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · Test one table for independence

A joint table has \(p(0,0) = 0.1\), \(p(0,1) = 0.2\), \(p(1,0) = 0.3\) and \(p(1,1) = 0.4\). Compute the marginals and decide whether X and Y are independent.

Solution

The marginals are \(P(X = 0) = 0.1 + 0.2 = 0.3\), \(P(X = 1) = 0.7\), \(P(Y = 0) = 0.1 + 0.3 = 0.4\) and \(P(Y = 1) = 0.6\). The cell \((0,0)\) needs \(P(X = 0)\,P(Y = 0) = 0.3 \times 0.4 = 0.12\), but \(p(0,0) = 0.1\). The cell fails, so the variables are dependent.

M2 · A conditional expectation from the table in M1

For the table in M1, compute \(E[X \mid Y = 1]\) exactly.

Solution

\(P(Y = 1) = 0.6\). The numerator is \(\sum_x x\,p(x,1) = 0 \cdot 0.2 + 1 \cdot 0.4 = 0.4\). So \(E[X \mid Y = 1] = 0.4/0.6 = 2/3\), which is 0.6667 to four places. This differs from \(E[X] = 0.7\), so in this table learning that Y = 1 changes the expected value of X, which is the dependence found in M1.

M3 · The product of independent indicators

Let X and Y be independent, with \(P(X = 1) = 1/4\), \(P(X = 0) = 3/4\), \(P(Y = 1) = 1/2\) and \(P(Y = 0) = 1/2\). Write out the joint table, check that it sums to 1, and compute \(E[XY]\) by two methods.

Solution

The cells are \(p(1,1) = 1/8\), \(p(1,0) = 1/8\), \(p(0,1) = 3/8\) and \(p(0,0) = 3/8\). They sum to \(1/8 + 1/8 + 3/8 + 3/8 = 1\). By Proposition 1.5, \(E[XY] = E[X]\,E[Y] = (1/4)(1/2) = 1/8\). Directly, \(XY = 1\) only at \((1,1)\), so \(E[XY] = p(1,1) = 1/8\). The two methods agree.

Proof

P1 · The covariance shortcut

Prove Proposition 1.7 by expanding the product, and state which property of expectation you use.

Solution

Expand \((X - E[X])(Y - E[Y]) = XY - E[Y]X - E[X]Y + E[X]E[Y]\). Expectation is linear over finite sums, and \(E[Y]\), \(E[X]\) and \(E[X]E[Y]\) are constants whose expectations equal themselves. The sum is \(E[XY] - E[Y]E[X] - E[X]E[Y] + E[X]E[Y] = E[XY] - E[X]E[Y]\). The only property used is linearity.

P2 · Proposition 2.4 for a two-valued Y

Suppose X and Y are independent and Y takes the value 1 with probability \(q\), where \(0 < q < 1\). Show that \(E[X \mid Y = 1] = E[X]\) and \(E[X \mid Y = 0] = E[X]\).

Solution

Both conditioning values have positive probability, because \(P(Y = 1) = q > 0\) and \(P(Y = 0) = 1 - q > 0\). Proposition 2.4 applies to each. Concretely, \(P(X = x \mid Y = y) = P(X = x)P(Y = y)/P(Y = y) = P(X = x)\) for \(y = 0\) and \(y = 1\). Summing \(x\,P(X = x)\) over x gives \(E[X]\) in both cases.

P3 · Disjoint events have a negative covariance

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be disjoint events with \(P(A) = a > 0\) and \(P(B) = b > 0\). Let \(X = 1_A\) and \(Y = 1_B\) be the indicators. Show that \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = -ab\).

Solution

Here \(XY = 1_{A \cap B} = 0\), because the events are disjoint. So \(E[XY] = 0\). Also \(E[X] = a\) and \(E[Y] = b\). By Proposition 1.7, \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = 0 - ab = -ab\), which is negative. This agrees with Proposition 1.4, since the two events are dependent.

Empirical

E1 · The total-expectation check from Lab 1

Read Part C of Lab 1. Using only its printed values, verify Proposition 2.3 for the dependent table.

Solution

Part C prints \(E[X \mid Y = 0] = 1\) with \(P(Y = 0) = 2/5\), and \(E[X \mid Y = 1] = 2/3\) with \(P(Y = 1) = 3/5\). The sum is \(1 \cdot 2/5 + (2/3)(3/5) = 2/5 + 2/5 = 4/5\). Part C prints \(E[X] = 4/5\) and equals_E[X] True, so the identity holds for this table.

E2 · The cell ratio in Part B

Read Part B of Lab 1. For the cell \((0,0)\), what is the ratio of the joint probability to the product of the marginals?

Solution

Part B prints the joint probability 1/3 and the product of marginals 1/9 for cell \((0,0)\). The ratio is \((1/3)/(1/9) = 3\). A ratio of 3 says the pair puts three times the independent probability on the cell where \(X = 0\) and \(Y = 0\) together. Covariance zero does not show this ratio.

E3 · A cell ratio below one

Read Part A of Lab 1. For the dependent table, the cell \((0,0)\) prints a joint probability of 1/10 and a product of marginals of 4/25. Compute the ratio, and say which direction the dependence goes in that cell.

Solution

The ratio is \((1/10)/(4/25) = 25/40 = 5/8 = 0.625\). A ratio below one means the cell has less probability than independence would give. In this cell, X = 0 and Y = 0 occur together less often than the marginals alone would predict.

Research

R1 · Zero covariance from a different function

Let X be uniform on \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\) and let \(Y = |X|\). Show that \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = 0\), and show that X and Y are dependent.

Solution

\(E[X] = 0\), \(E[Y] = (1 + 0 + 1)/3 = 2/3\), and \(XY = X|X|\) takes the values \(-1\), 0 and 1 with probability 1/3 each, so \(E[XY] = 0\). By Proposition 1.7, \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y) = 0 - 0 \cdot 2/3 = 0\). For dependence, \(p(0,0) = P(X = 0, Y = 0) = 1/3\), while \(P(X = 0)P(Y = 0) = (1/3)(1/3) = 1/9\). The cell fails the product rule.

R2 · Covariance of a variable with itself

For a random variable X with finitely many values, show that \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,X) = \operatorname{Var}(X)\), using the variance formula \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[X^{2}] - (E[X])^{2}\) from L02.

Solution

Set \(Y = X\) in Proposition 1.7. Then \(\operatorname{Cov}(X,X) = E[X \cdot X] - E[X]E[X] = E[X^{2}] - (E[X])^{2}\), which is \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) by the formula of L02. The covariance of a variable with itself is its variance.

R3 · A constant conditional expectation does not imply independence

Let X be uniform on \(\{-1, 0, 1\}\) and \(Y = X^{2}\), as in Example 1.9. Show that \(E[X \mid Y = 0] = E[X \mid Y = 1] = E[X]\), and show that X and Y are dependent.

Solution

Given \(Y = 0\), X must be 0, so \(E[X \mid Y = 0] = 0\). Given \(Y = 1\), X is \(-1\) or \(1\) with probability 1/2 each, so \(E[X \mid Y = 1] = (-1 + 1)/2 = 0\). Also \(E[X] = 0\). So all three agree. Dependence was shown in Example 1.9: \(p(0,0) = 1/3\) against \(1/9\). The conclusion is that equal conditional expectations do not imply independence.
