Laws of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem

  • Uses: random variable (Lesson 1); expectation and variance (Lesson 2); independence (Lesson 4); covariance (Lesson 5); characteristic function (Lesson 7); normal distribution (Lesson 6); limit (M2)
  • Introduces: law of large numbers and central limit theorem
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore

The average of \(n\) independent draws with a common variance \(\sigma^2\) has variance \(\sigma^2/n\), so its variance shrinks as \(n\) grows. This lesson proves that the probability of a large deviation of the sample mean tends to 0, using Chebyshev's inequality. It then proves the characteristic-function limit (Section 2) of the standardized sample mean for finitely valued laws, and states that a limit of distribution functions needs further steps that this page does not prove. Lab 1 prints exact values of \(P(Z_n \le 1)\) (with \(Z_n\) defined in Section 2) against \(\Phi(1)\), where \(\Phi\) is defined in Section 2, at five sizes, seeded running means, and a seeded simulation at three sizes; it prints no spread of the sample mean.

1Sample means and the weak law

Proposition 1.1, Theorem 1.3 and Propositions 2.1, 2.2 and 2.3 are stated for laws that take finitely many values, which keeps each expectation a finite sum, as in Lesson 2. Proposition 1.2 needs only identical distribution, a finite mean and variance, and pairwise independence. Section 3 compares these laws with the normal law, which is not finitely valued. Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be random variables that are identically distributed, meaning each has the same law as a fixed \(X\), with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), where \(0 < \sigma^2 < \infty\). The sample mean of the first \(n\) variables is \(\bar X_n = \frac1n (X_1 + \cdots + X_n)\). Let \[S_n = X_1 + \cdots + X_n\].

Proposition 1.1 (Chebyshev's inequality)

Let \(Y\) be a random variable with finitely many values, with mean \(m\) and variance \(v\). For every \(a > 0\), \(P(|Y - m| \ge a) \le v/a^2\).

Proof

Let \(W = (Y - m)^2\), which is nonnegative. On the event \(\{|Y - m| \ge a\}\), \(W \ge a^2\). Off that event, the indicator is 0 and \(0 \le W\). So pointwise \(a^2 \mathbf{1}\{|Y - m| \ge a\} \le W\). Taking expectations of both sides, which are finite sums, gives \[a^2 P(|Y - m| \ge a) \le E[W] = v\]. Divide by \(a^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.2 (mean and variance of the sample mean)

Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be identically distributed with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\), and suppose that \(X_i\) and \(X_j\) are independent whenever \(i \ne j\). Then \(E[\bar X_n] = \mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2/n\).

Proof

By linearity of expectation, \(E[\bar X_n] = \frac1n \sum_i E[X_i] = \mu\). For the variance, \[\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = E[(S_n - n\mu)^2] = \sum_{i} \sum_{j} E[(X_i - \mu)(X_j - \mu)]\]. For \(i \ne j\), independence of \(X_i\) and \(X_j\) (Lesson 4) gives \[E[(X_i - \mu)(X_j - \mu)] = E[X_i - \mu]\,E[X_j - \mu] = 0\]. For \(i = j\) the term is \(\sigma^2\). So \(\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = n\sigma^2\), and \[\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \operatorname{Var}(S_n)/n^2 = \sigma^2/n\], because the variance of \(cY\) is \(c^2\operatorname{Var}(Y)\) (Lesson 2). \(\blacksquare\)

Definition 1.4 (law of large numbers)

The law of large numbers holds for a sequence of identically distributed variables with mean \(\mu\) when \(P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\), for every \(\varepsilon > 0\). Theorem 1.3 proves it under the hypotheses of Proposition 1.2, for finitely valued laws.

Theorem 1.3 (weak law of large numbers)

Under the hypotheses of Proposition 1.2, with every law taking finitely many values, for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) and every \(n \ge 1\), \[ P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}, \] and therefore \(P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\).

Proof

Apply Proposition 1.1 to \(Y = \bar X_n\), with \(m = \mu\) by Proposition 1.2 and \(v = \sigma^2/n\), and with \(a = \varepsilon\). This gives the bound. The bound is a fixed number divided by \(n\), and \(\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2) \to 0\) as \(n \to \infty\) by the limit of \(1/n\) (M2), so the probability tends to 0 by the squeeze of the limit (M2). \(\blacksquare\)

The hypotheses of Theorem 1.3 are: identical distribution, a finite mean and variance, independence of each pair of distinct draws, and finitely many values for each law. The proof uses pairwise independence, through the zero covariance in Proposition 1.2, and does not need mutual independence. The theorem concerns the probability at each \(n\); it does not say that the path \(\bar X_1, \bar X_2, \ldots\) stays near \(\mu\).

2The standardized sample mean and its characteristic functions

Assume in this section that the \(X_i\) are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), which means that the joint probability of any values factorizes: \[P(X_1 = x_1, \ldots, X_n = x_n) = \prod_i P(X_i = x_i)\] for every \(n\) and every tuple of values, with each \(X_i\) having the law of \(X\). Put \[Y = (X - \mu)/\sigma\], which has mean 0 and variance 1, and put \[ Z_n = \frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} Y_i, \] where \(Y_i = (X_i - \mu)/\sigma\). The characteristic function of a random variable \(W\) is \(\varphi_W(t) = E[e^{itW}]\), defined for each real \(t\) as in Lesson 7, where \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) is the complex exponential with \(|e^{i\theta}| = 1\).

Assumption 1 (two limits used without proof)

The exponential function is continuous on the real line, and \(\ln(1 + u)/u \to 1\) as \(u \to 0\). The page uses both facts and does not prove either. Each result that uses them says so in its statement.

Proposition 2.1 (product rule for independent variables)

Let \(X_1, \ldots, X_n\) be finitely valued and independent in the sense above, and let each \(g_i\) be a complex-valued function on the values of \(X_i\). Then \[E[\prod_i g_i(X_i)] = \prod_i E[g_i(X_i)]\]. In particular, \(\varphi_{X_1 + \cdots + X_n}(t) = \prod_i \varphi_{X_i}(t)\) for real \(t\), and for i.i.d. variables this is \(\varphi_X(t)^n\).

Proof

Write \(p_i(x) = P(X_i = x)\). By the factorization of the joint probabilities, \[ E\Bigl[\prod_i g_i(X_i)\Bigr] = \sum_{x_1, \ldots, x_n} \prod_i g_i(x_i)\,p_i(x_i) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \Bigl(\sum_{x} g_i(x)\,p_i(x)\Bigr) = \prod_i E[g_i(X_i)]. \] The middle equality is the distributive law for a finite sum over a product of finite index sets. Taking \(g_i(x) = e^{it x}\) gives the characteristic function of the sum, since \(e^{it(X_1 + \cdots + X_n)} = \prod_i e^{itX_i}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.2 (expansion of the characteristic function near zero)

Let \(Y\) take finitely many values, with \(E[Y] = 0\), \(E[Y^2] = 1\), and \[M = \max|Y| > 0\]. For real \(s\) with \(|s| \le 1/M\), \[ \bigl| \varphi_Y(s) - (1 - s^2/2) \bigr| \le \frac{|s|^3\,E|Y|^3}{3}. \]

Proof

Let \(f(x) = \cos x - 1 + x^2/2\) and \(g(x) = \sin x - x\). Then \(f(0) = g(0) = 0\), \(f'(x) = x - \sin x = -g(x)\), and \(g'(x) = \cos x - 1\), which lies in \([-x^2/2, 0]\) because \(0 \le 1 - \cos u \le u^2/2\) for every real \(u\), by the double-angle identity \(1 - \cos u = 2\sin^2(u/2)\) and \(|\sin v| = \bigl|\int_0^v \cos t\,dt\bigr| \le |v|\), since \(|\cos t| \le 1\). Hence \(|g(x)| \le \int_0^{|x|} u^2/2\,du = |x|^3/6\). Also \[|f(x)| = |\int_0^x (u - \sin u)\,du| \le \int_0^{|x|} u^3/6\,du = x^4/24\]. For \(|x| \le 1\), \(x^4/24 \le |x|^3/24\), so \(|f(x)| + |g(x)| \le |x|^3/3\). Since \[e^{ix} - 1 - ix + x^2/2 = f(x) + i\,g(x)\], its modulus is at most \(|x|^3/3\) for \(|x| \le 1\). Apply this with \(x = sY\), which satisfies \(|sY| \le |s| M \le 1\). Taking expectations and using \(E[Y] = 0\) and \(E[Y^2] = 1\), \[\varphi_Y(s) = 1 + is\,E[Y] - \frac{s^2}{2} E[Y^2] + E[R] = 1 - s^2/2 + E[R]\], where \[R = e^{isY} - 1 - isY + s^2Y^2/2\] satisfies \(|R| \le |s|^3|Y|^3/3\). Then \[|E[R]| \le E|R| \le |s|^3E|Y|^3/3\]. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.3 (the standardized mean: second moment and characteristic functions)

Under the i.i.d. hypotheses of this section, with the variables finitely valued and \(Z_n\) as defined above: (a) \(E[Z_n] = 0\) and \(E[Z_n^2] = 1\) for every \(n\); (b) assuming Assumption 1, for every real \(t\), \(\varphi_{Z_n}(t) \to e^{-t^2/2}\) as \(n \to \infty\).

Proof

(a) Proposition 1.2 gives \(E[\bar X_n] = \mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2/n\). So \(E[Z_n] = 0\), and by the scale rule of Lesson 2, \(\operatorname{Var}(Z_n) = (\sigma^2/n)/(\sigma^2/n) = 1\). Since \(E[Z_n] = 0\), \(E[Z_n^2] = \operatorname{Var}(Z_n) = 1\).

(b) Fix real \(t\). By Proposition 2.1 with \(g_i(y) = e^{ity/\sqrt n}\), \(\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = a_n^{\,n}\), where \(a_n = \varphi_Y(t/\sqrt n)\). Let \[b_n = 1 - t^2/(2n)\]. For \(n\) large enough that \(|t|/\sqrt n \le 1/M\) and \(t^2 \le 2n\), both \(|a_n| \le 1\) (since \(|E[W]| \le E|W|\) and \(|e^{i\theta}| = 1\)) and \(|b_n| \le 1\). Telescoping, \[a^n - b^n = (a - b)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a^k b^{n-1-k}\], so \(|a^n - b^n| \le n|a - b|\) when \(|a|, |b| \le 1\). By Proposition 2.2 with \(s = t/\sqrt n\), \[ n|a_n - b_n| \le n \cdot \frac{|t|^3\,E|Y|^3}{3\,n^{3/2}} = \frac{|t|^3\,E|Y|^3}{3\sqrt n} \to 0. \] Finally \(b_n^n = (1 - t^2/(2n))^n \to e^{-t^2/2}\). For \(t = 0\) this is trivial, so let \(t \ne 0\), and write \(b_n^n = \exp(n\ln(1 - x/n))\) with \(x = t^2/2\). By Assumption 1, \(\ln(1 + u)/u \to 1\) as \(u \to 0\), so we get \[n\ln(1 - x/n) = -x\cdot \frac{\ln(1 - x/n)}{-x/n} \to -x\]. The bound above gives \(a_n^n - b_n^n \to 0\), because \(|a_n^n - b_n^n| \le n|a_n - b_n| \to 0\). Then \(n\ln(1 - x/n) \to -x\) by the limit just shown, and the continuity of exp (Assumption 1) gives \(b_n^n = \exp(n\ln(1 - x/n)) \to e^{-t^2/2}\). Therefore \(\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = a_n^n \to e^{-t^2/2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.3(b) is the characteristic-function limit. The conclusion about distribution functions is not established on this page.

Definition 2.4 (central limit theorem)

The central limit theorem holds for \(X\) when \(P(Z_n \le z) \to \Phi(z)\) for every real \(z\), where \(\Phi\) is the distribution function of the standard normal law of Lesson 6.

The status of the distribution-function limit is therefore: the characteristic-function limit is proved, and the distribution-function limit is not established on this page. Lab 1 compares \(P(Z_n \le 1)\) with \(\Phi(1)\) for a fair die, and it does not establish that limit.

Lab 1 · A fair die: exact values, a running mean, and a seeded standardized mean

Predicted before running, for the fair die with \(\mu = \tfrac72\) and \(\sigma^2 = \tfrac{35}{12}\). Part A should print the exact values of \(P(Z_n \le 1)\) for \(n = 1, 5, 10, 20, 40\), each with its gap to \(\Phi(1) = 0.841345\). Part B should print the running sample mean of one seeded path of 10000 dice at \[n = 10, 100, 1000, 10000\], with the Chebyshev bound \(\sigma^2/(n \cdot 0.5^2)\) on \(P(|\bar X_n - 3.5| \ge 0.5)\). Part C should print, for \(n = 5, 30, 200\), a simulated estimate of \(P(Z_n \le 1)\) from 4000 seeded samples, with its standard error and gap to \(\Phi(1)\). The exact gaps in Part A should be small for these sizes, and the simulated estimates are random.

"""M4 lab L09: a fair die, its running sample mean, and the standardized sample mean against Phi(1).

SIMULATED: the draws come from seeded generators; no data is used.
Standard library only (fractions, math, random, statistics). The law is a fair die: values 1 to 6, each with
probability 1/6, so mu = 7/2 and sigma^2 = 35/12. Part A computes P(S_n <= 3.5 n + sqrt(35 n / 12)) exactly for
the sum S_n of n independent dice. It convolves the exact probability mass function of the sum, with Fraction
arithmetic, and takes the threshold to the largest integer not above it (math.floor). The exact values are
compared with Phi(1), the standard normal distribution function, from statistics.NormalDist. Part B draws one
seeded path of 10000 dice (seed 2026) and prints its running sample mean at n = 10, 100, 1000 and 10000, with the
Chebyshev bound sigma^2 / (n eps^2) for eps = 0.5. Part C draws 4000 seeded samples (seed 2026) for each size
n = 5, 30 and 200, estimates P(Z_n <= 1) for the standardized sample mean Z_n = (Xbar_n - mu) / (sigma / sqrt(n)),
and prints the gap to Phi(1) with the Monte Carlo standard error of the estimate. A sample is one random run.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
import statistics
from fractions import Fraction

FACES = range(1, 7)
MU = 3.5
SIGMA2 = 35 / 12
SIGMA = math.sqrt(SIGMA2)
SEED = 2026
EXACT_SIZES = (1, 5, 10, 20, 40)
PATH_LENGTH = 10000
PATH_CHECKPOINTS = (10, 100, 1000, 10000)
REPLICATIONS = 4000
MC_SIZES = (5, 30, 200)


def die_sum_mass(n: int) -> list[Fraction]:
    """Exact probability mass function of S_n, the sum of n independent fair dice, indexed by the sum."""
    mass = [Fraction(1)]
    for _ in range(n):
        new = [Fraction(0)] * (len(mass) + 6)
        for total, p in enumerate(mass):
            if p:
                for face in FACES:
                    new[total + face] += p / 6
        mass = new
    return mass


def exact_cdf_at(mass: list[Fraction], m: int) -> Fraction:
    """P(S_n <= m) from the exact mass function; m may be below 0 or above the largest sum."""
    if m < 0:
        return Fraction(0)
    return sum(mass[: m + 1], Fraction(0))


def standardized_threshold(n: int) -> int:
    """The largest integer not above 3.5 n + sqrt(35 n / 12), so that S_n <= threshold iff Z_n <= 1."""
    return math.floor(MU * n + math.sqrt(SIGMA2 * n))


def main() -> None:
    phi1 = statistics.NormalDist().cdf(1.0)
    print(f"part A: Phi(1) = {phi1:.6f}")
    for n in EXACT_SIZES:
        mass = die_sum_mass(n)
        if sum(mass) != 1:
            raise AssertionError("the mass function does not sum to 1")
        m = standardized_threshold(n)
        exact = exact_cdf_at(mass, m)
        print(
            f"part A: n = {n:2d}, P(S_n <= {m:3d}) = P(Z_n <= 1) exactly = {float(exact):.6f}, "
            f"gap to Phi(1) = {float(exact) - phi1:+.6f}"
        )

    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    path = [rng.randint(1, 6) for _ in range(PATH_LENGTH)]
    bound_eps = 0.5
    for n in PATH_CHECKPOINTS:
        running = sum(path[:n]) / n
        bound = SIGMA2 / (n * bound_eps**2)
        print(
            f"part B: n = {n:5d}, running sample mean = {running:.4f}, gap to 3.5 = {running - MU:+.4f}, "
            f"Chebyshev bound on P(|Xbar_n - 3.5| >= 0.5) = {bound:.4f}"
        )

    for n in MC_SIZES:
        rng = random.Random(SEED)
        hits = 0
        for _ in range(REPLICATIONS):
            total = sum(rng.randint(1, 6) for _ in range(n))
            z = (total / n - MU) / (SIGMA / math.sqrt(n))
            if z <= 1.0:
                hits += 1
        estimate = hits / REPLICATIONS
        standard_error = math.sqrt(estimate * (1 - estimate) / REPLICATIONS)
        print(
            f"part C: n = {n:3d}, estimated P(Z_n <= 1) = {estimate:.4f} (standard error {standard_error:.4f}), "
            f"gap to Phi(1) = {estimate - phi1:+.4f}"
        )

3Where the canon breaks

  • The variance is used. Theorem 1.3 and Proposition 2.3 use \(\sigma^2 < \infty\): Chebyshev's inequality needs a finite variance, and the normalization divides by \(\sigma\). A law with infinite variance is outside both results, and Lesson 10 treats such a law.
  • A finite-valued sample mean is not normal. For each \(n\), \(\bar X_n\) takes finitely many values, so its distribution function jumps. A normal law has a density (Lesson 6), so its distribution function has no jumps. Therefore no \(\bar X_n\) has a normal law, and the central limit theorem is a statement about a limit of laws, not about each \(n\).
  • Chebyshev's bound can exceed 1. Theorem 1.3 bounds a probability by \(\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)\). The number exceeds 1 whenever \(n\varepsilon^2 < \sigma^2\), and the bound says nothing in that case. Lab 1 prints 1.1667 at \(n = 10\) for \(\varepsilon = 0.5\).

4Misconceptions

  1. “The law of large numbers says the sample mean is close to \(\mu\) at every \(n\).”

    Theorem 1.3 bounds the probability of a large deviation at each \(n\), and that bound tends to 0 as \(n\) grows. Lab 1, Part B, shows a path whose running mean is 0.30 above 3.5 at \(n = 10\) and 0.31 above at \(n = 100\). Theorem 1.3 bounds a probability at each \(n\), and the law of large numbers is the limit of those probabilities. Neither says that one path stays near \(\mu\).

  2. “The central limit theorem means the sample mean is normally distributed for large \(n\).”

    The theorem concerns the standardized mean \(Z_n\) and its limit (Definition 2.4). Section 3 shows that \(\bar X_n\) has no normal law for any finite \(n\) when the law is finitely valued. The approximation is a statement about probabilities at each fixed \(z\) as \(n\) increases, and Lab 1 prints gaps from \(\Phi(1)\) of at most 0.0139 in absolute value at the sizes it prints, for the die. Those gaps come from one exact computation (Part A, at \(z = 1\) only) and from seeded simulations (Part C), and they say nothing about other \(z\).

  3. “The central limit theorem needs the variables to be normal.”

    The die of Lab 1 is not normal, since it takes six values. The statement needs i.i.d. copies with finite positive variance, and normality of \(X\) is not one of its hypotheses. Proposition 2.3(b) gives convergence of the characteristic functions to \(e^{-t^2/2}\), and it assumes nothing about \(X\) being normal. The step from there to distribution functions is not established on this page (Section 2).

Mechanics

M1 · The Chebyshev bound for the die at \(n = 100\)

For the fair die, with \(\sigma^2 = 35/12\), find the Chebyshev bound on \(P(|\bar X_{100} - 3.5| \ge 0.5)\).

Solution

By Theorem 1.3 the bound is \[\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2) = (35/12)/(100 \cdot 0.25) = (35/12)/25 = 35/300 = 7/60\], which is about 0.1167. This is the value that Lab 1 prints at \(n = 100\), to four decimals, as 0.1167.

M2 · The variance of the sample mean of four dice

For four independent fair dice, find the variance of their sample mean.

Solution

By Proposition 1.2 with \(n = 4\) and \(\sigma^2 = 35/12\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_4) = (35/12)/4 = 35/48\). This is about 0.729.

M3 · A two-value law at \(n = 2\)

Let \(X\) take the values 0 and 2 with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, and let \(X_1, X_2\) be i.i.d. copies. Compute \(P(|\bar X_2 - 1| \ge \tfrac12)\) exactly, and compare it with the bound of Theorem 1.3.

Solution

Here \(\mu = 1\) and \(\sigma^2 = 1\). The sample mean \(\bar X_2\) takes the value 0 with probability \(\tfrac14\), 1 with probability \(\tfrac12\), and 2 with probability \(\tfrac14\). The event \(|\bar X_2 - 1| \ge \tfrac12\) is \(\bar X_2 \in \{0, 2\}\), with probability \(\tfrac12\). The bound is \(\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2) = 1/(2 \cdot \tfrac14) = 2\), which is greater than 1 and so says nothing here, while the exact probability is \(\tfrac12\).

P1 · Chebyshev's inequality on a finite law

Prove Proposition 1.1 without citing any other result of this lesson.

Solution

Let \(W = (Y - m)^2 \ge 0\). For each outcome, \(a^2\mathbf{1}\{|Y - m| \ge a\} \le W\): if \(|Y - m| \ge a\), then \(W = (Y-m)^2 \ge a^2\), and otherwise the left side is 0 and \(W \ge 0\). Taking expectations, which preserves inequalities between finite sums, gives \[a^2 P(|Y - m| \ge a) \le E[W] = v\]. Dividing by \(a^2 > 0\) gives the bound.

P2 · The product rule for two independent variables

Prove \(E[g(X)h(Y)] = E[g(X)]E[h(Y)]\) for independent \(X\) and \(Y\) with finitely many values, and complex-valued \(g\) and \(h\).

Solution

Independence means \[P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x)P(Y = y)\]. Then \[E[g(X)h(Y)] = \sum_{x,y} g(x)h(y)P(X=x)P(Y=y) = \bigl(\sum_x g(x)P(X=x)\bigr)\bigl(\sum_y h(y)P(Y=y)\bigr)\], by the distributive law, which is the product of the two expectations.

P3 · The telescoping bound

For complex numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(|a|, |b| \le 1\), show that \(|a^n - b^n| \le n|a - b|\).

Solution

The identity \[a^n - b^n = (a - b)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a^k b^{n-1-k}\] holds by expanding the sum, which telescopes. Each term has modulus at most 1, since \(|a^k| \le 1\) and \(|b^{n-1-k}| \le 1\). Taking moduli, \[|a^n - b^n| \le |a - b|\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} 1 = n|a - b|\].

Empirical

E1 · The exact value at \(n = 10\) (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints \(P(Z_{10} \le 1) = 0.843496\) exactly, from \(P(S_{10} \le 40)\). Verify the threshold 40 and the gap from \(\Phi(1) = 0.841345\).

Solution

\(Z_{10} \le 1\) if and only if \[S_{10} \le 10 \cdot 3.5 + \sqrt{35 \cdot 10/12} = 35 + \sqrt{29.1667} = 35 + 5.4006 = 40.4006\]. Since \(S_{10}\) takes integers, the event is \(S_{10} \le 40\), which the lab's floor computes. The gap is \[0.843496 - 0.841345 = 0.002151\], which the lab prints as \(+0.002151\).

E2 · The standard error of a simulated estimate (Lab 1)

Lab 1, Part C, prints an estimate 0.8552 for \(n = 5\) with standard error 0.0056. Verify the standard error, and state what it does and does not say about the gap to \(\Phi(1)\).

Solution

The lab's standard error is \(\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/4000}\), computed here from the printed 4-decimal estimate \(\hat p = 0.8552\). That is \[\sqrt{0.8552 \cdot 0.1448/4000} = \sqrt{3.096 \times 10^{-5}} = 0.00556\], printed as 0.0056. The gap of \[0.8552 - 0.841345 = 0.0139\] is \(0.0139 / 0.0056 = 2.48\) printed standard errors, which is 2.5 to one decimal (a ratio of printed values). The unrounded estimate of this run is \(\hat p = 3421/4000 = 0.85525\), printed as 0.8552, so the gap is \(0.85525 - 0.841345 = 0.013905\) and the standard error is \(0.005563\), and the ratio is \(2.50\). This is a statement about this run only. The standard error does not cover the choice of seed, and the three sizes share one seed, so the three gaps are not independent evidence.

E3 · The Chebyshev bound at \(n = 10000\) (Lab 1)

Lab 1, Part B, prints the bound 0.0012 at \(n = 10000\). Compute it, and say what the bound does not establish about the running mean printed at that \(n\).

Solution

The bound is \[(35/12)/(10000 \cdot 0.25) = 2.9167/2500 = 0.0011667\], printed as 0.0012. It bounds the probability that the sample mean lies 0.5 or more from 3.5, and it says nothing about the specific printed mean 3.4689, which is 0.0311 from 3.5. The lab's path is one realization, and the bound is a bound on a probability, not on a path.

Research

R1 · Second moments are not enough

Proposition 2.3(a) gives \(E[Z_n^2] = 1\) for every \(n\). Explain why this does not establish the central limit theorem, and identify which step of Proposition 2.3(b) uses more than the second moment.

Solution

A statement about \(E[Z_n^2]\) fixes the scale of \(Z_n\) for each \(n\), but it does not determine the law of \(Z_n\), so it cannot give \(P(Z_n \le z) \to \Phi(z)\) for every \(z\). Two laws with the same variance can have different probabilities of exceeding a given level. The step that uses more than the second moment is the expansion of Proposition 2.2, which uses the absolute third moment \(E|Y|^3\), and the limit that follows from it. In the expansion, the second moment enters through the coefficient \(-s^2/2\) and the normalization \(E[Y^2] = 1\) of Proposition 2.2.

R2 · Why the distribution-function limit is not established here

Explain why Proposition 2.3(b) does not by itself give the distribution-function limit of Definition 2.4, and which tools this lesson does not state.

Solution

Every law of \(Z_n\) is finitely valued, so the distribution function of \(Z_n\) is a step function, while the target \(\Phi\) is continuous (Lesson 6, by its density). The lesson proves a limit of characteristic functions only. It states no result that converts such a limit into a limit of distribution functions, and it does not identify \(e^{-t^2/2}\) with a characteristic function of a normal law.

R3 · The limit in Proposition 2.3(b)

Assume Assumption 1. Deduce \((1 - x/n)^n \to e^{-x}\) for fixed real \(x \ne 0\) (the case \(x = 0\) is trivial), and say which step uses the assumption.

Solution

Assumption 1 gives \(\ln(1 + u)/u \to 1\) as \(u \to 0\), and it is not derived here. For fixed \(x\), \(u = -x/n \to 0\), and \(-x/u = n\), so \[n\ln(1 - x/n) = -x \cdot \ln(1 + u)/u \to -x\], a product of two limits. The continuity of \(\exp\), the other part of Assumption 1, then gives \[(1 - x/n)^n = \exp(n\ln(1 - x/n)) \to e^{-x}\]. Both steps use the assumption.
