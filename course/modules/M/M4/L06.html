Normal and Student-t distributions

This lesson defines two laws that the rest of M4 uses, both built from the density \(e^{-z^{2}/2}\). It proves under Assumption 1 that the first density integrates to one and that its two parameters are its mean and variance, and it derives the variance of the second law. That variance is \(\nu/(\nu - 2)\) for \(\nu > 2\), where \(\nu\) is a positive integer that labels the law, and for \(\nu \le 2\) the second moment is infinite (Proposition 2.6). Lab 1 compares sample variances of draws from the second law with that formula. Lesson L10 returns to sample means in the infinite-variance case.

1Density, normalization and standardization

The random variables in this lesson have densities, so their probabilities are integrals rather than finite sums. Expectations are integrals of the variable against its density, using the definite integral of M2. The linearity of the definite integral gives the same linearity of expectation that L02 proved for finite sums. Results from L04 and L05 that used finitely many values are restated where they are used.

Assumption 1 (calculus and measure facts used without proof)

The page uses the following standard facts without proof. (a) The substitution rule, integration by parts, the fundamental theorem of calculus for improper integrals of continuous functions over the real line, and the derivative of \(e^{u}\), which gives \(\varphi'(z) = -z\varphi(z)\). (b) Polar coordinates in the plane, with Jacobian \(r\). (c) Tonelli's theorem for nonnegative integrands, which lets a double integral equal an iterated integral. (d) For independent variables X and Y, the joint law of the pair is the product of their two laws. Functions of independent variables are independent: if X and Y are independent, then f(X) and g(Y) are independent for the functions f and g that the page applies, and a function of a finite family of independent variables is independent of any variable that is independent of the whole family. Each result that uses Assumption 1 says so in its statement.

Definition 1.1 (normal distribution)

For a real \(\mu\) and \(\sigma > 0\), the normal distribution \(N(\mu, \sigma^{2})\) is the law with density \(f(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\Big(-\dfrac{(x - \mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)\) on the real line. The standard normal law is \(N(0,1)\), with density \(\varphi(z) = e^{-z^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\). A variable Z with this law is a standard normal variable.

Proposition 1.2 (the Gaussian integral)

Assuming Assumption 1, the integral \(I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^{2}/2}\,dx\) converges and equals \(\sqrt{2\pi}\).

Proof

The integrand is positive and is at most \(e^{-|x|}\) for \(|x| \ge 2\), since \(x^{2}/2 \ge |x|\) there. So the integral converges. Let \(J = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-(x^{2} + y^{2})/2}\,dx\,dy\) over the plane. Since the integrand is nonnegative, Tonelli's theorem (Assumption 1(c)) lets the double integral equal \(I^{2}\), because \(e^{-(x^{2}+y^{2})/2} = e^{-x^{2}/2}e^{-y^{2}/2}\). Change to polar coordinates (Assumption 1(b)) \(x = r\cos\theta\), \(y = r\sin\theta\), with Jacobian r and \(r \ge 0\), \(\theta \in [0, 2\pi)\): \[ I^{2} = \int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-r^{2}/2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta = 2\pi\Big[-e^{-r^{2}/2}\Big]_{0}^{\infty} = 2\pi. \] Since \(I > 0\), \(I = \sqrt{2\pi}\).

Proposition 1.3 (the normal density integrates to one)

Assuming Assumption 1, for \(\sigma > 0\) and real \(\mu\), the function f of Definition 1.1 is a probability density: it is nonnegative and \(\int f = 1\). In particular \(\varphi\) is a density.

Proof

Substitute \(x = \mu + \sigma u\), so \(dx = \sigma\,du\). Then \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-u^{2}/2}\,\sigma\,du = \frac{I}{\sqrt{2\pi}} = 1\) by Proposition 1.2.

Proposition 1.4 (standardization)

Assuming Assumption 1, let \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^{2})\) with \(\sigma > 0\). Then \(Z = (X - \mu)/\sigma\) has the standard normal law \(N(0,1)\). Conversely, if Z is standard normal, then \(\mu + \sigma Z \sim N(\mu, \sigma^{2})\).

Proof

For real z, \(P(Z \le z) = P(X \le \mu + \sigma z) = \int_{-\infty}^{\mu + \sigma z} f(x)\,dx\). Substituting \(x = \mu + \sigma u\) as in Proposition 1.3 gives \(\int_{-\infty}^{z} \varphi(u)\,du\), which is the distribution function of N(0,1). The converse follows by reversing the substitution.

Proposition 1.5 (moments of the standard normal)

Assuming Assumption 1, if Z is standard normal, then \(E[Z] = 0\) and \(E[Z^{2}] = 1\), so \(\operatorname{Var}(Z) = 1\). Consequently, if \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^{2})\), then \(E[X] = \mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \sigma^{2}\).

Proof

The integrand \(z\varphi(z)\) is odd, and \(|z|\varphi(z)\) is integrable. Write \(|z|\varphi(z) = (|z|e^{-z^{2}/4})\,e^{-z^{2}/4}/\sqrt{2\pi}\). For \(z \ge 0\), the derivative of \(z e^{-z^{2}/4}\) is \((1 - z^{2}/2)e^{-z^{2}/4}\), which is positive on \([0, \sqrt{2})\) and negative after \(\sqrt{2}\), so the maximum of \(|z|e^{-z^{2}/4}\) is \(\sqrt{2}\,e^{-1/2} < 0.86\). Since \(1/\sqrt{2\pi} < 1\), \(|z|\varphi(z) \le 0.86\,e^{-z^{2}/4}\). The function \(e^{-z^{2}/4}\) is integrable on the line by the substitution \(z = \sqrt{2}\,x\) and Proposition 1.2. So \(\int z\varphi(z)\,dz = 0\), because the integral over \((-\infty, 0]\) is minus the integral over \([0, \infty)\). For the second moment, note \(\varphi'(z) = -z\varphi(z)\). Integrating by parts with \(u = z\) and \(dv = z\varphi(z)\,dz\), so \(v = -\varphi(z)\), \[ \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} z^{2}\varphi(z)\,dz = \Big[-z\varphi(z)\Big]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi(z)\,dz = 0 + 1 = 1. \] The boundary term vanishes because \(z\varphi(z) \to 0\) at both ends, and the last integral is 1 by Proposition 1.3. So \(\operatorname{Var}(Z) = E[Z^{2}] - (E[Z])^{2} = 1\). For \(X = \mu + \sigma Z\), which has the law \(N(\mu, \sigma^{2})\) by Proposition 1.4, linearity gives \(E[X] = \mu + \sigma E[Z] = \mu\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = E[(\sigma Z)^{2}] = \sigma^{2}E[Z^{2}] = \sigma^{2}\), because the constant \(\sigma^{2}\) comes out of the integral by linearity.

2Sums of squared normals and their ratio

Definition 2.1 (chi-square variable, defined by its sum)

For a positive integer \(\nu\), a chi-square variable with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom is \(W_{\nu} = Z_{1}^{2} + \dots + Z_{\nu}^{2}\), where \(Z_{1}, \dots, Z_{\nu}\) are independent standard normal variables. Its law is fixed by this sum. The page uses the sum as the definition.

Remark 2.2 (independence for variables with densities)

Variables X and Y are independent when \(P(X \in A,\ Y \in B) = P(X \in A)\,P(Y \in B)\) for all sets A and B. Assuming Assumption 1(d), two consequences are used below. For independent variables and functions g and h that are nonnegative, \(E[g(X)h(Y)] = E[g(X)]\,E[h(Y)]\). This is Tonelli's theorem (Assumption 1(c)) applied to the product of the two laws. Functions of independent variables are independent (Assumption 1(d)). The page uses both facts in the proofs below.

Lemma 2.3 (a Laplace transform of a squared normal)

Assuming Assumption 1, for \(s \ge 0\), \(E[e^{-sZ^{2}}] = (1 + 2s)^{-1/2}\) for a standard normal Z. For a chi-square variable W with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, \(W > 0\) with probability one, and \(E[1/W] = \int_{0}^{\infty} (1 + 2s)^{-\nu/2}\,ds\), which is a value in \((0, \infty]\).

Proof

First, \(E[e^{-sZ^{2}}] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-sz^{2}} e^{-z^{2}/2}\,dz = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-(1 + 2s)z^{2}/2}\,dz\). Substitute \(u = \sqrt{1 + 2s}\,z\), so \(dz = du/\sqrt{1 + 2s}\). The integral is \((1 + 2s)^{-1/2}\) times \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-u^{2}/2}\,du = 1\), by Proposition 1.2 and Proposition 1.3. Second, W is zero only if every \(Z_i\) is zero, and \(P(Z_1 = 0) = 0\) because a single point has zero length under a density. So \(W > 0\) with probability one. For \(w > 0\), \(1/w = \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-sw}\,ds\). Substitute \(w = W\) and use Tonelli's theorem, since the integrand is nonnegative: \[ E[1/W] = \int_{0}^{\infty} E[e^{-sW}]\,ds. \] By independence (Remark 2.2), \(E[e^{-sW}] = \prod_{i=1}^{\nu} E[e^{-sZ_i^{2}}] = (1 + 2s)^{-\nu/2}\), using the first step for each \(Z_i\). This gives the formula.

Proposition 2.4 (the mean of the reciprocal of a chi-square)

Assuming Assumption 1 and Lemma 2.3, let W be chi-square with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, a positive integer. If \(\nu > 2\), then \(E[1/W] = 1/(\nu - 2)\). If \(\nu \le 2\), then \(E[1/W] = \infty\).

Proof

Let \(a = \nu/2\). By Lemma 2.3, \(E[1/W] = \int_{0}^{\infty} (1 + 2s)^{-a}\,ds\). Suppose \(a > 1\). An antiderivative of \((1 + 2s)^{-a}\) is \((1 + 2s)^{1-a}/(2(1 - a))\), which tends to 0 as \(s \to \infty\), because \(1 - a < 0\). So the integral is \(0 - 1/(2(1 - a)) = 1/(2(a - 1)) = 1/(\nu - 2)\). Now suppose \(a \le 1\). For \(s \ge 0\), \(1 + 2s \ge 1\) and \(-a \ge -1\), so \((1 + 2s)^{-a} \ge (1 + 2s)^{-1}\). The integral of \((1 + 2s)^{-1}\) over \([0, \infty)\) is infinite, because its antiderivative \(\tfrac12\ln(1 + 2s)\) tends to infinity. By comparison the integral is infinite.

Definition 2.5 (Student-t variable)

For a positive integer \(\nu\), the Student-t distribution with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom is the law of \(\mathrm{t}_{\nu} = Z / \sqrt{W_{\nu}/\nu}\), where Z is standard normal and \(W_{\nu}\) is a chi-square variable with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom, independent of Z. The page takes \(\nu\) to be a positive integer, as Lab 1 does. It does not define the Student-t for non-integer \(\nu\).

Proposition 2.6 (mean and variance of the Student-t)

Assuming Assumption 1, let \(\mathrm{t} = \mathrm{t}_{\nu}\) with \(\nu\) a positive integer. If \(\nu > 2\), then \(E[\mathrm{t}] = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{t}) = \nu/(\nu - 2)\). If \(\nu \le 2\), then \(E[\mathrm{t}^{2}] = \infty\). In either case the law of \(\mathrm{t}\) is symmetric about 0.

Proof

Let \(V = (\nu/W)^{1/2}\), a function of W, so that \(\mathrm{t} = ZV\) and Z and V are independent (Remark 2.2). Since \(E[Z^{2}] = 1\) (Proposition 1.5) and the two factors are independent and nonnegative in \(Z^{2}V^{2}\), Remark 2.2 gives \[ E[\mathrm{t}^{2}] = E[Z^{2}]\,E[V^{2}] = \nu\,E[1/W]. \] If \(\nu > 2\), Proposition 2.4 gives \(E[\mathrm{t}^{2}] = \nu/(\nu - 2)\), which is finite. If \(\nu \le 2\), Proposition 2.4 gives \(E[\mathrm{t}^{2}] = \infty\). For the mean when \(\nu > 2\): \(E[V] \le (1 + E[V^{2}])/2 < \infty\), because \(v \le (1 + v^{2})/2\) for every real v, and \(E[V^{2}] = \nu E[1/W] < \infty\). So \(E[|Z|]\,E[V] < \infty\), where \(E[|Z|] = \sqrt{2/\pi}\) is finite (Problem R1). Write \(Z = Z^{+} - Z^{-}\) with both parts nonnegative. Remark 2.2 gives \(E[Z^{+}V] = E[Z^{+}]\,E[V]\) and \(E[Z^{-}V] = E[Z^{-}]\,E[V]\), both finite, so \(E[ZV] = E[Z]\,E[V] = 0\), since \(E[Z] = 0\) by Proposition 1.5. Therefore \(E[\mathrm{t}] = 0\), and \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{t}) = E[\mathrm{t}^{2}] - (E[\mathrm{t}])^{2} = \nu/(\nu - 2)\). For symmetry, \(-Z\) has the standard normal law, because \(\varphi\) is even, and \(-Z\) is independent of V, a function of W (Remark 2.2). So \((-Z, V)\) has the same joint law as \((Z, V)\) (Assumption 1(d)), and \(-\mathrm{t} = (-Z)V\) has the same law as \(\mathrm{t}\).

Remark 2.7 (what the formula does and does not say)

Proposition 2.6 gives the variance for \(\nu > 2\) and says that \(E[\mathrm{t}^{2}]\) is infinite for \(\nu \le 2\). It does not say what the sample variance does for \(\nu \le 2\). For \(\nu = 3\), the variance is 3, which is larger than the variance 1 of the standard normal \(N(0,1)\). The Student-t kurtosis, the fourth standardized moment of L03, is not computed on this page.

Lab 1 · A Student-t draw and its variance

The inputs are SIMULATED, not data. Stated before running. Each draw is \(\mathrm{t} = Z/\sqrt{W/\nu}\), where Z is standard normal and W is a chi-square variable with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom (the sum of \(\nu\) squared standard normals), drawn independently of Z. Part A predicts that the sample mean of \(\mathrm{t}\) is near 0, by Proposition 2.6, and that the sample variance is near \(\nu/(\nu - 2)\) for \(\nu = 5\) and \(\nu = 10\). Part B predicts that the sample mean of W is near \(\nu\), and that the sample mean of \(1/W\) is near \(1/(\nu - 2)\), by Proposition 1.5 and Proposition 2.4 respectively. Part C prints the formula for \(\nu = 3\) to 8, and it predicts a ValueError at \(\nu = 2\). The generator is seeded, so the output is fixed.

"""M4 L06 lab 1: a Student-t draw built from a standard normal over an independent chi-square, and its variance.

Inputs are simulated, not data. Every draw uses one standard normal Z and an independent chi-square W with nu
degrees of freedom, where W is the sum of nu squared standard normals. The draw is t = Z / sqrt(W / nu). Part A seeds
random.Random(20260602 + nu) for nu = 5 and nu = 10, and Part B seeds random.Random(20261102). Each draw takes Z
first, then the nu normals of W, in that order.

Part A: for nu = 5 and nu = 10, the sample mean and the sample variance (divisor n - 1) of t over n = 100000
draws, beside nu / (nu - 2) from the formula for the variance of t.
Part B: for nu = 5, the sample mean of W beside nu, and the sample mean of 1 / W beside 1 / (nu - 2).
Part C: the function t_variance(nu) = nu / (nu - 2) for nu = 3 to 8, and the ValueError it raises for nu = 2.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random

SEED = 20260602
N_DRAWS = 100000


def chi_square_and_t(rng: random.Random, nu: int) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """One pair (W, t): W is the sum of nu squared standard normals, and t = Z / sqrt(W / nu) with Z drawn first."""
    z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
    w = sum(rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) ** 2 for _ in range(nu))
    return w, z / (w / nu) ** 0.5


def t_variance(nu: int) -> float:
    """The variance nu / (nu - 2) of a Student-t with nu degrees of freedom. Raises ValueError unless nu > 2."""
    if nu <= 2:
        raise ValueError(f"the variance of a Student-t with nu = {nu} is infinite; the formula needs nu > 2")
    return nu / (nu - 2)


def mean_and_variance(xs: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """The sample mean and the sample variance with divisor n - 1."""
    n = len(xs)
    mean = sum(xs) / n
    variance = sum((x - mean) ** 2 for x in xs) / (n - 1)
    return mean, variance


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A, B and C. The seed is fixed, so every run prints the same lines."""
    print("Part A: sample mean and variance of t against nu / (nu - 2)")
    for nu in (5, 10):
        rng = random.Random(SEED + nu)
        ts: list[float] = []
        for _ in range(N_DRAWS):
            _, t = chi_square_and_t(rng, nu)
            ts.append(t)
        mean, variance = mean_and_variance(ts)
        exact = t_variance(nu)
        print(f"nu {nu} sample_mean {mean:.6f} sample_variance {variance:.6f} formula {exact:.6f}")
        print(f"nu {nu} relative_error {(variance - exact) / exact:.6f}")

    print("Part B: chi-square moments for nu = 5")
    rng = random.Random(SEED + 500)
    ws: list[float] = []
    inv_ws: list[float] = []
    for _ in range(N_DRAWS):
        w, _ = chi_square_and_t(rng, 5)
        ws.append(w)
        inv_ws.append(1.0 / w)
    mean_w = sum(ws) / N_DRAWS
    mean_inv = sum(inv_ws) / N_DRAWS
    print(f"nu 5 sample_mean_W {mean_w:.6f} formula {5:.6f}")
    print(f"nu 5 sample_mean_1_over_W {mean_inv:.6f} formula {1 / (5 - 2):.6f}")

    print("Part C: the variance formula for nu = 3 to 8, and the guard at nu = 2")
    for nu in range(3, 9):
        print(f"nu {nu} t_variance {t_variance(nu):.6f}")
    try:
        t_variance(2)
    except ValueError as error:
        print(f"nu 2 raises ValueError {error}")

3Where the canon breaks

  • The Student-t needs independence. Problem R3 constructs a variable from a dependent denominator \(W = Z^{2}\) and gets variance 1, where the independent construction with \(\nu = 1\) gives \(E[\mathrm{t}^{2}] = \infty\) by Proposition 2.6. The independence in Definition 2.5 is part of the definition, not a detail.
  • The variance formula applies for \(\nu > 2\). Proposition 2.6 gives the variance for \(\nu > 2\). For \(\nu \le 2\), \(E[\mathrm{t}^{2}]\) is infinite and the formula does not apply. Lab 1 Part A compares sample variances with the formula at \(\nu = 5\) and \(\nu = 10\).
  • Mean zero is not unit variance. The Student-t of Definition 2.5 has mean 0, but its variance is \(\nu/(\nu - 2)\), not 1. Dividing a Student-t variable by its sample standard deviation gives a different variable from the one in Definition 2.5.
  • The page does not compute the Student-t moment-generating function (L07, Definition 1.1) or the kurtosis. Lessons 7 to 10 do not compute the Student-t moment-generating function either. For the Student-t(3) law, L10, Proposition 2.3 gives an infinite fourth moment under L10's Assumption 1 (the density of that law), so on that assumption the kurtosis of Lesson 3 is not defined for it.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A Student-t variable has variance 1, because it is standardized.”

    The Student-t of Definition 2.5 has mean 0 but variance \(\nu/(\nu - 2)\). For \(\nu = 5\) this is \(5/3\). Lab 1 Part A prints a sample variance of 1.669114 against the formula 1.666667 at \(\nu = 5\), and 1.240325 against 1.25 at \(\nu = 10\).

  2. “Every Student-t has a finite second moment, as every normal does.”

    Proposition 2.6 gives \(E[\mathrm{t}^{2}] = \infty\) for \(\nu \le 2\). The argument is Proposition 2.4: \(E[1/W]\) diverges for \(\nu \le 2\), and \(E[\mathrm{t}^{2}] = \nu E[1/W]\).

  3. “The normal variance is a free parameter, separate from the mean.”

    The normal has two parameters, and they are its mean and variance. Proposition 1.5 gives \(E[X] = \mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(X) = \sigma^{2}\) for \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^{2})\), so the parameters are fixed by the moments of the law.

Mechanics

M1 · A normal probability and second moment

Let X be \(N(3, 4)\). Find \(P(X \le 3)\) and \(E[X^{2}]\).

Solution

Here \(\sigma = 2\), so \(P(X \le 3) = P(Z \le (3 - 3)/2) = P(Z \le 0)\). Since \(\varphi\) is symmetric about 0 and integrates to 1, \(P(Z \le 0) = 1/2\). For the second moment, \(E[X^{2}] = \operatorname{Var}(X) + (E[X])^{2} = 4 + 9 = 13\), using Proposition 1.5.

M2 · Student-t variances for small degrees of freedom

Find \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{t}_{\nu})\) for \(\nu = 3, 4, 5\), and say which of these is the smallest.

Solution

By Proposition 2.6, \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{t}_{\nu}) = \nu/(\nu - 2)\). For \(\nu = 3\) this is \(3/1 = 3\), for \(\nu = 4\) it is \(4/2 = 2\), and for \(\nu = 5\) it is \(5/3\). The smallest is \(5/3\), at \(\nu = 5\), so the three values fall as \(\nu\) grows.

M3 · Standardizing a normal

Let X be \(N(-1, 9)\). Find the standard normal value z with \(P(X \le 2) = P(Z \le z)\).

Solution

\(\sigma = 3\), so \(z = (2 - (-1))/3 = 3/3 = 1\). By Proposition 1.4, \(P(X \le 2) = P(Z \le 1)\), which is the standard normal distribution function at 1.

Proof

P1 · The Laplace transform of a squared normal

Prove Lemma 2.3's first step: for \(s \ge 0\), \(E[e^{-sZ^{2}}] = (1 + 2s)^{-1/2}\).

Solution

Write the expectation as \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-(1 + 2s)z^{2}/2}\,dz\). Substitute \(u = \sqrt{1 + 2s}\,z\), so \(dz = du/\sqrt{1 + 2s}\). The integral becomes \((1 + 2s)^{-1/2}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-u^{2}/2}\,du\). By Proposition 1.2 the last integral is \(\sqrt{2\pi}\), so the factor is 1, and the result is \((1 + 2s)^{-1/2}\).

P2 · The divergence at \(\nu = 2\)

Show directly that \(\int_0^{\infty}(1 + 2s)^{-1}\,ds = \infty\), and deduce that \(E[1/W_2] = \infty\).

Solution

An antiderivative of \((1 + 2s)^{-1}\) is \(\tfrac12\ln(1 + 2s)\). As \(s \to \infty\) this tends to infinity, so the integral is infinite. For \(\nu = 2\), \(a = \nu/2 = 1\), and Lemma 2.3 gives \(E[1/W_2] = \int_0^{\infty}(1 + 2s)^{-1}\,ds = \infty\). This is the case \(a \le 1\) of Proposition 2.4.

P3 · The second moment of the standard normal

Prove \(E[Z^{2}] = 1\) for a standard normal Z by integration by parts, stating the boundary term and why it vanishes.

Solution

With \(u = z\) and \(dv = z\varphi(z)\,dz\), \(v = -\varphi(z)\) since \(\varphi' = -z\varphi\). The integral is \([-z\varphi(z)]_{-\infty}^{\infty} + \int\varphi(z)\,dz\). The boundary term is zero because \(z\varphi(z) = z e^{-z^{2}/2}/\sqrt{2\pi} \to 0\) as \(|z| \to \infty\). The remaining integral is 1 by Proposition 1.3. So \(E[Z^{2}] = 1\).

Empirical

E1 · The relative error at \(\nu = 5\)

Read Part A of Lab 1. The printed relative error at \(\nu = 5\) is 0.001469. Recompute it from the printed sample variance 1.669114 and formula 1.666667, and explain any difference.

Solution

From the printed six-place values, \((1.669114 - 1.666667)/1.666667 = 0.002447/1.666667 = 0.001468\). The lab's 0.001469 is computed from the unrounded values, and the printed values are shown to six places. The gap of one unit in the last digit comes from that printing step. The lab's figure is the one to quote.

E2 · Which degrees of freedom is further from its formula

Read Part A of Lab 1. Is the relative error at \(\nu = 5\) or at \(\nu = 10\) larger in absolute value?

Solution

The printed relative errors are 0.001469 at \(\nu = 5\) and \(-0.007740\) at \(\nu = 10\). Research

R1 · The mean of the absolute normal

Show that \(E[|Z|] = \sqrt{2/\pi}\) for a standard normal Z.

Solution

The integrand \(|z|\varphi(z)\) is even, so \(E[|Z|] = 2\int_0^{\infty} z\,e^{-z^{2}/2}\,dz/\sqrt{2\pi}\). The antiderivative of \(z e^{-z^{2}/2}\) is \(-e^{-z^{2}/2}\), so the integral from 0 to infinity is \(0 - (-1) = 1\). Hence \(E[|Z|] = 2/\sqrt{2\pi} = \sqrt{2/\pi}\), which is finite. This is the finiteness used in the mean step of Proposition 2.6.

R2 · Why the denominator is centred at one

Show that \(E[W_{\nu}/\nu] = 1\), and explain why this makes \(\sqrt{W_{\nu}/\nu}\) a natural scale for the Student-t.

Solution

Each \(E[Z_i^{2}] = 1\) by Proposition 1.5, and expectation is linear over the \(\nu\) terms, so \(E[W_{\nu}] = \nu\). Then \(E[W_{\nu}/\nu] = 1\). So the denominator \(W_{\nu}/\nu\) has mean one. The page uses this to fix the scale of the construction. The variance formula of Proposition 2.6 comes from \(E[1/W_{\nu}]\), not from this mean.

R3 · A dependent denominator changes the answer

Let \(W = Z^{2}\), which is not independent of Z, and let \(\mathrm{t}' = Z/\sqrt{W}\). Show that \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{t}') = 1\), and say why this does not contradict Proposition 2.6.

Solution

Here \(\mathrm{t}' = Z/|Z| = \operatorname{sign}(Z)\), which is \(\pm 1\) with \(P(Z = 0) = 0\). So \(E[\mathrm{t}'^{2}] = 1\), and \(E[\mathrm{t}'] = 0\) by symmetry of Z. Hence \(\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{t}') = 1\). Proposition 2.6 needs the denominator to be chi-square with \(\nu\) degrees of freedom and independent of Z. Here W is chi-square with \(\nu = 1\) in its law, but it is not independent of Z, and the independence step fails. So the two results do not conflict.
