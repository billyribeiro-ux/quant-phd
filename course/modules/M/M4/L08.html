A vector of returns has a mean vector and a covariance matrix, and a model of returns needs both. This lesson constructs the multivariate normal family as linear images of independent standard normals. It shows that the mean and covariance of such an image are the vector and the matrix that define it, that the factor of the covariance is not unique, and that a singular covariance matrix has no inverse (Section 2). Lab 1 compares a seeded sample covariance matrix (defined in Section 3) with the target matrix.

1Standard normal vectors and their linear images

Throughout this lesson, a standard normal vector of length \(k\) is a vector \[Z = (Z_1, \ldots, Z_k)\] whose coordinates are independent, and each \(Z_i\) has the standard normal law of Lesson 6, with \(E[Z_i] = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Z_i) = 1\). Vectors are columns, \(A^{\top}\) is the transpose of a matrix \(A\), and the entries of a matrix are indexed by row and column. Propositions 1.1 and 1.3 rest on Lesson 6's Assumption 1: they use the product rule of Lesson 6, Remark 2.2 and the facts \(E[Z_i] = 0\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(Z_i) = 1\) of Lesson 6, Proposition 1.5, and each states that assumption.

Proposition 1.1 (second moments of a standard normal vector)

Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1, let \(Z\) be a standard normal vector of length \(k\). Then \(E[Z_i Z_j] = 1\) if \(i = j\) and \(E[Z_i Z_j] = 0\) if \(i \ne j\). In matrix form, \(E[ZZ^{\top}] = I_k\), the identity matrix.

Proof

If \(i = j\), then \[E[Z_i Z_j] = E[Z_i^2] = \operatorname{Var}(Z_i) + (E[Z_i])^2 = 1 + 0 = 1\]. If \(i \ne j\), the coordinates \(Z_i\) and \(Z_j\) are independent, so \[E[Z_i Z_j] = E[Z_i]\,E[Z_j] = 0\]. The product rule for independent variables is the factorization of Lesson 4, which Lesson 6, Remark 2.2 states for nonnegative functions. Write \(Z_i = Z_i^{+} - Z_i^{-}\) and \(Z_j = Z_j^{+} - Z_j^{-}\) with nonnegative parts. Each part has a finite expectation, because \(|z| \le (1 + z^2)/2\) and \(E[Z_i^2] = 1\). Applying the rule to each of the four products of parts gives \(E[Z_i Z_j] = E[Z_i]\,E[Z_j]\), which is 0. The matrix identity follows because entry \((i, j)\) of \(ZZ^{\top}\) is \(Z_i Z_j\). \(\blacksquare\)

Definition 1.2 (multivariate normal)

Let \(\mu\) be a vector of length \(d\), let \(\Sigma\) be a symmetric \(d \times d\) matrix, and let \(Z\) be a standard normal vector of length \(k\). A random vector \(X\) of length \(d\) is multivariate normal with mean \(\mu\) and covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) when \(X\) has the same law as \(\mu + AZ\) for some \(d \times k\) matrix \(A\) with \[AA^{\top} = \Sigma\]. The matrix \(A\) is called a factor of \(\Sigma\). This lesson gives an explicit factor for one example, in Example 1.5. It does not prove that every symmetric nonnegative \(\Sigma\) has a factor, and no result below needs that fact.

Proposition 1.3 (mean and covariance of a linear image)

Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1, let \(X = \mu + AZ\), where \(Z\) is a standard normal vector of length \(k\), \(\mu\) is a constant vector of length \(d\), and \(A\) is a constant \(d \times k\) matrix. Then \(E[X] = \mu\), and the covariance matrix of \(X\), whose entry \((r, s)\) is \(\operatorname{Cov}(X_r, X_s)\) of Lesson 5, equals \(AA^{\top}\).

Proof

By linearity of expectation (Lesson 2) and \(E[Z] = 0\) from Lesson 6, \(E[X] = \mu + A\,E[Z] = \mu\). The covariance of \(X_r\) and \(X_s\) is \[E[(X_r - E[X_r])(X_s - E[X_s])]\], as in Lesson 5. Write \(\mu_r\) for the \(r\)-th entry of \(\mu\). Then \(X_r - \mu_r = \sum_{i=1}^{k} A_{ri} Z_i\), so \[ \operatorname{Cov}(X_r, X_s) = E\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{j=1}^{k} A_{ri} A_{sj} Z_i Z_j\Bigr] = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sum_{j=1}^{k} A_{ri} A_{sj}\, E[Z_i Z_j]. \] The second equality uses linearity of expectation for finite sums. By Proposition 1.1, \(E[Z_i Z_j]\) is \(1\) when \(i = j\) and \(0\) otherwise, so the double sum collapses to \(\sum_{i} A_{ri} A_{si}\). That sum is entry \((r, s)\) of \(AA^{\top}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.4 (the factor is not unique)

If \(Q\) is a \(k \times k\) matrix with \(QQ^{\top} = I\), then \(A\) and \(AQ\) are both factors of \(AA^{\top}\). Indeed \[(AQ)(AQ)^{\top} = A(QQ^{\top})A^{\top} = AA^{\top}\]. Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1, Proposition 1.3 then gives the same mean and covariance for \(\mu + AQZ\) as for \(\mu + AZ\). A covariance matrix does not determine its factor.

Example 1.5 (a two-dimensional factor, with a second factor)

Take \(\mu = (1, -2)\) and \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \[ \Sigma = AA^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}. \] The rotation \(Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) satisfies \(QQ^{\top} = I\), since its rows are orthonormal. The second factor is \[B = AQ = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\], and \(BB^{\top} = \Sigma\) by Remark 1.4. The matrix \(A^{\top}A = \begin{pmatrix} 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) is a different matrix, and it is not a covariance matrix of \(X = \mu + AZ\). Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1, Proposition 1.3 gives \(E[X] = (1, -2)\) and covariance \(\Sigma\) for both \(X = \mu + AZ\) and \(Y = \mu + BZ\).

Proposition 1.6 (covariance matrices are symmetric and nonnegative)

Let \(\Sigma = AA^{\top}\) for a real matrix \(A\). Then \(\Sigma\) is symmetric, and \(w^{\top}\Sigma w \ge 0\) for every vector \(w\) of the matching length.

Proof

\((AA^{\top})^{\top} = (A^{\top})^{\top} A^{\top} = AA^{\top}\), so \(\Sigma\) is symmetric. For any \(w\), \[w^{\top}AA^{\top}w = (A^{\top}w)^{\top}(A^{\top}w)\], which is the sum of the squares of the entries of \(A^{\top}w\), and so is nonnegative. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.6 is the statement of Lesson 5, Proposition 2.3 (a covariance matrix is positive semidefinite), proved here for the matrices that this lesson uses. A symmetric matrix \(\Sigma\) is positive definite when \(w^{\top}\Sigma w > 0\) for every nonzero vector \(w\). Example 1.5 is positive definite, because \(w^{\top}\Sigma w = (2w_1 + w_2)^2 + w_2^2\), which is zero only when \(w_2 = 0\) and \(2w_1 = 0\), that is, when \(w = 0\).

2Singular covariance matrices

This section proves one fact about covariance matrices that are not positive definite. It states no density formula for a multivariate normal law with a general factor. Section 4 uses one density only, the product density of two independent standard normal variables. A square matrix is singular when it has no inverse. A matrix with a nonzero \(w\) such that \(\Sigma w = 0\) is not positive definite, because then \(w^{\top}\Sigma w = 0\); Proposition 2.1 gives the converse for \(\Sigma = AA^{\top}\).

Proposition 2.1 (a singular covariance has no inverse)

Let \(\Sigma = AA^{\top}\) for a real matrix \(A\), and suppose that \(\Sigma\) is not positive definite. Then there is a nonzero vector \(w\) with \(\Sigma w = 0\), and \(\Sigma\) has no inverse.

Proof

Since \(\Sigma\) is not positive definite, some nonzero \(w\) has \(w^{\top}\Sigma w \le 0\). By Proposition 1.6, \(w^{\top}\Sigma w \ge 0\), so \(w^{\top}\Sigma w = 0\). Write \(u = A^{\top}w\). The proof of Proposition 1.6 gives \(w^{\top}\Sigma w = u^{\top}u = \sum_j u_j^2\), so every \(u_j\) is 0 and \(u = 0\). Hence \(\Sigma w = A(A^{\top}w) = Au = 0\). If \(\Sigma\) had an inverse \(\Sigma^{-1}\), then \(w = \Sigma^{-1}\Sigma w = 0\), which contradicts \(w \ne 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Sample covariance against AAT

Given draws \(X^{(1)}, \ldots, X^{(n)}\) of a random vector, the sample covariance matrix with divisor \(n - 1\) has entry \((r, s)\) equal to \(\frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{t=1}^{n} (X_r^{(t)} - \bar X_r)(X_s^{(t)} - \bar X_s)\), where \(\bar X_r\) is the sample mean of coordinate \(r\). It is the sample analogue of the covariance of Lesson 5. Lab 1 draws standard normal vectors from a seeded generator, forms \(X = \mu + AZ\) and \(Y = \mu + BZ\) from the same draws, and compares their sample covariance matrices with the exact matrix \(\Sigma\). Part A is exact integer arithmetic. Part B is one random run.

Lab 1 · The linear image of a standard normal vector, against AAT

Predicted before running, for the example of Section 1 with \(\mu = (1, -2)\), \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Part A should print \[AA^{\top} = ((4, 2), (2, 2))\], should print True for \(BB^{\top} = \Sigma\) with \(B = AQ\), and should print \(A^{\top}A = ((5, 1), (1, 1))\) with False for equality with \(\Sigma\). Part B should print a sample mean near \((1, -2)\) and a sample covariance matrix near \(\Sigma\), for \(n = 20000\) draws with seed 2026. The sample covariance of \(X\) should be closer to \(\Sigma\) than to \(A^{\top}A\), which differs from \(\Sigma\) by 1 in absolute value in each of its four entries.

"""M4 lab L08: a linear image of a standard normal vector, checked against its covariance A A^T.

SIMULATED: the draws come from a seeded generator; no data is used.
Standard library only (random). The mean is mu = (1, -2) and the factor is A = [[2, 0], [1, 1]], so the
target covariance is Sigma = A A^T = [[4, 2], [2, 2]]. Part A uses exact integer matrix arithmetic. It computes
A A^T and takes it as Sigma, checks that B = A Q, with Q the rotation [[0, -1], [1, 0]], has B B^T equal to Sigma,
and that A^T A is a different matrix. Part B draws n = 20000 standard normal vectors Z (two random.gauss(0, 1)
values per draw, seed 2026), forms X = mu + A Z and Y = mu + B Z from the same Z, and prints the sample mean of X
beside mu, the sample covariance matrix of X (divisor n - 1) beside Sigma, the largest entry gaps of that matrix
from Sigma and from A^T A, and the sample covariance matrix of Y with its largest entry gap from Sigma.
A sample is one random run, so its entries estimate the exact entries and are not equal to them.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from collections.abc import Sequence

Matrix = tuple[tuple[int, ...], ...]

SEED = 2026
N_DRAWS = 20000
MU: tuple[float, float] = (1.0, -2.0)
A: Matrix = ((2, 0), (1, 1))
Q: Matrix = ((0, -1), (1, 0))


def matmul(x: Matrix, y: Matrix) -> Matrix:
    """Exact product of two integer matrices given as tuples of rows."""
    inner = len(y)
    return tuple(tuple(sum(x[i][k] * y[k][j] for k in range(inner)) for j in range(len(y[0]))) for i in range(len(x)))


def transpose(x: Matrix) -> Matrix:
    """Transpose of an integer matrix given as tuples of rows."""
    return tuple(tuple(row[j] for row in x) for j in range(len(x[0])))


def affine(x: Matrix, z: Sequence[float], mu: Sequence[float]) -> list[float]:
    """The vector mu + x z, where x is a 2 by 2 integer matrix and z has two coordinates."""
    return [mu[i] + sum(x[i][k] * z[k] for k in range(len(z))) for i in range(len(x))]


def sample_mean(rows: Sequence[Sequence[float]]) -> list[float]:
    """Coordinate-wise sample mean of a list of vectors."""
    n = len(rows)
    return [sum(r[i] for r in rows) / n for i in range(len(rows[0]))]


def sample_covariance(rows: Sequence[Sequence[float]]) -> list[list[float]]:
    """Sample covariance matrix of a list of vectors, with divisor n - 1."""
    n = len(rows)
    mean = sample_mean(rows)
    d = len(mean)
    return [[sum((r[i] - mean[i]) * (r[j] - mean[j]) for r in rows) / (n - 1) for j in range(d)] for i in range(d)]


def largest_gap(sample: Sequence[Sequence[float]], exact: Matrix) -> float:
    """Largest absolute entry-wise difference between a sample matrix and an exact integer matrix."""
    return max(abs(sample[i][j] - exact[i][j]) for i in range(len(exact)) for j in range(len(exact)))


def fmt(m: Sequence[Sequence[float]]) -> str:
    """Format a 2 by 2 matrix with four decimals per entry."""
    return "((" + f"{m[0][0]:.4f}, {m[0][1]:.4f}" + "), (" + f"{m[1][0]:.4f}, {m[1][1]:.4f}" + "))"


def main() -> None:
    sigma = matmul(A, transpose(A))
    b = matmul(A, Q)
    print(f"part A: A A^T = {sigma} = Sigma")
    print(f"part A: B = A Q = {b}, and B B^T equals Sigma: {matmul(b, transpose(b)) == sigma}")
    ata = matmul(transpose(A), A)
    print(f"part A: A^T A = {ata}, and A^T A equals Sigma: {ata == sigma}")

    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    rows_a: list[list[float]] = []
    rows_b: list[list[float]] = []
    for _ in range(N_DRAWS):
        z = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0), rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)]
        rows_a.append(affine(A, z, MU))
        rows_b.append(affine(b, z, MU))
    mean_a = sample_mean(rows_a)
    cov_a = sample_covariance(rows_a)
    cov_b = sample_covariance(rows_b)
    print(f"part B: sample mean of X = ({mean_a[0]:.4f}, {mean_a[1]:.4f}), against mu = (1, -2)")
    print(f"part B: sample covariance of X = {fmt(cov_a)}, against Sigma = {sigma}")
    print(f"part B: largest entry gap of X's sample covariance from Sigma = {largest_gap(cov_a, sigma):.4f}")
    print(f"part B: largest entry gap of X's sample covariance from A^T A = {largest_gap(cov_a, ata):.4f}")
    print(f"part B: sample covariance of Y = {fmt(cov_b)}")
    print(f"part B: largest entry gap of Y's sample covariance from Sigma = {largest_gap(cov_b, sigma):.4f}")


main()

part A: B = A Q = ((0, -2), (1, -1)), and B B^T equals Sigma: True
part A: A^T A = ((5, 1), (1, 1)), and A^T A equals Sigma: False
part B: sample mean of X = (0.9770, -2.0055), against mu = (1, -2)
part B: sample covariance of X = ((4.0073, 2.0187), (2.0187, 1.9936)), against Sigma = ((4, 2), (2, 2))
part B: largest entry gap of X's sample covariance from Sigma = 0.0187
part B: largest entry gap of X's sample covariance from A^T A = 1.0187
part B: sample covariance of Y = ((3.9070, 1.9385), (1.9385, 1.9636))

Part A is exact. The first line is \(AA^{\top}\), which equals \(\Sigma\). The second line confirms Remark 1.4 for \(B = AQ\), and the third shows that \(A^{\top}A\) is a different matrix, so in this example transposing the factor on the wrong side gives a different covariance. Part B is one run with seed 2026. Its sample mean of \(X\) is \((0.9770, -2.0055)\), which is \(0.0230\) below 1 in the first coordinate and \(0.0055\) below \(-2\) in the second. A different seed gives other sample values, and the printed numbers say nothing about the size of that spread.

4Where the canon breaks

  • Normal margins do not fix the joint law. Let \(X\) be standard normal and let \(\varepsilon\) take the values \(1\) and \(-1\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, independent of \(X\). Put \(Y = \varepsilon X\). Then \(E[\varepsilon] = 0\), so \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = E[\varepsilon X^2] = E[\varepsilon]\,E[X^2] = 0\), using the independence of \(\varepsilon\) and \(X\). Also \(Y\) is standard normal, because \(X\) and \(-X\) have the same law, so \[P(Y \le y) = \tfrac12 P(X \le y) + \tfrac12 P(-X \le y) = P(X \le y)\]. Yet \(P(X + Y = 0) \ge P(\varepsilon = -1) = \tfrac12\), because \(Y = -X\) on the event \(\{\varepsilon = -1\}\). Now let \(X'\) be a standard normal variable independent of \(X\). The pair \((X, X')\) has the joint density \(\varphi(x)\varphi(x')\), where \(\varphi(x) = (2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/2}\) is the standard normal density of Lesson 6, and the line \(\{x + x' = 0\}\) has area zero in the plane. So \(P(X + X' = 0) = 0\). Both pairs have standard normal coordinates, and their sums are zero with different probabilities, so the two joint laws differ. Normal margins do not determine the joint law.
  • The factor is not the covariance. Remark 1.4 and Example 1.5 show that \(A\) and \(AQ\) give the same covariance, and that \(A^{\top}A\) is a different matrix. A factor is not a square root that is determined by \(\Sigma\), and a computation with \(A\) that uses \(A^{\top}A\) in place of \(AA^{\top}\) gives the wrong matrix.
  • A singular covariance has no inverse. A covariance matrix need not be positive definite. Section 2 shows that a factored \(\Sigma = AA^{\top}\) that is not positive definite has a nonzero \(w\) with \(\Sigma w = 0\), so no inverse exists, and a formula that uses \(\Sigma^{-1}\) is undefined for it.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Normal margins determine the joint law.”

    Section 4 gives two pairs with standard normal coordinates. In \((X, Y)\) the sum is zero with probability at least one half. In \((X, X')\), with \(X'\) independent of \(X\), the sum is zero with probability 0. Definition 1.2 is a statement about the whole vector, and it needs the factor \(A\) of the whole vector.

  2. “The covariance of \(AZ\) is \(A^{\top}A\).”

    Proposition 1.3 gives \(AA^{\top}\). Example 1.5 computes both products for the same \(A\), and Lab 1 prints them as different: \[AA^{\top} = ((4, 2), (2, 2))\] and \(A^{\top}A = ((5, 1), (1, 1))\). The two products agree when \(A\) is symmetric, but this \(A\) is not symmetric, and the proof of Proposition 1.3 shows which product appears.

  3. “The factor of a covariance matrix is unique, so it can be read off the covariance.”

    Remark 1.4 shows that \(AQ\) is another factor of \(AA^{\top}\) whenever \(QQ^{\top} = I\). Example 1.5 gives two factors, \(A\) and \(B\), of the same \(\Sigma\). The covariance determines \(AA^{\top}\), and it does not determine \(A\).

Mechanics

M1 · The covariance of a second factor

Compute \(AA^{\top}\) for \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\).

Solution

The first row of \(A\) is \((1, 1)\), and its inner products with itself and with the second row \((0, 2)\) are \(1 + 1 = 2\) and \(0 + 2 = 2\). The second row has inner product \(0 + 4 = 4\) with itself. So \(AA^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\), which is symmetric, as Proposition 1.6 requires.

M2 · A diagonal factor

Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1, let \(X = \mu + AZ\) with \(\mu = (0, 5)\) and \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). State \(E[X]\) and the covariance matrix of \(X\).

Solution

By Proposition 1.3, \(E[X] = \mu = (0, 5)\). The covariance is \(AA^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix}\), since \(A\) is diagonal, so \(AA^{\top} = A^{\top}A = A^2\), and the diagonal entries are \(1^2 = 1\) and \(3^2 = 9\). The coordinates are uncorrelated, with variances 1 and 9.

M3 · Checking the rotation

Verify that \(Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) satisfies \(QQ^{\top} = I\).

Solution

\(Q^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\). The product \(QQ^{\top}\) has entries \((0)(0) + (-1)(-1) = 1\) in position \((1,1)\), \((0)(1) + (-1)(0) = 0\) in position \((1,2)\), \((1)(0) + (0)(-1) = 0\) in position \((2,1)\), and \((1)(1) + (0)(0) = 1\) in position \((2,2)\). So \(QQ^{\top} = I\).

P1 · The mean of a linear image

Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1, prove that \(E[\mu + AZ] = \mu\) for a standard normal vector \(Z\), stating each property of expectation that you use.

Solution

Each coordinate of \(AZ\) is \(\sum_i A_{ri} Z_i\), a finite sum of constant multiples of the coordinates. Linearity of expectation for finite sums (Lesson 2) gives \(E[\sum_i A_{ri} Z_i] = \sum_i A_{ri} E[Z_i]\), and each \(E[Z_i] = 0\) by Lesson 6. So \(E[AZ] = 0\), and adding the constant vector \(\mu\) gives \(E[\mu + AZ] = \mu + E[AZ] = \mu\), since the expectation of a constant is that constant.

P2 · Symmetry of AA transposed

Prove that \(AA^{\top}\) is symmetric for every real matrix \(A\), using only the rule for the transpose of a product.

Solution

The transpose of a product reverses the order of the factors, \((XY)^{\top} = Y^{\top}X^{\top}\). Applying this with \(X = A\) and \(Y = A^{\top}\) gives \[(AA^{\top})^{\top} = (A^{\top})^{\top}A^{\top} = AA^{\top}\], because the transpose of a transpose is the original matrix. So \(AA^{\top}\) equals its own transpose.

P3 · Nonnegativity of the quadratic form

Show that \(w^{\top}AA^{\top}w \ge 0\) for every \(w\), and identify the case of equality for the factor of Example 1.5.

Solution

Write \(u = A^{\top}w\). Then \[w^{\top}AA^{\top}w = (A^{\top}w)^{\top}(A^{\top}w) = u^{\top}u = \sum_j u_j^2 \ge 0\]. For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), \(A^{\top}w = (2w_1 + w_2, w_2)\), so the form is \((2w_1 + w_2)^2 + w_2^2\), which is zero only when \(w_2 = 0\) and \(2w_1 = 0\). That is \(w = 0\), so the form is positive definite, as stated in Section 1.

Empirical

E1 · The entry gaps of the X sample (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints the sample covariance of \(X\) as \(((4.0073, 2.0187), (2.0187, 1.9936))\). Compute the four entry gaps from \(\Sigma\), and check that the largest is the 0.0187 that the lab prints.

Solution

The gaps are \(|4.0073 - 4| = 0.0073\) for entry \((1,1)\), \(|2.0187 - 2| = 0.0187\) for entries \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\), and \(|1.9936 - 2| = 0.0064\) for entry \((2,2)\). The gaps are differences of the printed 4-decimal entries, so each is accurate to about 0.0001. The largest is 0.0187, which matches the lab's printed largest entry gap. The largest gap sits in the off-diagonal entries, and the lab prints only the largest value, not the entry where it occurs.

E2 · The gap from the wrong matrix (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints a largest entry gap of 1.0187 between the sample covariance of \(X\) and \(A^{\top}A\). Compute the exact gap between \(A^{\top}A\) and \(\Sigma\), and explain how the sample gap of 1.0187 arises.

Solution

\(A^{\top}A - \Sigma\) has entries \(5 - 4 = 1\), \[1 - 2 = -1\], \[1 - 2 = -1\] and \[1 - 2 = -1\], so the exact largest entry gap between \(A^{\top}A\) and \(\Sigma\) is 1. In entry \((1,2)\), the target \(\Sigma_{12} = 2\) lies 1 above \(A^{\top}A_{12} = 1\), and the sample value 2.0187 lies a further 0.0187 above \(\Sigma_{12}\). The sample gap from \(A^{\top}A\) in that entry is therefore \(2.0187 - 1 = 1.0187\), which is the printed value. It is the exact gap of 1 plus the sample error of 0.0187.

E3 · Two samples from the same draws (Lab 1)

Lab 1 forms \(X = \mu + AZ\) and \(Y = \mu + BZ\) from the same draws, and reports largest entry gaps of 0.0187 for \(X\) and 0.0930 for \(Y\). State what these two numbers do and do not show.

Solution

Both numbers are gaps from the same exact matrix \(\Sigma\), since \(BB^{\top} = \Sigma\) by Part A. They show that, in this one run, every entry of the sample covariance matrix of \(X\) lies within 0.0187 of the matching entry of \(\Sigma\), and every entry of that of \(Y\) lies within 0.0930 of it. They do not show which sample is closer in general, because the draws \(Z\) are the same but \(X\) and \(Y\) are different linear images of them, and one run cannot separate the factor's effect from the sampling error. The gap of 0.0930 is the largest entry gap printed for \(Y\), in entry \((1,1)\), where the sample value 3.9070 lies 0.0930 below 4.

R1 · A lower-triangular factor is unique for this \(\Sigma\)

Show that the only lower-triangular matrix \(L = \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}\) with \(a > 0\), \(c > 0\) and \(LL^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) is \(A\) of Example 1.5.

Solution

\(LL^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} a^2 & ab \\ ab & b^2 + c^2 \end{pmatrix}\). Matching entries gives \(a^2 = 4\), so \(a = 2\) because \(a > 0\). Then \(ab = 2\) gives \(b = 1\), and \(b^2 + c^2 = 2\) gives \(c^2 = 1\), so \(c = 1\) because \(c > 0\). Hence \(L = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = A\). The second factor \(B\) of Example 1.5 is not lower-triangular, since its \((1,2)\) entry is \(-2\), so this result does not contradict Remark 1.4. It shows that uniqueness holds within the class of lower-triangular factors with positive diagonal, and not for all factors.

R2 · Two factors, one law of the mean

Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1, let \(X = \mu + AZ\) and \(Y = \mu + BZ\) with \(B = AQ\) and \(QQ^{\top} = I\). Show that \(E[X] = E[Y]\) and that both covariance matrices equal \(\Sigma\). Explain what this does not show about the laws of \(X\) and \(Y\).

Solution

Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1, Proposition 1.3 applied to each gives \(E[X] = E[Y] = \mu\), and covariances \(AA^{\top}\) and \(BB^{\top} = AQQ^{\top}A^{\top} = AA^{\top}\), both equal to \(\Sigma\). This shows that the first two moments agree. It does not show that \(X\) and \(Y\) have the same joint law, because the proof uses only the mean and the covariance, and Definition 1.2 asks for equality of laws. The page does not prove that the law depends on \(A\) only through \(AA^{\top}\).

R3 · The probability in the normal-margins example

In Section 4, show that \(X\) and \(-X\) have the same law and deduce \(P(Y \le y) = P(X \le y)\) for \(Y = \varepsilon X\).

Solution

The standard normal density of Lesson 6 is an even function of \(x\), so \(X\) and \(-X\) have the same law. Therefore \(P(-X \le y) = P(X \ge -y)\), and by the same evenness \(P(X \ge -y) = P(X \le y)\). Next, \(Y = \varepsilon X\) equals \(X\) when \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(-X\) when \(\varepsilon = -1\). Since \(\varepsilon\) is independent of \(X\), each sign has probability \(\tfrac12\) and is independent of \(X\), so \[P(Y \le y) = \tfrac12 P(X \le y) + \tfrac12 P(-X \le y) = P(X \le y)\]. The independence used here is the one of Lesson 4.

  1. For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), what is the \((1,1)\) entry of \(AA^{\top}\)?

    The first row of \(A\) is \((2, 0)\), and \(2^2 + 0^2 = 4\) (Example 1.5).

  2. Which matrix is the covariance matrix of \(X = \mu + AZ\) for \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\)?

    Options

    Proposition 1.3 gives \(AA^{\top}\). The second matrix is \(A^{\top}A\), which is a different product (Example 1.5).

  3. For the same \(A\), what is the \((2,2)\) entry of \(AA^{\top}\)?

    The second row of \(A\) is \((1, 1)\), and \(1^2 + 1^2 = 2\).

  4. For the same \(A\), what is the \((1,1)\) entry of \(A^{\top}A\)?

    The first column of \(A\) is \((2, 1)\), and \(2^2 + 1^2 = 5\). This matrix is not the covariance (Example 1.5).

  5. Let \(Q\) satisfy \(QQ^{\top} = I\). Which statement is true of the factor \(AQ\)?

    Options

    \[(AQ)(AQ)^{\top} = A(QQ^{\top})A^{\top} = AA^{\top}\], so Proposition 1.3 gives the same covariance.

  6. For \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(w = (1, 1)\), what is \(w^{\top}\Sigma w\)?

    \(w^{\top}\Sigma w = 4 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 10\), the sum of all four entries of \(\Sigma\) because \(w = (1, 1)\).

  7. What is the mean of \(X = \mu + AZ\), where \(Z\) is a standard normal vector?

    Options

    Proposition 1.3: \(E[AZ] = 0\), so \(E[X] = \mu\).

  8. In the example of Section 4, what is the lower bound on \(P(X + Y = 0)\) that the argument gives?

    \(P(X + Y = 0) \ge P(\varepsilon = -1) = \tfrac12\), because \(Y = -X\) on that event.

  9. Is the factor \(A\) of a covariance matrix \(\Sigma\) determined by \(\Sigma\)?

    Options

    Remark 1.4. Example 1.5 gives two different factors of the same \(\Sigma\).

  10. Which statement about normal coordinates is correct?

    Options

    Section 4: normal coordinates that are uncorrelated give two joint laws, one with \(P(X + Y = 0) \ge \tfrac12\) and one with \(P(X + X' = 0) = 0\).
