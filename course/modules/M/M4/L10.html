Heavy Tails and Infinite Variance

  • Uses: expectation and variance (Lesson 2); normal distribution and Student-t distribution (Lesson 6); moment-generating function (Lesson 7); law of large numbers and central limit theorem (Lesson 9); limit and definite integral (M2)
  • Introduces: heavy-tailed distribution and infinite variance, where the CLT hypothesis fails
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

The laws of Lesson 9 need a finite variance, and this lesson studies laws that may not have one. It gives a tail-decay definition (Definition 1.1), computes the tails of two laws exactly, and shows that one of them has no mean. The curriculum lab draws sample means under three laws and records what they do as the sample grows. The lab results are simulation output, not theorems.

1Heavy tails by tail decay

Definition 1.1 (heavy-tailed law)

A random variable \(X\) is heavy-tailed when, for every \(c > 0\), \[ \lim_{x \to \infty} e^{cx}\,P(|X| > x) = \infty. \] A law that is not heavy-tailed is called light-tailed in this lesson. Not heavy-tailed means that for some \(c > 0\) the quantity \(e^{cx}P(|X| > x)\) does not tend to infinity.

The definition is a statement about the tail probability alone. It does not say whether a moment is finite. Proposition 1.5 checks the two laws used here against it.

Assumption 1 (density of the Student-t law with three degrees of freedom)

The Student-t law \(V\) with \(\nu = 3\) has the density \(f_V(t) = \dfrac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}(1 + t^2/3)^{-2}\). Lesson 6 defines \(V\) as \(Z/\sqrt{W/3}\) and proves its variance, but it does not derive this density. This page takes the density as Assumption 1, and each result that uses it says so.

Definition 1.2 (standard Cauchy law and Student-t with three degrees of freedom)

The standard Cauchy law \(C\) has density \(f_C(x) = \dfrac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}\). The Student-t law \(V\) with \(\nu = 3\) degrees of freedom has the density \(f_V\) of Assumption 1, \[f_V(t) = \dfrac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}\,(1 + t^2/3)^{-2}\]. Lesson 6 defines this law as \(V = Z/\sqrt{W/3}\), with \(Z\) standard normal and \(W\) the sum of three squared independent standard normals, and this lesson uses only the density.

Proposition 1.3 (distribution functions in closed form)

For real \(x\), the distribution function of \(C\) is \(F_C(x) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi \arctan x\), and, assuming Assumption 1, the distribution function of \(V\) is \[ F_V(x) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi \Bigl(\arctan\frac{x}{\sqrt3} + \frac{x/\sqrt3}{1 + x^2/3}\Bigr). \]

Proof

The tangent function has derivative \(1 + \tan^2\theta\) on \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\), so by the inverse function rule \(\arctan'(x) = 1/(1 + x^2)\) (M2). Hence \(\frac{d}{dx}F_C(x) = f_C(x)\). Also \(\arctan x \to \pm\pi/2\) as \(x \to \pm\infty\), so \(F_C(-\infty) = 0\) and \(F_C(\infty) = 1\). A distribution function is the integral of its density, so \(F_C\) is the distribution function of \(C\).

For \(V\), set \(y = x/\sqrt3\). The derivative of \(\arctan(x/\sqrt3)\) is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + y^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{3}{3 + x^2}\). The derivative of \(\frac{x/\sqrt3}{1 + x^2/3}\) is \[\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{(1 + x^2/3) - x\cdot 2x/3}{(1 + x^2/3)^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{1 - x^2/3}{(1 + x^2/3)^2}\]. Adding these, with \(\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{3}{3 + x^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + x^2/3}\), the sum is \[\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{(1 + x^2/3) + (1 - x^2/3)}{(1 + x^2/3)^2} = \frac{2}{\sqrt3\,(1 + x^2/3)^2}\]. Multiplying by \(1/\pi\) gives \[\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}(1 + x^2/3)^{-2} = f_V(x)\]. As \(x \to \pm\infty\), \(\arctan(x/\sqrt3) \to \pm\pi/2\) and \(\frac{x/\sqrt3}{1 + x^2/3} \to 0\), so \(F_V(\pm\infty) = 1\) and \(0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.4 (tails of the two laws)

For \(x > 0\), \(P(|C| > x) = \dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\dfrac{1}{x}\), and, assuming Assumption 1, \(P(|V| > x) = \dfrac{2}{\pi}\Bigl(\arctan\dfrac{\sqrt3}{x} - \dfrac{x\sqrt3}{3 + x^2}\Bigr)\).

Proof

By symmetry of the density, \[P(|C| > x) = 2P(C > x) = 2(1 - F_C(x)) = 1 - \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan x\]. For \(x > 0\), \(\arctan x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(1/x)\), which gives \[P(|C| > x) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan(1/x)\].

For \(V\), the same symmetry and Proposition 1.3 give \[P(|V| > x) = 2(1 - F_V(x))\]. Write \(y = x/\sqrt3 > 0\). Then \(\arctan y = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(1/y)\), and \[1 - F_V(x) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac1\pi\bigl(\arctan y + \tfrac{y}{1 + y^2}\bigr) = \tfrac1\pi\bigl(\arctan(\sqrt3/x) - \tfrac{y}{1 + y^2}\bigr)\]. Since \(\tfrac{y}{1 + y^2} = \tfrac{x/\sqrt3}{1 + x^2/3} = \tfrac{x\sqrt3}{3 + x^2}\), the second formula follows with the factor 2. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 1.5 (the Cauchy and Student-t(3) laws are heavy-tailed; the normal law is light-tailed)

(a) \(C\) is heavy-tailed. (b) Assuming Assumption 1, \(V\) is heavy-tailed. (c) A standard normal \(Z\) is light-tailed: \[P(|Z| > x) \le 2e^{-x^2/2}\] for \(x > 0\), so \(e^{cx}P(|Z| > x) \to 0\) for every \(c > 0\).

Proof

Two elementary inequalities. For \(u \ge 0\), \(h(u) = \arctan u - u + u^3/3\) has \(h(0) = 0\) and \[h'(u) = \frac{1}{1 + u^2} - 1 + u^2 = \frac{u^4}{1 + u^2} \ge 0\], so \(\arctan u \ge u - u^3/3\). Also \[e^{cx} \ge (cx)^2/2\] for \(x \ge 0\), by the first terms of the exponential series, so \(e^{cx}/x \ge c^2 x/2 \to \infty\).

(a) For \(x \ge 1\), with \(u = 1/x\), Proposition 1.4 and the first inequality give \(P(|C| > x) \ge \tfrac{2}{\pi}\bigl(\tfrac1x - \tfrac{1}{3x^3}\bigr) \ge \tfrac{1}{\pi x}\) (since \(\tfrac1{3x^3} \le \tfrac{1}{2x}\) for \(x \ge 1\)). Hence \(e^{cx}P(|C| > x) \ge e^{cx}/(\pi x) \to \infty\).

(b) For \(t^2 \ge 3\), \(1 + t^2/3 \le 2t^2/3\), so \(f_V(t) \ge \frac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{9}{4t^4}\). Integrating over \([x, \infty)\) for \(x \ge \sqrt3\) gives \[P(V > x) \ge \frac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{9}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{3x^3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2\pi x^3}\], and by symmetry \(P(|V| > x) \ge \frac{\sqrt3}{\pi x^3}\). Then \(e^{cx}P(|V| > x) \ge e^{cx}\,\frac{\sqrt3}{\pi x^3} \to \infty\), because \[e^{cx}/x^3 \ge (cx)^4/(24 x^3) = c^4 x/24 \to \infty\].

(c) By the Markov step for the nonnegative variable \(e^{sZ}\): \(e^{sx}\mathbf{1}\{Z > x\} \le e^{sZ}\) for \(s > 0\), so \[P(Z > x) \le e^{-sx}E[e^{sZ}]\]. The moment-generating function of Lesson 7 gives \(E[e^{sZ}] = e^{s^2/2}\). Choose \(s = x\) to get \[P(Z > x) \le e^{-x^2/2}\]. By symmetry, \[P(|Z| > x) \le 2e^{-x^2/2}\], and \(e^{cx}\cdot 2e^{-x^2/2} \to 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.6 (the Markov step)

Sections 1 and 2 integrate densities. For a law with a density, the expectation is the integral of \(x\) times the density, in the definite-integral form that Lesson 6 uses (M2). The Markov step of Proposition 1.5(c), the integral in Proposition 2.1, and the tails of Proposition 1.4 are of this kind. Lesson 9's finite sums do not cover them, and this lesson does not extend Lesson 9's theorems.

Proposition 1.5 says that the Cauchy and Student-t(3) laws are heavy-tailed in the sense of Definition 1.1, and the normal law is not. The next proposition gives the limits of the scaled tails, which the lab prints.

Proposition 1.7 (the scaled tails)

As \(x \to \infty\), \(x\,P(|C| > x) \to \tfrac{2}{\pi}\), and, assuming Assumption 1, \(x^3\,P(|V| > x) \to \tfrac{4\sqrt3}{\pi}\).

Proof

For the Cauchy law, \(xP(|C| > x) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\arctan u}{u}\) with \(u = 1/x \to 0\), and \(\arctan u / u \to 1\) because \(\arctan'(0) = 1\) (M2). For \(V\), let \[y = \sqrt3/x \to 0\]. By Proposition 1.4, \[P(|V| > x) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\bigl(\arctan y - \tfrac{y}{1 + y^2}\bigr)\], since \(\tfrac{x\sqrt3}{3 + x^2} = \tfrac{y}{1 + y^2}\) for \(y = \sqrt3/x\). Taylor's theorem with remainder (M2) gives \[\arctan y = y - y^3/3 + O(y^5)\] and \[\tfrac{y}{1 + y^2} = y - y^3 + O(y^5)\]. The difference is \(\tfrac{2}{3}y^3 + O(y^5)\), so \(P(|V| > x) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac23 y^3 + O(y^5)\). With \(y^3 = 3\sqrt3/x^3\), this gives \[P(|V| > x) = \tfrac{4}{3\pi}\cdot 3\sqrt3\,x^{-3} + O(x^{-5}) = \tfrac{4\sqrt3}{\pi}x^{-3} + O(x^{-5})\]. Multiply by \(x^3\) and let \(x \to \infty\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Moments, and infinite variance

Proposition 2.1 (the standard Cauchy law has no mean and no variance)

For the standard Cauchy law \(C\), \(E|C| = \infty\) and \(E[C^2] = \infty\). Hence \(E[C]\) is not defined, and \(\operatorname{Var}(C)\) is not finite.

Proof

Since \(f_C\) is even, \[E|C| = 2\int_0^\infty x\,f_C(x)\,dx \ge 2\int_1^\infty \frac{x}{\pi(1 + x^2)}\,dx\], because \(x \ge 1\) on the range. Substitute \(v = 1 + x^2\), so \(dv = 2x\,dx\): the integral is \[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_2^\infty \frac{dv}{v} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\lim_{M \to \infty}(\ln M - \ln 2) = \infty\], so \(E|C| = \infty\). For the second moment, \(x^2 \ge |x|\) when \(|x| \ge 1\), so \[E[C^2] \ge \int_{|x| \ge 1} |x|\,f_C(x)\,dx = E|C| - \int_{|x| < 1}|x|\,f_C(x)\,dx\]. The subtracted integral is at most \(2/\pi\), because \(|x| f_C(x) \le 1/\pi\) on \([-1, 1]\) and that interval has length 2. Since \(E|C| = \infty\), the right side is \(\infty\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.2 (the Student-t(3) variance)

Lesson 6 gives \(\operatorname{Var}(V) = \nu/(\nu - 2)\) for \(\nu > 2\). With \(\nu = 3\) this is \(3\). So the Student-t(3) law has a finite variance, while Proposition 1.5(b) shows it is heavy-tailed. Heavy-tailed and infinite-variance are different properties, and this lesson does not claim that the two coincide. The variance formula is cited from Lesson 6 and not derived here.

Proposition 2.3 (the fourth moment of the Student-t(3) law is infinite)

Assuming Assumption 1, for the Student-t law \(V\) with \(\nu = 3\), \(E[V^4] = \infty\). Hence the kurtosis of Lesson 3 is not defined for \(V\).

Proof

For \(t^2 \ge 3\), the density bound from the proof of Proposition 1.5(b) gives \(f_V(t) \ge \frac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{9}{4t^4} = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi t^4}\). Since \(f_V\) is even, \[E[V^4] = 2\int_0^\infty t^4 f_V(t)\,dt \ge 2\int_{\sqrt3}^\infty t^4 \cdot \frac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi t^4}\,dt = \frac{3\sqrt3}{\pi}\int_{\sqrt3}^\infty dt = \infty\]. The integrand \(t^4 f_V(t)\) is at least the constant \(3\sqrt3/(2\pi)\), so the integral diverges. \(\blacksquare\)

Definition 2.4 (infinite variance: the CLT hypothesis fails)

The infinite variance, where the finite-variance hypothesis of the CLT fails is the situation in which the variance of \(X\) is not finite. The standardization \((\bar X_n - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\) of Lesson 9 then has no finite \(\sigma\) to use, and the normal limit of Definition 2.4 in Lesson 9 is not available for that standardization. Proposition 2.1 gives a law where this holds. This page proves only that the finite-variance hypothesis fails for \(C\); it does not prove that the normal limit fails. Remark 2.5 states a claim about the sample mean of \(C\), without proof.

Remark 2.5 (the stable limit: stated, not proved, not cited, not graded)

For the standard Cauchy law, the sample mean of \(n\) independent copies is again standard Cauchy for every \(n\). A stable limit law of this kind would be the candidate limit for the sample mean under infinite variance. This page does not prove the statement, cites no source for it, and gives it no grade. Lab 1 prints quantities that are consistent with it, and the lab does not establish it. No source for this statement is identified on this page.

The theorems of Lesson 9 are proved for finitely valued laws, and neither \(C\) nor \(V\) is finitely valued. So this lesson does not apply Lesson 9 to either law. The statements about \(V\) above use Lesson 6's variance, the tails of Proposition 1.5, and Assumption 1. The Cauchy law does not satisfy the hypotheses of the weak law of Lesson 9, because it has no mean (Proposition 2.1), so Theorem 1.3 of Lesson 9 does not apply to it.

"""M4 lab L10: sample means under Gaussian, Student-t(3) and standard Cauchy draws.

SIMULATED: every draw comes from a seeded generator; no data is used. Standard library only (math, random,
statistics). Three laws are drawn. The standard normal is drawn with random.gauss(0, 1). The Student-t law with
3 degrees of freedom is V = Z / sqrt(W / 3), with Z a standard normal and W the sum of three squared independent
standard normals. The standard Cauchy law is drawn by its distribution function's inverse, tan(pi (U - 1/2)), for a
uniform U from random.random().
Part A checks the closed-form distribution function of the t(3) law against a midpoint integral of its density (2 /
(pi sqrt 3)) (1 + t^2 / 3)^(-2), and prints the tail probabilities P(|X| > x) at x = 10 for the three laws. Part B
prints the running sample mean of one seeded path of each law at n = 10, 100, 1000 and 10000. Part C runs 400
seeded replications at n = 10 and n = 100, and prints the interquartile range of the sample mean (the 75th minus
the 25th percentile, with statistics.quantiles, method "inclusive") for each law, and the sample variance of
sqrt(n) Xbar_n / sigma for the normal and t(3) laws, where sigma is 1 for the normal and sqrt(3) for the t(3). Part
D prints the products x P(|X| > x) for the Cauchy law and x^3 P(|V| > x) for the t(3) law at x = 10, 100 and 1000.
A sample is one random run.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
import statistics
from collections.abc import Callable

SEED = 2026
CHECKPOINTS = (10, 100, 1000, 10000)
REPLICATIONS = 400
SAMPLE_SIZES = (10, 100)
SQRT3 = math.sqrt(3.0)


def draw_normal(rng: random.Random) -> float:
    """One standard normal draw."""
    return rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)


def draw_t3(rng: random.Random) -> float:
    """One Student-t draw with 3 degrees of freedom: Z / sqrt(W / 3), W a sum of three squared normals."""
    z = rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
    w = sum(rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) ** 2 for _ in range(3))
    return z / math.sqrt(w / 3.0)


def draw_cauchy(rng: random.Random) -> float:
    """One standard Cauchy draw by the inverse distribution function tan(pi (U - 1/2))."""
    u = rng.random()
    return math.tan(math.pi * (u - 0.5))


def t3_density(t: float) -> float:
    """Density of the Student-t law with 3 degrees of freedom."""
    return 2.0 / (math.pi * SQRT3) * (1.0 + t * t / 3.0) ** -2


def t3_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Distribution function of the Student-t law with 3 degrees of freedom, in closed form."""
    y = x / SQRT3
    return 0.5 + (math.atan(y) + y / (1.0 + x * x / 3.0)) / math.pi


def t3_tail_one_sided(x: float) -> float:
    """P(T > x) for x > 0, written so that the leading terms of the two arctangents cancel without loss."""
    y = x / SQRT3
    return (math.atan(1.0 / y) - y / (1.0 + y * y)) / math.pi


def cauchy_two_sided_tail(x: float) -> float:
    """P(|X| > x) for the standard Cauchy law and x > 0."""
    return 1.0 - 2.0 * math.atan(x) / math.pi


def midpoint_integral(f: Callable[[float], float], a: float, b: float, steps: int) -> float:
    """Midpoint rule for the integral of f over [a, b] with equal steps."""
    h = (b - a) / steps
    return h * sum(f(a + (k + 0.5) * h) for k in range(steps))


def part_a() -> None:
    check = midpoint_integral(t3_density, -2.0, 2.0, 200000)
    exact = t3_cdf(2.0) - t3_cdf(-2.0)
    print(f"part A: midpoint integral of the t(3) density on [-2, 2] = {check:.9f}")
    print(f"part A: t(3) distribution function difference F(2) - F(-2) = {exact:.9f}")
    print(f"part A: the two agree to 1e-8: {abs(check - exact) < 1e-8}")
    x = 10.0
    normal_tail = math.erfc(x / math.sqrt(2.0))
    cauchy_tail = cauchy_two_sided_tail(x)
    t_tail = 2.0 * t3_tail_one_sided(x)
    print(f"part A: P(|Z| > 10) = {normal_tail:.4e}")
    print(f"part A: P(|X| > 10) for the Cauchy law = {cauchy_tail:.6f}")
    print(f"part A: P(|V| > 10) for the t(3) law = {t_tail:.6f}")
    print(f"part A: the Cauchy tail is {cauchy_tail / normal_tail:.3e} times the normal tail at x = 10")


def running_means(draw: Callable[[random.Random], float], seed: int) -> list[tuple[int, float]]:
    """Running sample means of one seeded path at the checkpoints."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    total = 0.0
    out: list[tuple[int, float]] = []
    count = 0
    for target in CHECKPOINTS:
        while count < target:
            total += draw(rng)
            count += 1
        out.append((target, total / target))
    return out


def part_b() -> None:
    laws = (
        ("normal", draw_normal, SEED),
        ("t(3)", draw_t3, SEED + 1),
        ("Cauchy", draw_cauchy, SEED + 2),
    )
    for name, draw, seed in laws:
        for n, mean in running_means(draw, seed):
            print(f"part B: {name:6s} n = {n:5d}, running sample mean = {mean:+.4f}")


def part_c() -> None:
    for name, draw, seed, sigma in (
        ("normal", draw_normal, SEED, 1.0),
        ("t(3)", draw_t3, SEED + 1, SQRT3),
        ("Cauchy", draw_cauchy, SEED + 2, None),
    ):
        for n in SAMPLE_SIZES:
            rng = random.Random(seed + 100 * n)
            means = [sum(draw(rng) for _ in range(n)) / n for _ in range(REPLICATIONS)]
            q1, _, q3 = statistics.quantiles(means, n=4, method="inclusive")
            line = f"part C: {name:6s} n = {n:4d}, interquartile range of the sample mean = {q3 - q1:.4f}"
            if sigma is not None:
                standardized = [math.sqrt(n) * m / sigma for m in means]
                line += f", variance of sqrt(n) Xbar_n / sigma = {statistics.variance(standardized):.4f}"
            print(line)
    exact_iqr = math.tan(math.pi / 4) - math.tan(-math.pi / 4)
    print(f"part C: exact interquartile range of one standard Cauchy draw = {exact_iqr:.6f}")


def part_d() -> None:
    for x in (10.0, 100.0, 1000.0):
        cauchy_product = x * cauchy_two_sided_tail(x)
        t_product = x**3 * 2.0 * t3_tail_one_sided(x)
        print(
            f"part D: x = {x:6.0f}, x P(|X| > x) for Cauchy = {cauchy_product:.6f}, "
            f"x^3 P(|V| > x) for t(3) = {t_product:.6f}"
        )


def main() -> None:
    part_a()
    part_b()
    part_c()
    part_d()

The Student-t(3) path is \(-0.0235\) at \[n = 10000\], and the Cauchy path is \(-57.9246\) at \(n = 10\), \(-5.5268\) at \(n = 100\), \(-0.9062\) at \(n = 1000\) and \(+0.9919\) at \[n = 10000\]. This Cauchy path has not settled by \[n = 10000\], and it is a single path, so the lab does not show how other paths behave. Part C is over 400 replications. The normal interquartile range of the sample mean is 0.4116 at \(n = 10\) and 0.1279 at \(n = 100\), and the t(3) range is 0.6121 and 0.2302. The Cauchy range is 1.8220 at \(n = 10\) and 1.8838 at \(n = 100\). The exact interquartile range of one standard Cauchy draw is 2, and the Cauchy sample-mean ranges stay near that value in this run. The variance of the standardized mean is 1.0211 and 0.9396 for the normal, and 0.8807 and 1.1987 for the t(3). These differ from 1 by between \(-0.12\) and \(+0.20\), and they are sample estimates with no standard error printed. Part D shows \(x\,P(|C| > x)\) rising from 0.634510 to 0.636620 and \(x^3 P(|V| > x)\) rising from 2.128399 to 2.205308 between \(x = 10\) and \(x = 1000\), which is consistent with Proposition 1.7 (the Student-t part under Assumption 1). The lab prints these values only for the three points, and it does not check the limit beyond them.

3Where the canon breaks

  • Heavy is not frequent. The Cauchy tail is far heavier than the normal tail: at \(x = 10\), \(P(|C| > 10)\) is \(0.063451\) and \(P(|Z| > 10)\) is \(1.5240 \times 10^{-23}\). Yet the Cauchy probability of exceeding 10 in absolute value is 0.063451 (Lab 1, Part A). Heavy-tailed describes the decay of the tail, and it is not a claim about how often large values occur in a sample of a given size.
  • The printed t(3) variance is not a normal-limit check. Remark 2.2 gives Student-t(3) a finite variance of 3. Lab 1 prints a standardized variance of 0.8807 at \(n = 10\) and 1.1987 at \(n = 100\), but it does not compare the distribution of the standardized mean with \(\Phi\), and Lesson 9's theorems are proved only for finitely valued laws, so the lab's Student-t(3) results are not theorems on this page.
  • Averaging need not narrow the spread. For the normal law, the printed interquartile range falls from 0.4116 to 0.1279 between \(n = 10\) and \(n = 100\). The ratio of the two printed ranges, \(0.4116 / 0.1279\), is 3.2, which is close to \(\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16\). This ratio is observed in the run, and the exact scaling is not proved on this page. For the Cauchy law, the printed range stays near 2 at both sizes. The averaging that narrows the normal case does not narrow the Cauchy case in this run.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A heavy-tailed law produces large values often.”

    Proposition 1.4 at \(x = 10\) gives \[P(|C| > 10) = 0.063451\], which Lab 1, Part A prints. That is large compared with the normal's \(1.5240 \times 10^{-23}\), but it is not frequent in absolute terms. Heavy tails are a statement about the limit of \(e^{cx}P(|X| > x)\) as \(x\) grows, for every \(c > 0\) (Definition 1.1), and not about the size of one tail probability.

  2. “Averaging any sample reduces its spread by the factor \(\sqrt n\).”

    The factor \(\sqrt n\) is the scaling of Lesson 9 for finite variance. For the standard Cauchy law the interquartile range of one draw is exactly 2 (Lab 1, Part C), and the printed interquartile ranges of the sample mean are 1.8220 at \(n = 10\) and 1.8838 at \(n = 100\). If the range shrank by \(\sqrt n\) as in Lesson 9, it would be about \(1.8220/\sqrt{10} = 0.576\) at \(n = 100\). The printed value is 1.8838, so in this run it did not shrink.

  3. “The Cauchy law has a mean of 0, by symmetry, so the sample mean estimates 0.”

    Symmetry gives \(P(C > x) = P(C < -x)\), but Proposition 2.1 shows \(E|C| = \infty\), so the expectation \(E[C]\) is not defined. Symmetry does not supply a mean when the integral does not converge absolutely. The Cauchy path in Lab 1, Part B, has running mean \(-57.9246\) at \(n = 10\) and \(+0.9919\) at \(n = 10000\), so this one path has not settled at 0.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · The Cauchy tail at one

Find \(P(|C| > 1)\) exactly.

Solution

By Proposition 1.4 with \(x = 1\), \(P(|C| > 1) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 1 = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac12\). This is the exact value, and it agrees with the symmetry of \(C\) about 0 and with \(F_C(1) = \tfrac34\).

M2 · The Student-t(3) distribution function at \(\sqrt3\)

Evaluate \(F_V(\sqrt3)\) exactly.

Solution

Here \(x/\sqrt3 = 1\) and \(x^2/3 = 1\). So \[F_V(\sqrt3) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi(\arctan 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi(\tfrac\pi4 + \tfrac12) = \tfrac34 + \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\], which is about 0.9092.

M3 · The two-sided Student-t(3) tail at zero

Use Proposition 1.4 to find \(\lim_{x \to 0^+} P(|V| > x)\), and check the limit against \(P(|V| > 0) = 1\).

Solution

As \(x \to 0^+\), \(\sqrt3/x \to \infty\), so \(\arctan(\sqrt3/x) \to \tfrac\pi2\), and \(\tfrac{x\sqrt3}{3 + x^2} \to 0\). Therefore \(P(|V| > x) \to \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac\pi2 = 1\). This is the value that \(P(|V| > 0) = 1\) requires, because the t(3) law has a density and so no atom at 0.

Proof

P1 · The inequality \(\arctan u \ge u - u^3/3\)

Prove that \(\arctan u \ge u - u^3/3\) for \(u \ge 0\), using the derivative of \(h(u) = \arctan u - u + u^3/3\).

Solution

\[h'(u) = \frac{1}{1 + u^2} - 1 + u^2 = \frac{1 - (1 + u^2) + u^2(1 + u^2)}{1 + u^2} = \frac{u^4}{1 + u^2}\], which is nonnegative. So \(h\) is nondecreasing on \([0, \infty)\), and \(h(0) = 0\). Hence \(h(u) \ge 0\), which is the inequality.

P2 · The Cauchy median and quartiles

Using \(F_C\) from Proposition 1.3, find the 25th and 75th percentiles of \(C\) and their difference.

Solution

\(F_C(q) = \tfrac14\) gives \(\arctan q = -\tfrac\pi4\), so \(q = -1\). \(F_C(q) = \tfrac34\) gives \(\arctan q = \tfrac\pi4\), so \(q = 1\). The difference is \[1 - (-1) = 2\], which is the interquartile range used in Lab 1, Part C.

P3 · The integral test for the Cauchy mean

Show directly that \(\int_1^\infty \frac{x}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \infty\), and deduce that \(E|C| = \infty\).

Solution

With \(v = 1 + x^2\), \(dv = 2x\,dx\), so \[\int_1^M \frac{x}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \tfrac12\int_2^{1 + M^2} \frac{dv}{v} = \tfrac12(\ln(1 + M^2) - \ln 2)\], which tends to \(\infty\) as \(M \to \infty\). Since \[E|C| = 2\int_0^\infty x f_C(x)\,dx \ge \tfrac{2}{\pi}\int_1^\infty \frac{x}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \infty\], \(E|C| = \infty\).

Empirical

E1 · The scaled Cauchy tail at ten (Lab 1)

Lab 1, Part D, prints \[10\,P(|C| > 10) = 0.634510\]. Verify it from Proposition 1.4, and state how it compares with the limit \(2/\pi\).

Solution

Proposition 1.4 gives \[P(|C| > 10) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan(0.1)\]. Since \(\arctan(0.1) = 0.0996687\), this is \(0.0634510\), and \(10\) times it is \(0.634510\), which the lab prints. The limit is \(2/\pi = 0.636620\), so the printed value at \(x = 10\) is about 0.002 below the limit, and the printed value at \(x = 1000\) is 0.636620.

E2 · The Cauchy interquartile range against its exact value (Lab 1)

Lab 1, Part C, prints interquartile ranges of 1.8220 at \(n = 10\) and 1.8838 at \(n = 100\) for the Cauchy sample mean, and it prints the exact range of one draw as 2. Say what the printed numbers do and do not show.

Solution

Both printed sample ranges are within 0.2 of 2 in this run, so in this run the sample mean's spread does not shrink with \(n\). The two numbers come from 400 replications with one seed for each size, and no standard error is printed for them. They do not show the exact range of the sample mean for any \(n\). Problem P2 gives the exact range of one draw, and Remark 2.5 is the only statement on this page about the law of the sample mean, which is not proved here.

E3 · The Student-t(3) standardized variance (Lab 1)

Lab 1, Part C, prints a variance of \(\sqrt n\,\bar X_n/\sqrt3\) equal to 0.8807 at \(n = 10\) and 1.1987 at \(n = 100\). State the value that the finite-variance calculation of Lesson 9 gives for this variance, say whether this page proves it, and say what the printed values can and cannot show.

Solution

Under the finite-variance calculation of Lesson 9, Proposition 1.2, \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \operatorname{Var}(V)/n = 3/n\), with \(\operatorname{Var}(V) = 3\) from Remark 2.2, so \(\operatorname{Var}(\sqrt n\,\bar X_n/\sqrt3) = n \cdot (3/n)/3 = 1\). Proposition 1.2 is proved on this page for finitely valued laws only (Lesson 9), and \(V\) is not finitely valued, so the value 1 is not proved here. The printed values can differ from 1 through sampling error alone, since each is the sample variance of 400 replications, and the lab prints no standard error, so this page does not measure that error.

Research

R1 · An exponential tail is light

Let \(X\) have \[P(|X| > x) = e^{-x}\] for \(x \ge 0\). Decide, with proof, whether \(X\) is heavy-tailed in the sense of Definition 1.1.

Solution

Take \(c = \tfrac12\). Then \[e^{cx}P(|X| > x) = e^{-x/2} \to 0\], not \(\infty\). So Definition 1.1 fails for this \(c\), and \(X\) is light-tailed. The definition requires the limit to be infinite for every \(c > 0\), and one failing \(c\) is enough to make the law light-tailed in this lesson's sense.

R2 · The Student-t(3) variance and tail together

Using Remark 2.2 and Proposition 2.3, state which of \(E[V^2]\) and \(E[V^4]\) are finite, and say which of the claims of this lesson would change if \(V\) had infinite variance.

Solution

\(E[V^2] = 3\) is finite (Remark 2.2), and \(E[V^4] = \infty\) (Proposition 2.3). This page does not settle whether \(E|V|^3\) is finite, and it does not claim the general range of finite moments. The claim that the scaled sample mean is standardized by \(\sqrt3\) would change if the variance were infinite, because the standardization needs a finite \(\sigma\). The heavy-tail claim of Proposition 1.5(b) uses only the density, so it would not change.

R3 · Why the Cauchy case is not a counterexample to Lesson 9

Explain which hypothesis of Theorem 1.3 of Lesson 9 fails for the Cauchy law, and why the failure is not repaired by taking a larger sample.

Solution

Theorem 1.3 needs a finite variance \(\sigma^2\), and Proposition 1.2 needs a finite mean \(\mu\) for \(E[\bar X_n] = \mu\). Proposition 2.1 shows \(E|C| = \infty\), so the Cauchy law has no mean and its variance is infinite. A larger sample does not change the law of each draw, so the hypothesis remains false at every \(n\). The theorem does not claim anything about a law that violates its hypothesis, so the case is outside it, not a counterexample.
