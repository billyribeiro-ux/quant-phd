Sequences, Cauchy Sequences and Series

  • Uses: the completeness of \(\R\) (Lesson 1, Definition 1.1), the Archimedean property (Lesson 1, Theorem 1.2), and the geometric series of M1 (geometric series)
  • Introduces: Cauchy sequence (defined in §2)
  • Ttheorem, proved on the page
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A sequence can be checked for convergence without knowing its limit. Its terms must eventually stay close to one another, and in the real numbers this property is enough: the terms then converge to a limit. This lesson defines convergence and the Cauchy property, proves that convergence implies the Cauchy property, and proves that in \(\R\) the converse holds, using the completeness axiom of Lesson 1. It then applies this to series through their partial sums (Definition 3.1, Section 3). Two examples follow: the harmonic series, whose partial sums fail the Cauchy condition of Corollary 3.2 (§3), and the geometric series, whose partial sums satisfy it from a computable index. Lab 1 checks that condition on both.

1Sequences and convergence

A sequence of real numbers is a list \((s_1, s_2, s_3, \dots)\), written \((s_n)\), with one real number for each natural number \(n\). Convergence of a sequence is defined as follows.

Definition 1.1 (convergence of a sequence)

A sequence \((s_n)\) converges to \(L \in \R\), written \(s_n \to L\), when for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(N \in \N\) such that \(|s_n - L| < \varepsilon\) for every \(n \ge N\).

This lesson defines the limit of a sequence directly, in Definition 1.1, and does not take it from M2. Proposition 1.2 shows that the limit of a convergent sequence is unique, so the notation \(\lim s_n\) is meaningful when the limit exists.

Proposition 1.2 (uniqueness of the limit)
  1. (H1) \(s_n \to L\) and \(s_n \to L'\), for real numbers \(L\) and \(L'\).

Conclusion: \(L = L'\).

Proof

Let \(d = |L - L'|\) and suppose \(d > 0\). Take \(\varepsilon = d/2\). By (H1) there are \(N_1\) and \(N_2\) with \(|s_n - L| < \varepsilon\) for \(n \ge N_1\) and \(|s_n - L'| < \varepsilon\) for \(n \ge N_2\). For \(n = \max(N_1, N_2)\), the triangle inequality gives \(d = |L - L'| \le |L - s_n| + |s_n - L'| < 2\varepsilon = d\), which is impossible. So \(d = 0\), and \(L = L'\). \(\blacksquare\)

2Cauchy sequences and the completeness of \(\R\)

Definition 2.1 (Cauchy sequence)

A sequence \((s_n)\) of real numbers is a Cauchy sequence when for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(N \in \N\) such that \(|s_m - s_n| < \varepsilon\) for all \(m, n \ge N\). The index \(N\) depends on \(\varepsilon\), and nothing in the definition refers to a limit.

Proposition 2.2 (convergent implies Cauchy)
  1. (H1) \((s_n)\) is a sequence of real numbers with \(s_n \to L\) for some \(L \in \R\).

Conclusion: \((s_n)\) is a Cauchy sequence.

Proof

Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). By (H1), with \(\varepsilon/2\) in Definition 1.1, there is \(N\) with \(|s_n - L| < \varepsilon/2\) for \(n \ge N\). For \(m, n \ge N\), \(|s_m - s_n| \le |s_m - L| + |L - s_n| < \varepsilon/2 + \varepsilon/2 = \varepsilon\). \(\blacksquare\)

A sequence \((s_n)\) of real numbers is bounded when there is a real number \(C\) with \(|s_n| \le C\) for every \(n\). A set of real numbers is bounded below when some real number \(m\) satisfies \(m \le b\) for every member \(b\) of the set; such an \(m\) is a lower bound of the set. Bounded above is defined in Lesson 1, Section 1.

Lemma 2.3 (Cauchy implies bounded)
  1. (H1) \((s_n)\) is a Cauchy sequence of real numbers.

Conclusion: there is a real number \(C\) with \(|s_n| \le C\) for every \(n\).

Proof

Apply Definition 2.1 with \(\varepsilon = 1\) to get \(N\) with \(|s_n - s_N| < 1\) for \(n \ge N\). For such \(n\), \(|s_n| < |s_N| + 1\). Put \(C = \max\{|s_1|, \dots, |s_N|, |s_N| + 1\}\), a finite maximum. Then \(|s_n| \le C\) for \(n < N\) by the choice of \(C\), and for \(n \ge N\) by the inequality just shown. \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 2.4 (greatest lower bound)
  1. (H1) \(B\) is a nonempty set of real numbers that is bounded below.
  2. (H2) Every nonempty subset of \(\R\) that is bounded above has a supremum in \(\R\) (Lesson 1, Definition 1.1).

Conclusion: \(B\) has a greatest lower bound \(L\) in \(\R\), so \(L \le b\) for every \(b \in B\), and for every \(\eta > 0\) some \(b \in B\) satisfies \(b < L + \eta\).

Proof

Let \(D = \{-b : b \in B\}\), which is nonempty. If \(m\) is a lower bound of \(B\), then \(-m\) is an upper bound of \(D\), so \(D\) is bounded above. By (H2), \(t = \sup D\) exists. For \(b \in B\), \(-b \le t\), so \(-t \le b\); hence \(-t\) is a lower bound of \(B\). If \(u\) is any lower bound of \(B\), then \(-u\) is an upper bound of \(D\), so \(t \le -u\), that is, \(u \le -t\). So \(L = -t\) is the greatest lower bound. For \(\eta > 0\), the number \(L + \eta\) is not a lower bound of \(B\), since \(L\) is the greatest one, so some \(b \in B\) satisfies \(b < L + \eta\). \(\blacksquare\)

Theorem 2.5 (Cauchy criterion for real sequences)
  1. (H1) \((s_n)\) is a Cauchy sequence of real numbers.
  2. (H2) Every nonempty subset of \(\R\) that is bounded above has a supremum in \(\R\) (Lesson 1, Definition 1.1).

Conclusion: \((s_n)\) converges to some \(L \in \R\).

Proof

By Lemma 2.3 there is \(C\) with \(|s_n| \le C\) for all \(n\). For \(k \in \N\), the set \(\{s_n : n \ge k\}\) is nonempty and bounded above by \(C\), so by (H2) it has a supremum \(b_k\). Each \(s_n\) with \(n \ge k + 1\) is also in the set for \(k\), so \(b_{k+1} \le b_k\). Also \(b_k \ge s_k \ge -C\). So \(B = \{b_k : k \in \N\}\) is nonempty and bounded below, and Lemma 2.4 gives its greatest lower bound \(L\).

Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). By (H1), choose \(N\) with \(|s_m - s_n| < \varepsilon/3\) for all \(m, n \ge N\). Since \(L + \varepsilon/3\) is not a lower bound of \(B\), there is \(K\) with \(b_K < L + \varepsilon/3\). Let \(n \ge \max(N, K)\).

Upper bound. Since \(s_n\) lies in the set whose supremum is \(b_n\), and \(b_n \le b_K\) because \(n \ge K\), we get \(s_n \le b_n \le b_K < L + \varepsilon/3\).

Lower bound. Since \(b_n\) is a supremum, the number \(b_n - \varepsilon/3\) is less than \(b_n\), so it is not an upper bound of \(\{s_m : m \ge n\}\). Hence some \(m \ge n\) satisfies \(s_m > b_n - \varepsilon/3\). Since \(L\) is a lower bound of \(B\), \(b_n \ge L\), so \(s_m > L - \varepsilon/3\). Both \(m\) and \(n\) are at least \(N\), so \(|s_n - s_m| < \varepsilon/3\), and \(s_n > s_m - \varepsilon/3 > L - 2\varepsilon/3\).

Together, \(L - 2\varepsilon/3 < s_n < L + \varepsilon/3\), so \(|s_n - L| < \varepsilon\) for every \(n \ge \max(N, K)\). Hence \(s_n \to L\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.6 (where completeness is used)

Proposition 2.2 and Lemma 2.3 use no property of \(\R\) beyond the order and arithmetic of real numbers. (H2) is used in Lemma 2.4 and in Theorem 2.5. The proof of Theorem 2.5 uses (H2) twice: once directly, to form the suprema \(b_k\), and once through Lemma 2.4, to form the greatest lower bound \(L\) of their set. Without (H2), the argument has no candidate for \(L\).

3Series and the harmonic and geometric examples

Definition 3.1 (series and partial sums)

For a sequence \((a_n)\) of real numbers, the partial sums are \(S_N = a_1 + a_2 + \dots + a_N\). The series \(\sum_{n} a_n\) converges when the sequence \((S_N)\) converges, and its sum is the limit of \((S_N)\).

Corollary 3.2 (Cauchy criterion for series)
  1. (H1) \((a_n)\) is a sequence of real numbers, and \(S_N\) are its partial sums as in Definition 3.1.
  2. (H2) Every nonempty subset of \(\R\) that is bounded above has a supremum in \(\R\) (Lesson 1, Definition 1.1).

Conclusion: \(\sum_n a_n\) converges if and only if for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(N\) with \(|a_{n+1} + a_{n+2} + \dots + a_m| < \varepsilon\) for all \(m > n \ge N\).

Proof

For \(m > n\), \(S_m - S_n = a_{n+1} + \dots + a_m\). The series converges exactly when \((S_N)\) converges. By Proposition 2.2 and Theorem 2.5, \((S_N)\) converges exactly when it is Cauchy, which by Definition 2.1 says that for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(N\) with \(|S_m - S_n| < \varepsilon\) for all \(m, n \ge N\). Since \(|S_m - S_n| = |a_{n+1} + \dots + a_m|\) for \(m > n\), this is the stated condition. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.3 (harmonic partial sums are not Cauchy)
  1. (H1) \(H_n = 1 + 1/2 + \dots + 1/n\) for integers \(n \ge 1\).
  2. (H2) Every nonempty subset of \(\R\) that is bounded above has a supremum in \(\R\) (Lesson 1, Definition 1.1). Only the consequence on divergence uses it, through Corollary 3.2.

Conclusion: \(H_{2N} - H_N \ge 1/2\) for every integer \(N \ge 1\). Consequently the harmonic series diverges.

Proof

The difference \(H_{2N} - H_N\) is the sum of the \(N\) terms \(1/(N+1), 1/(N+2), \dots, 1/(2N)\). Each term is at least \(1/(2N)\), because its denominator is at most \(2N\). So the sum is at least \(N \cdot 1/(2N) = 1/2\). For the consequence, suppose the series converged. Then by Corollary 3.2, for \(\varepsilon = 1/2\) there would be \(N\) with \(|H_m - H_n| < 1/2\) for all \(m > n \ge N\). Taking \(n = N\) and \(m = 2N\) contradicts the inequality just proved. \(\blacksquare\)

Lemma 3.4 (a geometric tail)
  1. (H1) \(G_N = 1/2 + 1/4 + \dots + 1/2^N\) for integers \(N \ge 0\), with \(G_0 = 0\).
  2. (H2) \(n\) and \(m\) are integers with \(0 \le n < m\).

Conclusion: \(G_m - G_n = 1/2^n - 1/2^m\), and therefore \(0 < G_m - G_n < 1/2^n\).

Proof

For every integer \(k \ge 1\), \(1/2^{k-1} - 1/2^k = 1/2^k\), because \(1/2^{k-1} = 2/2^k\). So \(G_m - G_n = \sum_{k=n+1}^{m} 1/2^k = \sum_{k=n+1}^{m} (1/2^{k-1} - 1/2^k)\). This sum telescopes to \(1/2^n - 1/2^m\). Since \(m > n\), \(1/2^m > 0\), so \(G_m - G_n < 1/2^n\). Also \(1/2^n - 1/2^m > 0\) because \(2^m > 2^n\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 3.5 (the geometric threshold for \(\varepsilon = 2^{-10}\))
  1. (H1) \(G_N\) as in Lemma 3.4, and \(\varepsilon = 1/2^{10}\).
  2. (H2) \(N\) is an integer with \(N \ge 0\).

Conclusion: \(|G_m - G_n| < \varepsilon\) for all \(m > n \ge N\) if and only if \(N \ge 10\).

Proof

Suppose \(N \ge 10\). For \(m > n \ge N\), Lemma 3.4 gives \(|G_m - G_n| < 1/2^n \le 1/2^N \le 1/2^{10} = \varepsilon\). Suppose \(N \le 9\). Take \(n = N\) and \(m = 10\), which satisfy \(m > n \ge N\). By Lemma 3.4, \(G_{10} - G_N = 1/2^N - 1/2^{10} \ge 1/2^9 - 1/2^{10} = 1/2^{10} = \varepsilon\). So the condition fails for this pair. \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · The Cauchy test on harmonic and geometric partial sums

"""M5 lab L02: the Cauchy test on partial sums, for a series that diverges and one that converges.

Standard library only. No randomness and no network. All partial sums are exact fractions.Fraction
values. The test for a tolerance eps asks whether |S_m - S_n| < eps for all m, n >= N, and the lab
checks it on finite ranges. A failure found in a finite range is a counterexample to the test
holding from N on, and it is exact. A pass in a finite range is evidence only for that range. The
proofs on the page establish the claims for all indices: Proposition 3.3 for the harmonic gaps,
Lemma 3.4 for the geometric identity, and Proposition 3.5 for the threshold N = 10.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections.abc import Callable
from fractions import Fraction

HARMONIC_MAX_N = 200
GEOMETRIC_MAX_N = 20
GEOMETRIC_PAIR_LIMIT = 60
EPS_HARMONIC = Fraction(1, 2)
EPS_GEOMETRIC = Fraction(1, 1024)


def harmonic_partial(n: int) -> Fraction:
    """S_n = 1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/n for n >= 1, computed exactly."""
    return sum((Fraction(1, k) for k in range(1, n + 1)), Fraction(0))


def geometric_partial(n: int) -> Fraction:
    """G_n = 1/2 + 1/4 + ... + 1/2^n for n >= 0, computed exactly. Here G_n = 1 - 1/2^n."""
    return sum((Fraction(1, 2**k) for k in range(1, n + 1)), Fraction(0))


def cauchy_test_fails_from(partial: Callable[[int], Fraction], start: int, eps: Fraction, limit: int) -> bool:
    """True when some pair start <= n < m <= limit has |S_m - S_n| >= eps, so the test fails from start."""
    for n in range(start, limit + 1):
        for m in range(n + 1, limit + 1):
            if abs(partial(m) - partial(n)) >= eps:
                return True
    return False


def main() -> None:
    # Harmonic series: eps = 1/2 fails at each N in 1..HARMONIC_MAX_N; the pair (N, 2N) witnesses it.
    gaps = [harmonic_partial(2 * n) - harmonic_partial(n) for n in range(1, HARMONIC_MAX_N + 1)]
    print(f"harmonic partial sums, pairs (N, 2N) for N = 1 to {HARMONIC_MAX_N}")
    print(f"gaps S_2N - S_N that are at least 1/2: {sum(g >= EPS_HARMONIC for g in gaps)} of {len(gaps)}")
    print(f"smallest gap S_2N - S_N over these N: {min(gaps)}, at N = {gaps.index(min(gaps)) + 1}")
    failing = [n for n in range(1, HARMONIC_MAX_N + 1) if harmonic_partial(2 * n) - harmonic_partial(n) >= EPS_HARMONIC]
    print(
        f"indices N where the test with eps = 1/2 fails, N = 1 to {HARMONIC_MAX_N}: {len(failing)} of {HARMONIC_MAX_N}"
    )

    # Geometric series: exact identity for the tail, then the first index from which the test passes.
    identity = 0
    pairs = 0
    for n in range(0, GEOMETRIC_PAIR_LIMIT):
        for m in range(n + 1, GEOMETRIC_PAIR_LIMIT + 1):
            pairs += 1
            identity += geometric_partial(m) - geometric_partial(n) == Fraction(1, 2**n) - Fraction(1, 2**m)
    print(f"identity G_m - G_n = 1/2^n - 1/2^m for 0 <= n < m <= {GEOMETRIC_PAIR_LIMIT}: {identity} of {pairs} pairs")
    passing = [
        n
        for n in range(0, GEOMETRIC_MAX_N + 1)
        if not cauchy_test_fails_from(geometric_partial, n, EPS_GEOMETRIC, GEOMETRIC_PAIR_LIMIT)
    ]
    first = passing[0] if passing else -1
    print(f"first index N from which the test with eps = 1/1024 passes, N = 0 to {GEOMETRIC_MAX_N}: {first}")
    failing_geo = [n for n in range(0, GEOMETRIC_MAX_N + 1) if n not in passing]
    print(f"indices N in 0 to {GEOMETRIC_MAX_N} where it fails: {failing_geo[0]} to {failing_geo[-1]}")


main()

smallest gap S_2N - S_N over these N: 1/2, at N = 1

The count line and the minimum line confirm Proposition 3.3 on the stated range: each gap is at least \(1/2\), and the minimum, \(1/2\), is at \(N = 1\) (the printed minimum is over \(N\) from 1 to 200 only). 4Where the canon breaks

For real sequences, a sequence is Cauchy exactly when it converges: Proposition 2.2 gives the forward direction, and Theorem 2.5 gives the converse. Each of the following shows what fails without its parts.

  • A Cauchy sequence of rational numbers need not converge to a rational number. Let \(a_n\) be the left endpoints of the bisection brackets of Lesson 1 (Theorem 1.6, applied to the set \(S\) of Lesson 1, with \(a_0 = 0\) and \(b_0 = 2\)). Each \(a_n\) is rational. Each bisection step replaces one endpoint by a point of the current interval, so the brackets are nested and \(a_n \le a_m \le b_m \le b_n\) for \(m \ge n\). Hence \(|a_m - a_n| \le b_n - a_n = 2/2^n\) for \(m \ge n\). Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), choose \(N\) with \(2/2^N < \varepsilon\); then for \(m \ge n \ge N\), \(|a_m - a_n| \le 2/2^n \le 2/2^N < \varepsilon\), so \((a_n)\) is Cauchy. By Theorem 2.5 it converges to some real number \(L\). Theorem 1.6 gives \(a_n \le \sup S \le b_n\), so \(|a_n - \sup S| \le 2/2^n\). Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), the \(N\) chosen above gives \(2/2^n \le 2/2^N < \varepsilon\) for \(n \ge N\), so \(a_n \to \sup S\). By Proposition 1.2 the limit is \(L = \sup S\). If \(L\) were rational, then \(L^2 = 2\) by Lesson 1, Proposition 1.7, which is impossible for a rational number (M1, Lesson 2, Corollary 2.6). So the rational Cauchy sequence \((a_n)\) has no rational limit.
  • The terms of a series can tend to zero while the series diverges. The terms \(1/n\) tend to zero by Problem M1, and the harmonic series diverges by Proposition 3.3. Tending to zero is necessary for convergence, but it is not sufficient.
  • A sequence of real numbers that is bounded need not be Cauchy. The sequence \(s_n = (-1)^n\) is bounded by 1 but is not Cauchy (Problem M2). Lemma 2.3 gives the other direction only: a Cauchy sequence is bounded.

5Misconceptions

  1. “If the terms of a series tend to zero, the series converges.”

    The terms \(1/n\) tend to zero, yet Proposition 3.3 shows that the partial sums of \(\sum 1/n\) are not Cauchy, so the series diverges. Tending to zero is necessary for convergence (take \(m = n + 1\) in Corollary 3.2), but it is not sufficient.

  2. “If consecutive terms get closer together, the sequence is Cauchy.”

    Let \(s_n = \sqrt{n}\). Then \(|s_{n+1} - s_n| = 1/(\sqrt{n+1} + \sqrt{n}) \le 1/\sqrt{n+1}\), which tends to zero. But \(s_n\) is unbounded: for any real \(M\), Lesson 1, Theorem 1.2 gives \(n\) with \(n > M^2\), so \(\sqrt{n} > M\). A Cauchy sequence is bounded by Lemma 2.3, so \((\sqrt{n})\) is not Cauchy. So small consecutive gaps do not make a sequence Cauchy.

  3. “A Cauchy sequence of rational numbers has a limit among the rational numbers.”

    The bisection sequence \((a_n)\) of the first canon break is a Cauchy sequence of rational numbers whose limit \(\sup S\) is not rational. Theorem 2.5 gives a real limit, and the limit's membership in the rational numbers is a separate question that the Cauchy property does not decide.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · The sequence \(1/n\) converges to 0

Prove that \(1/n \to 0\) using Definition 1.1 and the Archimedean property.

Solution

Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). By Lesson 1, Theorem 1.2, there is \(N \in \N\) with \(N > 1/\varepsilon\). For \(n \ge N\), \(|1/n - 0| = 1/n \le 1/N < \varepsilon\). So \(1/n \to 0\).

M2 · The alternating sequence is not Cauchy

Show that \(s_n = (-1)^n\) is not a Cauchy sequence.

Solution

Take \(\varepsilon = 1\). For any \(N\), the indices \(n = N\) and \(m = N + 1\) are both at least \(N\), and \(s_m - s_n\) is \(\pm 2\), so \(|s_m - s_n| = 2 \ge 1\). No \(N\) works for \(\varepsilon = 1\), so the sequence is not Cauchy.

M3 · Lemma 3.4 for one pair

Check Lemma 3.4 for \(n = 1\) and \(m = 3\), using the values \(G_1\) and \(G_3\).

Solution

\(G_1 = 1/2\) and \(G_3 = 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 = 7/8\), so \(G_3 - G_1 = 3/8\). The identity gives \(1/2^1 - 1/2^3 = 1/2 - 1/8 = 3/8\), which agrees. The bound gives \(3/8 < 1/2^1 = 1/2\), which also holds.

Proof

P1 · Sums of Cauchy sequences

Let \((a_n)\) and \((b_n)\) be Cauchy sequences of real numbers. Prove that \((a_n + b_n)\) is a Cauchy sequence.

Solution

Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Choose \(N_1\) with \(|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon/2\) for \(m, n \ge N_1\), and \(N_2\) with \(|b_m - b_n| < \varepsilon/2\) for \(m, n \ge N_2\). For \(m, n \ge \max(N_1, N_2)\), \(|(a_m + b_m) - (a_n + b_n)| \le |a_m - a_n| + |b_m - b_n| < \varepsilon\). So the sum is Cauchy.

P2 · Sums of convergent sequences

Let \(s_n \to L\) and \(t_n \to L'\). Prove that \(s_n + t_n \to L + L'\).

Solution

Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Choose \(N_1\) with \(|s_n - L| < \varepsilon/2\) for \(n \ge N_1\), and \(N_2\) with \(|t_n - L'| < \varepsilon/2\) for \(n \ge N_2\). For \(n \ge \max(N_1, N_2)\), \(|(s_n + t_n) - (L + L')| \le |s_n - L| + |t_n - L'| < \varepsilon\).

P3 · A Cauchy sequence of integers is eventually constant

Let \((z_n)\) be a Cauchy sequence whose terms are all integers. Prove that there is \(N\) such that \(z_n = z_N\) for all \(n \ge N\).

Solution

Apply Definition 2.1 with \(\varepsilon = 1\) to get \(N\) with \(|z_m - z_n| < 1\) for all \(m, n \ge N\). The difference \(z_m - z_n\) is an integer with absolute value less than 1, so it is 0. Hence \(z_n = z_N\) for all \(n \ge N\).

Empirical

E1 · The count of failing indices for the harmonic test

Lab 1 prints 200 of 200 for the gaps \(H_{2N} - H_N\) with \(N\) from 1 to 200. Explain the count from Proposition 3.3, and say which index pair witnesses the failure at each \(N\).

Solution

Proposition 3.3 gives \(H_{2N} - H_N \ge 1/2\) for every \(N \ge 1\), so each of the 200 gaps is at least \(1/2\). The witness at index \(N\) is the pair \(n = N\), \(m = 2N\): both are at least \(N\), and \(|H_m - H_n| \ge 1/2 = \varepsilon\). So the test with \(\varepsilon = 1/2\) fails at every \(N\) in the range, and the count is 200.

E2 · The two smallest-gap values by hand

Lab 1 prints the smallest gap \(H_{2N} - H_N\) over \(N\) from 1 to 200 as \(1/2\), at \(N = 1\). Compute the gaps for \(N = 1\) and \(N = 2\) by hand, and say what the printed minimum does and does not establish.

Solution

For \(N = 1\), \(H_2 - H_1 = 1/2\). For \(N = 2\), \(H_4 - H_2 = 1/3 + 1/4 = 7/12\), which is larger than \(1/2\). The printed minimum over \(N = 1\) to \(200\) is \(1/2\), at \(N = 1\), and that agrees with the computed values. The minimum is over the printed range only. The bound \(1/2\) for all \(N\) comes from Proposition 3.3, not from the minimum.

E3 · The count of pairs in the identity check

Lab 1 checks the identity of Lemma 3.4 on pairs with \(0 \le n < m \le 60\), and prints 1830 of 1830. Explain the number 1830 and confirm it.

Solution

For each \(n\) from 0 to 59, the number of \(m\) with \(n < m \le 60\) is \(60 - n\). The total is \(\sum_{n=0}^{59} (60 - n) = \sum_{j=1}^{60} j = 60 \cdot 61/2 = 1830\). The lab checks each of these pairs exactly, and all of them satisfy the identity, which agrees with Lemma 3.4 on this range.

Research

R1 · What a finite search cannot show

Explain why Lab 1's search over \(m \le 60\) cannot by itself establish that the geometric partial sums are Cauchy from \(N = 10\) onward, and identify the step of this lesson that does establish it.

Solution

The search covers only the finitely many pairs with \(m \le 60\). The Cauchy condition at \(N = 10\) concerns every pair \(m > n \ge 10\), and there are infinitely many such pairs. The step that covers all of them is Lemma 3.4: for every \(m > n\), \(|G_m - G_n| < 1/2^n\), which is at most \(1/2^{10}\) when \(n \ge 10\). Proposition 3.5 then gives the threshold from this bound.

R2 · The harmonic partial sums are unbounded

Using Proposition 3.3, prove that \(H_{2^k} \ge 1 + k/2\) for every integer \(k \ge 0\), and conclude that the sequence \((H_n)\) is not bounded.

Solution

For \(k = 0\), \(H_1 = 1 \ge 1\). For the step, \(H_{2^{k+1}} - H_{2^k} \ge 1/2\) by Proposition 3.3 with \(N = 2^k\). Adding these for \(j = 0\) to \(k - 1\) gives \(H_{2^k} - H_1 \ge k/2\), so \(H_{2^k} \ge 1 + k/2\). Since \(k\) can be any nonnegative integer, \((H_n)\) is not bounded. Lemma 2.3 says a Cauchy sequence is bounded, so this gives a second proof that \((H_n)\) is not Cauchy.

R3 · A geometric decay bound gives a Cauchy sequence

Let \((a_n)\) satisfy \(|a_{n+1} - a_n| \le c\beta^n\) for all \(n\), where \(c > 0\) and \(0 < \beta < 1\). Show that \((a_n)\) is Cauchy, and that \(|a_m - a_n| \le c\beta^n/(1 - \beta)\) for \(m > n\). State the hypothesis of M1's geometric-sum formula that you use.

Solution

For \(m > n\), the triangle inequality gives \(|a_m - a_n| \le \sum_{k=n}^{m-1} |a_{k+1} - a_k| \le c\sum_{k=n}^{m-1} \beta^k\). The finite geometric sum formula of M1 (Lesson 6, Theorem 2.2), which needs \(\beta \ne 1\), gives \(\sum_{k=n}^{m-1} \beta^k = \beta^n(1 - \beta^{m-n})/(1 - \beta) \le \beta^n/(1 - \beta)\), since \(0 < \beta^{m-n} \le 1\). So \(|a_m - a_n| \le c\beta^n/(1 - \beta)\). Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), choose \(N\) with \(c\beta^N/(1 - \beta) < \varepsilon\). Such an \(N\) exists: write \(\beta = 1/(1 + h)\) with \(h > 0\). Induction on \(N\) gives \((1 + h)^N \ge 1 + Nh\), so \(\beta^N \le 1/(Nh)\) for \(N \ge 1\). Hence \(c\beta^N/(1 - \beta) < \varepsilon\) holds whenever \(N > c/(h(1 - \beta)\varepsilon)\), and Lesson 1, Theorem 1.2 supplies such an \(N\). Then, since \(\beta^n \le \beta^N\) for \(n \ge N\), \(|a_m - a_n| < \varepsilon\) for \(m > n \ge N\), and \((a_n)\) is Cauchy. Theorem 2.5 then gives convergence. The hypothesis of the formula is \(\beta \ne 1\), and it is satisfied here.
