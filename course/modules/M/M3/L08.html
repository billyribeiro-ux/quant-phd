Principal Component Analysis and Level-like, Slope-like and Curvature-like Directions

  • Uses: eigendecomposition, spectral theorem, positive-semidefinite matrix
  • Introduces: principal component analysis (PCA), level-like, slope-like and curvature-like principal directions
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Principal component analysis (PCA, Definition 1.3 of this lesson) looks for orthogonal directions that carry the largest share of the total squared movement in a data panel. The directions are the eigenvectors of a symmetric positive semidefinite matrix made from the data after each column's mean is subtracted, so the spectral theorem from Lesson 4 does the work. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, this lesson proves that the first direction carries the largest squared movement. It then checks the claims on a SIMULATED panel at six maturities from 0.25 to 10 years, with explained shares (Definition 1.3 of this lesson) computed from unrounded eigenvalues. It makes no claim about any real curve.

1Centred data and the matrix of cross-products

Let \(X\) be a real \(T \times n\) matrix with \(T \ge 2\) rows, one per observation, and \(n\) columns, one per variable. A tenor is the time to maturity, in years, of the bond whose yield is measured. In this lesson the variables are yield changes at \(n\) tenors, and a yield change is the difference between two dates' yields, measured in basis points (bp, where \(1\) bp is \(0.01\) of a percentage point). Let \(\bar x = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t} x_t\) be the vector of column means, where \(x_t\) is row \(t\) written as a column. The centred data are the rows \(\tilde x_t = x_t - \bar x\), and \(\tilde X\) is the matrix with these rows. Every column of \(\tilde X\) has mean zero. Define \[ M = \frac{1}{T - 1}\, \tilde X^{\top}\tilde X \in \R^{n \times n}. \] The trace \(\tr\) of a square matrix is as in Lesson 6, Proposition 2.4.

Proposition 1.1 (M is symmetric and positive semidefinite)

Let \(X\) be a real \(T \times n\) matrix with \(T \ge 2\). Then \(M\) is symmetric, and \(x^{\top}Mx \ge 0\) for every \(x \in \R^n\).

Proof

Since \((\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X)^{\top} = \tilde X^{\top}\tilde X\), the matrix \(M\) is symmetric. For \(x \in \R^n\), \(x^{\top}Mx = \frac{1}{T-1}(\tilde X x)^{\top}(\tilde X x) = \frac{1}{T-1}\|\tilde X x\|^2 \ge 0\).

Proposition 1.2 (the trace is the total squared movement)

For the matrix \(M\) of Proposition 1.1, \(\tr(M) = \dfrac{1}{T - 1}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \|\tilde x_t\|^2\).

Proof

The \(j\)th diagonal entry of \(\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X\) is the sum over \(t\) of the squared \(j\)th entries of \(\tilde x_t\). Summing over \(j\) gives \(\sum_t \|\tilde x_t\|^2\), and dividing by \(T - 1\) gives the claim.

Definition 1.3 (principal component analysis)

Let \(M = V\Lambda V^{\top}\) be a spectral decomposition, with \(V\) orthogonal, \(\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)\), and \(\lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_n\). Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, such a decomposition exists for every real symmetric matrix (the spectral theorem, Theorem 2.3 of that lesson). Principal component analysis of the data \(X\) consists of three objects. The principal directions are the columns \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) of \(V\). The score of observation \(t\) on component \(k\) is \(z_{tk} = \tilde x_t^{\top} v_k\). The explained share of component \(k\) is \(s_k = \lambda_k / \sum_{j} \lambda_j\), which is defined when \(\sum_j \lambda_j > 0\).

Theorem 1.4 (the scores have cross-products Lambda)

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(Z\) be the \(T \times n\) matrix with entries \(z_{tk}\), so \(Z = \tilde X V\). Then \(Z^{\top}Z/(T - 1) = \Lambda\). The off-diagonal entries of \(Z^{\top}Z\) are zero, and the diagonal entry for component \(k\) is \((T - 1)\lambda_k\). The shares sum to \(1\) whenever \(\sum_j \lambda_j > 0\).

Proof

\(Z^{\top}Z = V^{\top}\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X V = (T - 1)V^{\top}MV\). Since \(M = V\Lambda V^{\top}\) and \(V^{\top}V = I\), \(V^{\top}MV = \Lambda\), so \(Z^{\top}Z = (T - 1)\Lambda\). The shares sum to \(\sum_k \lambda_k / \sum_j \lambda_j = 1\).

2The leading direction is the best one

For a unit vector \(u\) (a vector of norm \(1\)), the squared scores along \(u\) are \(\sum_t (\tilde x_t^{\top}u)^2 = \|\tilde X u\|^2\), and by the definition of \(M\) this equals \((T - 1)\,u^{\top}Mu\). The next theorem gives the largest value of this quadratic form over unit vectors and a unit vector that attains it.

Theorem 2.1 (the leading directions maximize the quadratic form)

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(M\) be a real symmetric positive semidefinite \(n \times n\) matrix, with spectral decomposition \(M = V\Lambda V^{\top}\) as in Definition 1.3. Let \(k \in \{1, \ldots, n\}\).

  1. The maximum of \(u^{\top}Mu\) over unit vectors \(u\) is \(\lambda_1\), attained at \(u = v_1\).
  2. If \(k \ge 2\), the maximum of \(u^{\top}Mu\) over unit vectors \(u\) orthogonal to \(v_1, \ldots, v_{k-1}\) is \(\lambda_k\), attained at \(u = v_k\).
Proof

Since \(V\) is orthogonal, every \(u\) can be written as \(u = V\alpha\) with \(\alpha = V^{\top}u\), and \(\|u\| = \|\alpha\|\). Then \(u^{\top}Mu = \alpha^{\top}V^{\top}MV\alpha = \sum_i \lambda_i \alpha_i^2\), and \(\|u\|^2 = \sum_i \alpha_i^2 = 1\).

(a) \(\sum_i \lambda_i \alpha_i^2 \le \lambda_1 \sum_i \alpha_i^2 = \lambda_1\), with equality when \(\alpha = e_1\), that is \(u = v_1\). Equality holds exactly when \(\alpha_i = 0\) for every \(i\) with \(\lambda_i < \lambda_1\), because \(\lambda_1 - \sum_i \lambda_i \alpha_i^2 = \sum_i (\lambda_1 - \lambda_i) \alpha_i^2\) (using \(\sum_i \alpha_i^2 = 1\)) is a sum of nonnegative terms, and it is zero exactly when each term with \(\lambda_i < \lambda_1\) is zero.

(b) If \(u\) is orthogonal to \(v_1, \ldots, v_{k-1}\), then \(\alpha_1 = \cdots = \alpha_{k-1} = 0\), because \(\alpha_i = v_i^{\top}u\). So \(u^{\top}Mu = \sum_{i \ge k} \lambda_i \alpha_i^2 \le \lambda_k \sum_{i \ge k} \alpha_i^2 = \lambda_k\), using \(\lambda_i \le \lambda_k\) for \(i \ge k\). Equality holds at \(u = v_k\), which is orthogonal to \(v_1, \ldots, v_{k-1}\).

Corollary 2.2 (the first principal direction carries the largest squared movement)

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, for every unit vector \(u\), \(\sum_{t=1}^{T} (\tilde x_t^{\top}u)^2 = (T - 1)\,u^{\top}Mu \le (T - 1)\lambda_1\), with equality when \(u = v_1\).

Proposition 2.3 (scaling the data leaves the shares unchanged)

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(c\) be a nonzero real number and replace \(X\) by \(cX\), and suppose \(\sum_j \lambda_j \gt 0\), so that the shares of Definition 1.3 are defined. Then the matrix \(M\) becomes \(c^2M\), its eigenvalues become \(c^2\lambda_1, \ldots, c^2\lambda_n\), with the same eigenvectors, and every explained share is unchanged.

Proof

The centred data of \(cX\) are \(c\tilde X\), so the new matrix is \(\frac{1}{T-1}c^2\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X = c^2M\). If \(Mv = \lambda v\), then \((c^2M)v = c^2\lambda v\), so the eigenvectors are unchanged and the eigenvalues are multiplied by \(c^2 > 0\), which preserves their order. The new share is \(c^2\lambda_k / \sum_j c^2\lambda_j = \lambda_k / \sum_j \lambda_j\).

Remark 2.4 (rescaling one column is a different matrix)

Proposition 2.3 scales every entry by the same factor. Let \(j\) index the tenor whose column is multiplied, let \(e_j\) be the \(j\)th standard basis vector, and assume that \(M_{jl} \ne 0\) for some \(l \ne j\). Let \(c\) be a number other than \(0\) or \(1\). Multiplying column \(j\) of \(X\) by \(c\) then changes \(M\) in a way that is not a multiple of \(M\): the diagonal entry \(M_{jj}\) is multiplied by \(c^2\), and the off-diagonal entries in row and column \(j\) by \(c\). Taking \(x = e_j\) in Proposition 1.1 gives \(M_{jj} \ge 0\). We show \(M_{jj} \ne 0\) next, so \(M_{jj} > 0\). By Proposition 1.1, \(x^{\top}Mx \ge 0\) for every \(x\); if \(M_{jj} = 0\), then \(x = e_j + t e_l\) would give \(2tM_{jl} + t^2M_{ll} \ge 0\) for every \(t\), which is negative for small \(t\) of the sign opposite to \(M_{jl}\). Suppose \(M' = \kappa M\). The off-diagonal entry \(M'_{jl} = cM_{jl}\) forces \(\kappa = c\), and then \(c^2M_{jj} = cM_{jj}\) gives \(M_{jj}(c^2 - c) = 0\), which is false because \(M_{jj} > 0\) and \(c^2 \ne c\). So no single number \(\kappa\) gives \(M' = \kappa M\). For \(c = 1\) nothing changes. The shares can then change. Lab 1 shows one case, in which the first share moves when one column is multiplied by ten. The lab does not establish a general rule for the direction of such a change.

3Level, slope and curvature

For nonzero vectors \(u, v \in \R^n\), the cosine of the angle between them is \(\cos(u, v) = u^{\top}v / (\|u\|\,\|v\|)\). Let \(\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_n\) be the tenors, \(\bar\tau\) their mean, and \(\overline{\tau^{2}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tau_j^{2}\) the mean of the squared tenors. Write \(\boldsymbol{\iota}_n\) for the constant vector in \(\R^n\) whose \(n\) entries all equal \(1\), and \(\boldsymbol{\iota}_T\) for the analogous vector in \(\R^T\). Three shapes over the tenors are used below: \(\boldsymbol{\iota}_n\); the centred tenors \(\tau - \bar\tau\boldsymbol{\iota}_n\); and the centred squared tenors \(\tau^{2} - \overline{\tau^{2}}\,\boldsymbol{\iota}_n\), orthogonalized against the first two. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, orthogonalizing means subtracting from a shape its orthogonal projection onto the span of the earlier shapes (Lesson 7, Theorem 1.2). Assume that the tenors take at least three distinct values, which forces \(n \ge 3\). With exactly two distinct values, \(\tau^{2} = -ab\,\boldsymbol{\iota}_n + (a + b)\tau\) on the tenors, where \(a\) and \(b\) are the two values, so the centred squared tenors lie in the span of the first two shapes and the curvature shape is zero. With one distinct value the slope shape is zero as well. Under the three-value assumption the three shapes are nonzero and mutually orthogonal, and the earlier shapes are independent, so Lesson 7, Theorem 1.2 applies at each orthogonalization step. The curvature shape is nonzero because a relation \(\tau_j^{2} = \alpha + \beta\,\tau_j\) at every tenor would make the quadratic \(x^{2} - \beta x - \alpha\), which has leading coefficient \(1\), vanish at three distinct points, and a nonzero quadratic has at most two roots.

Definition 3.1 (level, slope and curvature shapes)

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4 and that the tenors take at least three distinct values (Section 3), let the three shapes above be normalized to norm \(1\), giving \(f_1\) (level), \(f_2\) (slope) and \(f_3\) (curvature), which are orthonormal by construction. Let \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) be the principal directions of Definition 1.3. The match of \(f_j\) is the principal direction \(v_k\) with the largest \(|\cos(v_k, f_j)|\) over \(k = 1, \ldots, n\), provided exactly one principal direction attains that largest value. If two or more principal directions attain it, \(f_j\) has no match and receives no label. A principal direction that is the match of \(f_1\), \(f_2\) or \(f_3\) is called level-like, slope-like or curvature-like, respectively. These labels, applied to principal directions, are the level-like, slope-like and curvature-like principal directions. They name each principal direction by the shape it matches, and they define no yield-curve factor. Lab 1 computes the matches on a SIMULATED panel, and they make no claim about the shape of any observed yield curve.

Lab 1 · PCA of a SIMULATED six-tenor yield-change panel

Stated before running: for a SIMULATED panel of 750 daily yield changes at six tenors (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 5 and 10 years), made from planted level, slope and curvature shapes with scales 8, 4 and 2 bp per day and independent noise of scale 0.5 bp, the matrix \(M\) is positive semidefinite, \(MV = V\Lambda\), the score cross-products equal \(\Lambda\), and the eigenvalues sum to the trace, each to \(10^{-9}\). The explained shares of the top three components are printed from unrounded eigenvalues. Each planted shape is matched to the component with the largest absolute cosine, and the three matches are distinct. Doubling every entry of the panel leaves the shares unchanged to \(10^{-9}\), and multiplying the 0.25-year column by ten changes the first share. The lab also prints the lower bound that Problem E1 derives for the new first share, with the two inputs it uses.

"""M3 L08 lab 1: principal component analysis of a SIMULATED six-tenor yield-change panel.

SIMULATED: the panel is generated here from a seeded generator (seed 8) with three planted factors, a level, a
slope and a curvature shape, and independent noise. No Treasury, Federal Reserve or other market data are read or
represented. The planted shapes and factor sizes are the generator's choices; nothing here estimates a real curve.
Units: daily changes in basis points (bp), one row per day, one column per tenor (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 5, 10 years).

The lab forms the centred data matrix Xc, the matrix M = Xc^T Xc / (T - 1), and its eigendecomposition with
numpy.linalg.eigh. It checks, to the tolerances printed: M is positive semidefinite, M V = V Lambda, the scores
Z = Xc V satisfy Z^T Z / (T - 1) = Lambda, and the eigenvalues sum to trace(M). The explained shares are computed from
unrounded eigenvalues and printed to one decimal place of a percent. Each planted shape is matched to the principal
component with the largest absolute cosine. Two scale checks show what a rescaling does to the shares. Standard
library and NumPy only. No randomness outside the seeded generator, and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np

TOL = 1e-9
TENORS = np.array([0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0])
T_DAYS = 750


def unit(v: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """Return v scaled to Euclidean norm 1."""
    scaled: np.ndarray = v / np.linalg.norm(v)
    return scaled


def planted_shapes() -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Return orthonormal level, slope and curvature shapes over the six tenors (unitless)."""
    level = unit(np.ones_like(TENORS))
    slope = unit(TENORS - TENORS.mean())
    quad = TENORS**2
    quad = quad - quad.mean()
    quad = quad - (quad @ level) * level - (quad @ slope) * slope  # remove the level and slope parts
    return level, slope, unit(quad)


def simulate_panel(rng: np.random.Generator) -> np.ndarray:
    """Return a T x 6 SIMULATED panel of daily yield changes in bp: planted factors plus noise."""
    level, slope, curv = planted_shapes()
    scores = np.column_stack(
        [
            rng.normal(scale=8.0, size=T_DAYS),  # level factor, bp per day
            rng.normal(scale=4.0, size=T_DAYS),  # slope factor, bp per day
            rng.normal(scale=2.0, size=T_DAYS),  # curvature factor, bp per day
        ]
    )
    shapes = np.column_stack([level, slope, curv])
    noise = rng.normal(scale=0.5, size=(T_DAYS, len(TENORS)))
    panel: np.ndarray = scores @ shapes.T + noise
    return panel


def pca(panel: np.ndarray) -> tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
    """Return (eigenvalues descending, eigenvectors as columns) of M = Xc^T Xc / (T - 1)."""
    xc = panel - panel.mean(axis=0)
    m = xc.T @ xc / (panel.shape[0] - 1)
    values, vectors = np.linalg.eigh(m)
    order = np.argsort(values)[::-1]
    return values[order], vectors[:, order]


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
    panel = simulate_panel(rng)
    t_count = panel.shape[0]
    xc = panel - panel.mean(axis=0)
    m = xc.T @ xc / (t_count - 1)
    lam, v = pca(panel)

    print(f"panel: T = {t_count} days, {len(TENORS)} tenors, SIMULATED, in bp")
    print(f"M positive semidefinite (smallest eigenvalue >= -{TOL:g}): {bool(lam[-1] >= -TOL)}")
    print(f"M V = V Lambda to {TOL:g}: {bool(np.max(np.abs(m @ v - v * lam)) < TOL)}")
    scores = xc @ v
    print(
        f"scores satisfy Z^T Z / (T - 1) = Lambda to {TOL:g}: "
        f"{bool(np.max(np.abs(scores.T @ scores / (t_count - 1) - np.diag(lam))) < TOL)}"
    )
    trace_ok = abs(float(np.sum(lam)) - float(np.trace(m))) < TOL
    print(f"eigenvalues sum to trace(M) to {TOL:g}: {trace_ok}")

    shares = lam / np.sum(lam)
    print(
        "explained share of PC1, PC2, PC3 (from unrounded eigenvalues), percent to one decimal: "
        f"{100 * shares[0]:.1f} {100 * shares[1]:.1f} {100 * shares[2]:.1f}"
    )
    print(f"explained shares of all six components sum to 1 to {TOL:g}: {bool(abs(float(np.sum(shares)) - 1) < TOL)}")

    level, slope, curv = planted_shapes()
    names = ("level", "slope", "curvature")
    matches = []
    for name, shape in zip(names, (level, slope, curv), strict=True):
        cosines = np.abs(v.T @ shape)
        best = int(np.argmax(cosines))
        matches.append(best)
        print(f"planted {name}: best-matching component PC{best + 1}, |cosine| {cosines[best]:.4f}")
    print(f"the three planted shapes match three different components: {len(set(matches)) == 3}")

    doubled = panel * 2.0
    lam2, _ = pca(doubled)
    print(
        f"doubling every entry leaves the shares unchanged to {TOL:g}: "
        f"{bool(np.max(np.abs(lam2 / np.sum(lam2) - shares)) < TOL)}"
    )
    rescaled = panel.copy()
    rescaled[:, 0] *= 10.0
    lam3, _ = pca(rescaled)
    print(
        f"first share before rescaling the 0.25-year column by 10: {shares[0]:.4f}; after: {lam3[0] / np.sum(lam3):.4f}"
    )
    m11 = float(m[0, 0])
    old_trace = float(np.trace(m))
    bound = 100.0 * m11 / (old_trace + 99.0 * m11)
    print(
        f"Problem E1 lower bound on the rescaled first share, 100 M11 / (trace + 99 M11): {bound:.4f}; "
        f"M11 {m11:.4f}, trace {old_trace:.4f}"
    )


main()

4Where the canon breaks

  • The shares can depend on the units of each column. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Proposition 2.3 covers scaling the whole matrix \(X\) by one common factor. Remark 2.4 and the rescaling line of Lab 1 show that multiplying one tenor's column by ten moves the first share from 0.7587 to 0.9791 in that run.
  • The curriculum's lab on the Treasury curve is not run here. That lab asks for level, slope and curvature from the U.S. Treasury yield curve. The program does not yet embed a multi-tenor series from which three or more tenors can be read, so this lesson uses the SIMULATED panel above. A panel with two series has at most two principal components, so it cannot give three, and the lesson does not claim the three components for any real curve.
  • Labels are not forced by the data. The level, slope and curvature labels in Definition 3.1 name shapes. Whether a principal direction of real data is level-like is a computation on that data, and no such computation is made here. Definition 3.1 gives a shape no match when two principal directions tie for the largest absolute cosine.
  • Directions are defined up to sign, and tied eigenvalues leave them non-unique. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, the equality case in the proof of Theorem 2.1(a) fixes the leading direction up to sign only when \(\lambda_1\) is simple. Problem R1 shows that a tie at the top gives many maximizers, and Lesson 6 notes the same sign freedom for the singular vectors.
  • The lab does not vary the sample size. The panel has \(T = 750\) observations. The lab does not test how the shares or the cosines change with \(T\), so no claim about sample size is made.
  • PCA describes a sample, not a process. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Theorem 2.1 ranks directions by the squared movement in the data, whatever generated that movement. The ranking does not forecast the factors, and this lesson makes no forecasting claim.

5Misconceptions

  1. “PCA keeps the components that carry the signal and drops the noise.”

    PCA ranks directions by their squared movement in the sample. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Theorem 2.1 uses only the matrix \(M\), so the ranking alone does not tell signal from noise. In Lab 1 the planted shapes are known because the generator is known, and the top three components match them with absolute cosines of at least 0.9985. In that run the match holds for planted scales of 8, 4 and 2 bp per day against noise of 0.5 bp per entry; the lab does not test a panel with other scales.

  2. “Doubling the data doubles the eigenvalues, so the shares double.”

    The matrix \(M\) is quadratic in \(X\), so doubling \(X\) multiplies \(M\) by \(4\) and each eigenvalue by \(4\). Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, the shares are unchanged, by Proposition 2.3 with \(c = 2\). Lab 1 prints True for the doubled panel.

  3. “The first principal component is always the level factor, because yield curves move in parallel.”

    Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, which component is level-like depends on the data, because the order of the components follows the eigenvalues, by Theorem 2.1. In Lab 1 the level component comes first, and the generator planted the largest scale, 8 bp per day, on the level factor. The lab does not vary the planted scales, so it does not show that the scale sets the order. Nothing here shows that real yield curves move in parallel.

6Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · A three-by-two panel

Let \(X\) have rows \((1, 2)\), \((3, 4)\) and \((5, 9)\). Compute \(\tilde X\), the matrix \(M\), and \(\tr(M)\). Check the trace against Proposition 1.2, and find the determinant of \(M\).

Solution

The column means are \(3\) and \(5\), so the centred rows are \((-2, -3)\), \((0, -1)\) and \((2, 4)\). Then \(\tilde X^{\top}\tilde X = \begin{pmatrix} 8 & 14 \\ 14 & 26 \end{pmatrix}\), and with \(T - 1 = 2\), \(M = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 7 & 13 \end{pmatrix}\). The trace is \(17\). Proposition 1.2 gives \((13 + 1 + 20)/2 = 17\), since the squared norms of the centred rows are \(13\), \(1\) and \(20\). The determinant is \(4 \cdot 13 - 7 \cdot 7 = 3\). The eigenvalues are \((17 \pm \sqrt{277})/2\), whose sum is \(17\) and whose product is \(3\).

M2 · Shares from a diagonal matrix

If \(M = \diag(4, 1, 1)\), find the explained shares of the three components.

Solution

The eigenvalues sum to \(6\). The shares are \(4/6 = 2/3\), \(1/6\) and \(1/6\). They sum to \(1\), as Theorem 1.4 requires.

M3 · Tripling the data

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, a panel has \(M\) with eigenvalues \(2\) and \(1\). If every entry of the data is multiplied by \(3\), find the new eigenvalues and the new shares.

Solution

By Proposition 2.3 the new matrix is \(9M\), so the eigenvalues become \(18\) and \(9\). The shares are \(18/27 = 2/3\) and \(9/27 = 1/3\), the same as before, which is \(2/(2+1)\) and \(1/(2+1)\).

Proof

P1 · The column means of centred data

Prove that every column of \(\tilde X\) has mean zero, so that \(\boldsymbol{\iota}_T^{\top}\tilde X = 0\).

Solution

The \(j\)th column of \(\tilde X\) has entries \(x_{tj} - \bar x_j\). Their sum over \(t\) is \(\sum_t x_{tj} - T\bar x_j = \sum_t x_{tj} - \sum_t x_{tj} = 0\), by the definition \(\bar x_j = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t x_{tj}\). So the column sums are zero, which is \(\boldsymbol{\iota}_T^{\top}\tilde X = 0\).

P2 · Scaling the matrix M

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(M = V\Lambda V^{\top}\) with \(V\) orthogonal, so that \(M\) has eigenvalues \(\lambda_k\) with orthonormal eigenvectors \(v_k\) (the columns of \(V\)). Prove that \(c^2M\) has eigenvalues \(c^2\lambda_k\) with the same eigenvectors, for every real \(c \ne 0\).

Solution

For each \(k\), \((c^2M)v_k = c^2(Mv_k) = c^2\lambda_k v_k\). So \(v_k\) is an eigenvector of \(c^2M\) with eigenvalue \(c^2\lambda_k\). Since \(v_1, \ldots, v_n\) is an orthonormal eigenbasis of \(M\), it is also one for \(c^2M\). The eigenvalues of \(c^2M\) are therefore \(c^2\lambda_k\), and the shares are unchanged, as the proof of Proposition 2.3 shows.

P3 · The top eigenvalue dominates a diagonal entry

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, prove that \(\lambda_1 \ge M_{11}\) for every real symmetric positive semidefinite \(M\) with spectral decomposition as in Definition 1.3.

Solution

Apply Theorem 2.1(a) with \(u = e_1\), a unit vector. It gives \(\lambda_1 \ge e_1^{\top}Me_1 = M_{11}\), since \(e_1^{\top}Me_1\) is the \((1,1)\) entry of \(M\). The argument uses only symmetry and positive semidefiniteness, not the particular data.

Empirical

E1 · Why the first share rises in the rescaling check

Lab 1 multiplies the 0.25-year column by ten and prints a larger first share, from 0.7587 to 0.9791. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, use Problem P3 to explain why the first eigenvalue cannot fall below the new \((1,1)\) entry of \(M\), and say what this does and does not explain.

Solution

After the rescaling, the \((1,1)\) entry of \(M\) becomes \(100 M_{11}\), and the trace becomes \(S_0 + 99 M_{11}\), where \(M_{11}\) and \(S_0\) are the old \((1,1)\) entry and trace. Problem P3 applied to the new matrix gives \(\lambda_1' \ge 100 M_{11}\). The new first share is \(\lambda_1' / (S_0 + 99 M_{11})\), so it is at least \(100 M_{11} / (S_0 + 99 M_{11})\). Lab 1 prints this bound with its inputs: \(M_{11} = 13.6818\) and \(S_0 = 88.2028\). The bound is computed from the unrounded values, which give \(0.9483\), and the printed inputs reproduce that value to four decimals. That exceeds the old share \(0.7587\), so on this run the bound alone implies the rise. The bound is a lower bound, and it does not give the exact new share \(0.9791\). The bound depends on \(M_{11}\) and \(S_0\) for each panel. For any panel, it establishes a rise whenever it exceeds that panel's old first share, and it says nothing when it does not. This run is one such case, and the lab does not test other panels.

E2 · Why the doubled-panel shares agree

Lab 1 prints True for the doubled panel and the shares of the original. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, explain which proposition guarantees this, and why the check is a finite numerical test rather than a proof.

Solution

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Proposition 2.3 with \(c = 2\) guarantees it: the new matrix is \(4M\), its eigenvalues are four times the old ones, and the shares are unchanged. The lab computes the new eigenvalues numerically and compares the shares to \(10^{-9}\). That is one panel and one tolerance, so it checks the identity on that input. The proposition covers every nonzero \(c\) and every data matrix whose eigenvalues have a positive sum, which is the condition under which the shares are defined.

E3 · Rounding the printed shares

Lab 1 prints the shares 75.9, 18.1 and 5.2 percent, each rounded to one decimal place from unrounded eigenvalues. Explain what the sum of the three printed values can and cannot tell you about the sum of the three unrounded shares.

Solution

Each rounded value differs from its unrounded share by at most \(0.05\) percentage points. So the sum of the three printed values, \(99.2\), differs from the sum of the three unrounded shares by at most \(0.15\) percentage points. The printed sum therefore fixes the unrounded sum only to within \(0.15\), and the lab does not print that sum. The lab computes each share from unrounded eigenvalues; the sum \(99.2\) is a sum of rounded values, so it carries that bound and is not a computed share.

Research

R1 · A tied leading eigenvalue

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, suppose \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2\) for a symmetric positive semidefinite \(M\). Prove that the maximizer in Theorem 2.1(a) is not unique, and identify the set of all maximizers.

Solution

Let \(u = a v_1 + b v_2\) with \(a^2 + b^2 = 1\). Then \(u^{\top}Mu = a^2\lambda_1 + b^2\lambda_2 = \lambda_1(a^2 + b^2) = \lambda_1\), so every unit vector in \(\operatorname{span}(v_1, v_2)\) attains the maximum. Conversely, by the equality case in the proof of Theorem 2.1(a), a unit vector attains the maximum only if its coefficients on the eigenvectors with eigenvalues below \(\lambda_1\) vanish. So the maximizers are exactly the unit vectors in the eigenspace of \(\lambda_1\), and the leading direction is not unique when the top eigenvalue is tied.

R2 · Adding a constant to a column leaves M unchanged

Let \(X' = X + \boldsymbol{\iota}_T d^{\top}\) for a constant row vector \(d\). Prove that the centred data and \(M\) are the same for \(X'\) as for \(X\).

Solution

The column means of \(X'\) are the column means of \(X\) plus \(d\). Subtracting them, the rows of the centred matrix are \(x_t + d - (\bar x + d) = x_t - \bar x\), so \(\tilde X' = \tilde X\). Therefore \(M' = M\), and the decomposition, the scores and the shares are unchanged by adding a constant to any column.

R3 · A panel with three planted factors and no noise

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(\tilde X = HB^{\top}\), where \(H\) is \(T \times 3\) with centred columns and \(B\) is \(n \times 3\) with orthonormal columns. Prove that \(M = BGB^{\top}\) with \(G = H^{\top}H/(T - 1)\). If \(G = Q\Gamma Q^{\top}\) is a spectral decomposition, show that the nonzero eigenvalues of \(M\) are the nonzero diagonal entries of \(\Gamma\), with eigenvectors \(BQ\). Show that \(M\) has exactly three nonzero eigenvalues when \(H\) has rank \(3\).

Solution

Substituting, \(M = \frac{1}{T-1}(HB^{\top})^{\top}(HB^{\top}) = \frac{1}{T-1}BH^{\top}HB^{\top} = BGB^{\top}\). Let \(W = BQ\). Then \(W^{\top}W = Q^{\top}B^{\top}BQ = Q^{\top}Q = I\), since \(B^{\top}B = I\), and \(MW = BGB^{\top}BQ = BGQ = BQ\Gamma = W\Gamma\). So each column of \(W\) is an eigenvector of \(M\), with the eigenvalue in the matching diagonal entry of \(\Gamma\). The columns of \(W\) span the column space of \(B\), because \(Q\) is invertible. If \(x\) is orthogonal to that column space, then \(B^{\top}x = 0\), so \(Mx = BGB^{\top}x = 0\). An orthonormal basis of that complement, taken as eigenvectors with eigenvalue \(0\), completes \(W\) to an orthonormal eigenbasis of \(M\). Hence the nonzero eigenvalues of \(M\) are exactly the nonzero diagonal entries of \(\Gamma\). If \(H\) has rank \(3\), then \(G\) is invertible, so all three diagonal entries of \(\Gamma\) are nonzero, and \(M\) has exactly three nonzero eigenvalues, those of \(G\).
