Orthogonal Projections and Least Squares

  • Uses: vector space
  • Introduces: orthogonal projection, least squares as orthogonal projection
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore

A regression replaces a target vector by a nearby point in a subspace. The nearest point is the orthogonal projection (Definition 1.1 of this lesson), and the coefficients that produce it solve the normal equations \(A^{\top}A\beta = A^{\top}y\), which are the linear system that a least-squares solution (Section 2 of this lesson) must satisfy (Theorem 2.2(b) of this lesson). Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, this lesson proves both facts when the columns of the matrix \(A\) of regressors (the design matrix) are independent, and it checks the formulas against NumPy's least-squares solver on seeded data. Lesson 8 uses orthogonal directions again, for principal components.

1Orthogonal projection onto a subspace

Throughout, vectors are columns in \(\mathbb{R}^n\), and the inner product is the dot product \(x^{\top}y = \sum_i x_i y_i\), with norm \(\|x\| = \sqrt{x^{\top}x}\), as in Lesson 6. A subspace \(W\) of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is a nonempty set closed under addition and under multiplication by real numbers (Lesson 1). A vector \(z\) is orthogonal to \(W\), written \(z \perp W\), when \(z^{\top}w = 0\) for every \(w \in W\). For a matrix \(A\) of size \(n \times k\), the column space of \(A\) is \(\{A\gamma : \gamma \in \mathbb{R}^k\}\), the span of its columns, and it is a subspace.

Definition 1.1 (orthogonal projection)

Let \(W\) be a subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) and \(y \in \mathbb{R}^n\). A vector \(p \in W\) is the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto \(W\) when \(y - p \perp W\).

Theorem 1.2 (projection onto a column space)

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A\) be a real \(n \times k\) matrix whose columns are linearly independent, and let \(W\) be its column space. Then:

  1. the \(k \times k\) matrix \(G = A^{\top}A\) is invertible;
  2. for every \(y \in \mathbb{R}^n\) there is exactly one orthogonal projection \(p\) of \(y\) onto \(W\), and \(p = A\beta\) with \(\beta = G^{-1}A^{\top}y\);
  3. the matrix \(P = A(A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}\) satisfies \(Py = p\) for every \(y\), and \(P\) is symmetric with \(P^2 = P\).
Proof

(a). The matrix \(G\) is symmetric, since \((A^{\top}A)^{\top} = A^{\top}A\). For \(x \ne 0\), \(x^{\top}Gx = \|Ax\|^2 > 0\), because \(Ax = 0\) would be a nontrivial combination of the independent columns. For an eigenvalue \(\lambda\) of \(G\) with unit eigenvector \(v\), \(\lambda = v^{\top}Gv = \|Av\|^2 > 0\). The spectral theorem (Lesson 4) gives \(G = V\Lambda V^{\top}\) with \(V\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda = \operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k)\), where every \(\lambda_j > 0\). Then \(G \cdot V\Lambda^{-1}V^{\top} = V V^{\top} = I\), using the fact that \(VV^{\top} = I\) for an orthogonal \(V\) (Fact 1.2 of Lesson 6). So \(G\) is invertible.

(b), existence. Let \(\beta = G^{-1}A^{\top}y\) and \(p = A\beta \in W\). Then \[ A^{\top}(y - p) = A^{\top}y - A^{\top}A\,G^{-1}A^{\top}y = A^{\top}y - A^{\top}y = 0. \] Every \(w \in W\) has the form \(w = A\gamma\), and \(w^{\top}(y - p) = \gamma^{\top}A^{\top}(y - p) = 0\). So \(y - p \perp W\).

(b), uniqueness. Let \(p'\) be another orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto \(W\). Then \(p' - p \in W\), because \(W\) is a subspace. Also \(p' - p = (y - p) - (y - p')\) is orthogonal to \(W\), because both terms are. So \(p' - p\) is orthogonal to itself, which gives \(\|p' - p\|^2 = 0\) and \(p' = p\).

(c). For every \(y\), \(Py = A(A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}y = A\beta = p\). Since \(G\) is symmetric, so is \(G^{-1}\), and \(P^{\top} = A(G^{-1})^{\top}A^{\top} = P\). Finally \(P^2 = AG^{-1}(A^{\top}A)G^{-1}A^{\top} = AG^{-1}A^{\top} = P\).

Theorem 1.3 (the projection is the closest point)

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A\), \(W\), \(y\) and \(p\) be as in Theorem 1.2. For every \(w \in W\) with \(w \ne p\), \(\|y - w\| > \|y - p\|\).

Proof

Write \(y - w = (y - p) + (p - w)\). Here \(p - w \in W\), so \(p - w\) is orthogonal to \(y - p\). Expanding the square, \(\|y - w\|^2 = \|y - p\|^2 + 2(y - p)^{\top}(p - w) + \|p - w\|^2 = \|y - p\|^2 + \|p - w\|^2\). If \(w \ne p\), then \(\|p - w\|^2 > 0\), which gives the strict inequality.

Remark 1.4 (the Pythagorean identity)

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.2, \(\|y\|^2 = \|Py\|^2 + \|y - Py\|^2\). The same expansion as in Theorem 1.3 applies with \(w = 0\): the vector \(Py\) lies in \(W\), and \(y - Py\) is orthogonal to \(W\), so the cross term vanishes.

2Least squares

A least-squares solution of the system \(A\beta \approx y\) is a vector \(\hat\beta \in \mathbb{R}^k\) that minimizes \(\|y - A\beta\|^2\) over \(\beta\). The vector \(A\hat\beta\) is then the fitted vector, and \(y - A\hat\beta\) is the residual.

Definition 2.1 (least-squares projection)

Let \(A\) and \(y\) be as in Theorem 1.2. The least-squares projection of \(y\) onto the column space of \(A\) is the fitted vector \(A\hat\beta\) of a least-squares solution \(\hat\beta\).

Theorem 2.2 (least squares is orthogonal projection)

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A\) be a real \(n \times k\) matrix with linearly independent columns, and let \(y \in \mathbb{R}^n\). Then:

  1. the least-squares solution \(\hat\beta\) exists and is unique;
  2. \(\hat\beta\) is the unique solution of the normal equations \(A^{\top}A\beta = A^{\top}y\), and \(\hat\beta = (A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}y\);
  3. the least-squares projection equals \(Py\) from Theorem 1.2, and the residual \(y - A\hat\beta\) is orthogonal to every column of \(A\).
Proof

As \(\beta\) ranges over \(\mathbb{R}^k\), the vector \(A\beta\) ranges over all of \(W\). So minimizing \(\|y - A\beta\|^2\) is the same as minimizing \(\|y - w\|\) over \(w \in W\). By Theorem 1.3 the unique minimizer is \(w = p\), the projection of \(y\). Since the columns of \(A\) are independent, \(A\beta = p\) has exactly one solution \(\beta\), namely \(\hat\beta = G^{-1}A^{\top}y\) from Theorem 1.2(b). This proves (a) and the formula in (b) and (c).

For (b) and (c): the residual \(y - A\hat\beta\) is orthogonal to \(W\) by Theorem 1.2, so \(A^{\top}(y - A\hat\beta) = 0\), which is \(A^{\top}A\hat\beta = A^{\top}y\). Conversely, any solution \(\beta\) of the normal equations satisfies \(A^{\top}(y - A\beta) = 0\), so \(y - A\beta\) is orthogonal to every column and therefore to \(W\), which makes \(A\beta\) the orthogonal projection \(p\). By the uniqueness in (a), \(\beta = \hat\beta\). Since \(G\) is invertible, the solution is unique.

Lab 1 · The projection matrix and the normal equations on a SIMULATED regression

Stated before running: for a SIMULATED regression of twelve seeded observations on an intercept and two seeded regressors, the matrix \(P = A(A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}\) is symmetric and idempotent (\(P^2 = P\)), \(Py = A\beta\) for the normal-equation solution \(\beta\), the residual is orthogonal to every column of \(A\), the Pythagorean identity of Remark 1.4 holds, and \(\beta\) agrees with NumPy's least-squares solver, all to \(10^{-9}\). The lab also prints the coefficients and the residual norm to four decimals, and then duplicates a column: the matrix \(A^{\top}A\) becomes singular, its rank drops to 2, and two coefficient vectors that differ by a null direction give the same fitted values.

"""M3 L07 lab 1: the projection matrix and the normal equations on a SIMULATED regression.

SIMULATED: the 12 observations and the response are generated here from a seeded generator (seed 7). No data
are read. Units: the response and the fitted values are in the same arbitrary units; no unit is claimed.

The design matrix A has an intercept and two regressors. The lab does not check its rank; it reports the rank of
A^T A only for the duplicated design. The lab checks, to the tolerances
printed, the statements of Theorem 1.2, Theorem 2.2 and Remark 1.4: the projection matrix
P = A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T is symmetric and idempotent, Py equals A beta where beta solves the normal equations
A^T A beta = A^T y, the residual is orthogonal to every column of A, and the Pythagorean identity holds. The
coefficients are also compared with NumPy's least-squares routine, used here only as an independent direct solver.
A last part duplicates a column: then A^T A is singular, two different coefficient vectors give the same fitted
values, and the lab reports the rank of A^T A.
Standard library and NumPy only. No network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import numpy as np

TOL = 1e-9


def main() -> None:
    rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
    n = 12
    x1 = rng.normal(size=n)
    x2 = rng.normal(size=n)
    noise = rng.normal(scale=0.3, size=n)
    y = 1.5 + 0.8 * x1 - 0.4 * x2 + noise
    a = np.column_stack([np.ones(n), x1, x2])

    gram = a.T @ a
    p_matrix = a @ np.linalg.solve(gram, a.T)
    beta = np.linalg.solve(gram, a.T @ y)
    fitted = p_matrix @ y
    residual = y - fitted

    print(f"P symmetric to {TOL:g}: {bool(np.max(np.abs(p_matrix - p_matrix.T)) < TOL)}")
    print(f"P idempotent (P P = P) to {TOL:g}: {bool(np.max(np.abs(p_matrix @ p_matrix - p_matrix)) < TOL)}")
    print(f"P y equals A beta to {TOL:g}: {bool(np.max(np.abs(fitted - a @ beta)) < TOL)}")
    print(f"residual orthogonal to every column of A to {TOL:g}: {bool(np.max(np.abs(a.T @ residual)) < TOL)}")
    print(
        f"Pythagorean identity |y|^2 = |Py|^2 + |residual|^2 to {TOL:g}: "
        f"{bool(abs(y @ y - fitted @ fitted - residual @ residual) < TOL)}"
    )

    direct = np.linalg.lstsq(a, y, rcond=None)[0]
    print(
        f"normal-equation coefficients agree with the direct least-squares solver to {TOL:g}: "
        f"{bool(np.max(np.abs(beta - direct)) < TOL)}"
    )
    print(f"coefficients (intercept, x1, x2), printed to 4 decimals: {beta[0]:.4f} {beta[1]:.4f} {beta[2]:.4f}")
    print(f"residual norm, printed to 4 decimals: {np.linalg.norm(residual):.4f}")

    dup = np.column_stack([a[:, 0], a[:, 1], a[:, 1]])
    rank = int(np.linalg.matrix_rank(dup.T @ dup))
    print(f"rank of A^T A with a duplicated column: {rank} (of 3)")
    beta_one = np.linalg.lstsq(dup, y, rcond=None)[0]
    beta_two = beta_one + np.array([0.0, 1.0, -1.0]) * 0.5
    same_fit = bool(np.max(np.abs(dup @ beta_one - dup @ beta_two)) < TOL)
    print(f"two coefficient vectors that differ by a null direction give the same fitted values: {same_fit}")

Every check prints True, so the identities of Theorem 1.2, Theorem 2.2 and Remark 1.4 hold on this one seeded design. The coefficients and the residual norm are printed to four decimals from unrounded values. The response is SIMULATED, with an intercept of 1.5, slopes of 0.8 and \(-0.4\), and noise of standard deviation 0.3 placed in the generator, so the coefficients estimate those generator values and nothing about any real data. The lab checks a finite example; assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, the proofs above cover every full-column-rank design. 3Where the canon breaks

  • Independent columns are needed for the formula. Theorem 1.2 needs \(G = A^{\top}A\) to be invertible, and Problem P3 shows that dependent columns make it singular. Lab 1 shows two coefficient vectors with the same fitted values in that case. The lab does not check that the fitted vector is unique without independent columns. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, for any \(A\), the fitted vector still minimizes \(\|y - w\|\) over \(w \in W\), because \(A\beta\) ranges over all of \(W\). Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Problem R1 shows that an orthogonal projection onto any subspace exists and is unique, and the expansion in the proof of Theorem 1.3 uses only \(y - p \perp W\) and \(p - w \in W\), so the projection is the only minimizer. The fitted vector is therefore unique there too, while the coefficients are not (Lab 1).
  • The projection depends on the inner product. Every formula here uses the dot product. A different inner product defines different orthogonality, and so a different projection. This lesson does not treat other inner products.
  • Orthogonality of the residual is not a test of the model. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Theorem 2.2(c) holds for every response vector \(y\). A residual that is orthogonal to the columns is what least squares produces by construction, so the orthogonality check in Lab 1 verifies the algebra and does not test whether a linear model is correct.
  • Two routes to \(\hat\beta\) are compared, and conditioning is not. Lab 1 compares the normal-equation solve with NumPy's direct solver on one design. The lab does not measure how the accuracy of either solve changes when \(G\) is nearly singular.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A good least-squares fit has a residual of zero.”

    Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, the residual is orthogonal to the columns of \(A\), not zero. For a response that is not in the column space, \(y - Py \ne 0\). Lab 1 prints a residual norm of 0.8951 for its simulated response, and, assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Remark 1.4 gives the squared residual norm as the part of \(\|y\|^2\) not captured by \(\|Py\|^2\).

  2. “A projection matrix is the identity, because projecting does nothing.”

    \(P\) is the identity only when \(W = \mathbb{R}^n\). For \(A = (1, 0)^{\top}\), \(P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), which sends \((3, 4)^{\top}\) to \((3, 0)^{\top}\). Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, projection leaves a vector unchanged only when the vector is already in \(W\): Problem P1 shows that \(Py \in W\) for every \(y\), so \(Py = y\) forces \(y \in W\).

  3. “Every projection matrix has an inverse, since \(P^2 = P\) looks like a stable operation.”

    The matrix \(P = \operatorname{diag}(1, 0)\) above satisfies \(P^2 = P\) and has no inverse. If \(P\) were invertible, multiplying \(P^2 = P\) on the left by \(P^{-1}\) would give \(P = I\), which is false for that \(P\).

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · Projection onto a line

Let \(A = (1, 1)^{\top}\) and \(y = (2, 0)^{\top}\). Compute \(P\), the projection \(p = Py\), and the residual \(y - p\), and check that the residual is orthogonal to \(A\).

Solution

Here \(A^{\top}A = 2\), so \(P = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(p = Py = (1, 1)^{\top}\), and the residual is \(y - p = (1, -1)^{\top}\). Its inner product with \(A\) is \(1 \cdot 1 + (-1) \cdot 1 = 0\), as Theorem 1.2 requires.

M2 · A line of best fit

Fit \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\) to the points \((0, 1)\), \((1, 2)\) and \((2, 2)\) by least squares. Give the coefficients, the fitted values and the residuals, and check that the residuals sum to zero and are orthogonal to \(x\).

Solution

Here \(A\) has rows \((1, 0)\), \((1, 1)\), \((1, 2)\) and \(y = (1, 2, 2)^{\top}\). Then \(A^{\top}A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(A^{\top}y = (5, 6)^{\top}\). The normal equations \(3\beta_0 + 3\beta_1 = 5\) and \(3\beta_0 + 5\beta_1 = 6\) give \(\beta_1 = 1/2\) and \(\beta_0 = 7/6\). The fitted values are \(7/6\), \(5/3\) and \(13/6\), so the residuals are \(-1/6\), \(1/3\) and \(-1/6\). They sum to \(0\), and their inner product with \(x = (0, 1, 2)^{\top}\) is \(0 + 1/3 - 2/6 = 0\), as Theorem 2.2(c) requires.

M3 · Projection onto a coordinate axis

For \(A = (1, 0)^{\top}\) and \(y = (3, 4)^{\top}\), find \(P\), \(p\) and the residual, and check the Pythagorean identity of Remark 1.4.

Solution

\(A^{\top}A = 1\), so \(P = AA^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(p = (3, 0)^{\top}\) and the residual is \((0, 4)^{\top}\), which is orthogonal to \(A\). The identity of Remark 1.4 reads \(25 = 9 + 16\), with \(\|y\|^2 = 25\), \(\|Py\|^2 = 9\) and \(\|y - Py\|^2 = 16\).

Proof

P1 · Fixed points of P

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.2, prove that \(Py = y\) for every \(y \in W\), and that \(Py \in W\) for every \(y\).

Solution

If \(y \in W\), then \(y = A\gamma\) for some \(\gamma\), so \(Py = A(A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}A\gamma = A\gamma = y\). For every \(y\), \(Py = A\beta\) with \(\beta = (A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}y\), which lies in the column space \(W\) by definition.

P2 · The Pythagorean identity for any point of W

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.2, prove that \(\|y - w\|^2 = \|y - p\|^2 + \|p - w\|^2\) for every \(w \in W\), where \(p = Py\).

Solution

Write \(y - w = (y - p) + (p - w)\). The vector \(p - w\) lies in \(W\), since both \(p\) and \(w\) do, and \(y - p\) is orthogonal to \(W\) by Definition 1.1. So the cross term \(2(y - p)^{\top}(p - w)\) is zero, and the identity follows by expanding the square.

P3 · Dependent columns make \(A^{\top}A\) singular

Prove that if the columns of \(A\) are linearly dependent, then \(A^{\top}A\) is not invertible.

Solution

Dependence means there is a nonzero \(\gamma\) with \(A\gamma = 0\). Then \(A^{\top}A\gamma = A^{\top}0 = 0\), so \(\gamma\) is a nonzero vector in the kernel of \(A^{\top}A\). A square matrix with a nonzero kernel vector is not invertible, because an inverse would send \(0\) to \(\gamma\) and to \(0\) at once, which is impossible for \(\gamma \ne 0\).

Empirical

E1 · Reading the residual norm

Lab 1 prints a residual norm of 0.8951. A reader concludes that the regression is poorly specified because the residual is not small. Explain what the lab's orthogonality check establishes about the residual, and why the norm alone cannot decide the question.

Solution

The check residual orthogonal to every column of A prints True, so \(A^{\top}r = 0\) for the lab's residual \(r\). Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, that holds for every response, by Theorem 2.2(c), so it says nothing about the specification. The norm measures the part of the response that the columns do not reproduce. Lab 1's response contains noise with standard deviation 0.3 by construction, so a nonzero residual is expected even for the correct model. The norm alone cannot decide specification, because specification is a question about the data-generating process, and this lab does not test it.

E2 · What a wrong coefficient vector would do to the check

Suppose a reader sets the coefficients to zero and runs the same orthogonality check. Predict the printed result, and explain it using the quantity that is tested.

Solution

With \(\beta = 0\), the residual is \(y\) itself, and the check computes \(A^{\top}y\). The check prints False exactly when some entry of \(A^{\top}y\) has absolute value at least \(10^{-9}\). For Lab 1's seeded response, \(A^{\top}y\) is approximately \((18.38, 3.10, -17.60)^{\top}\) (computed from the lab's seeded generator to two decimals; the lab does not print this vector), so the check prints False, and it rejects the zero coefficient vector. Passing the check is equivalent, up to the tolerance, to solving the normal equations, and, assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, by Theorem 2.2 that identifies the least-squares solution.

E3 · Reading the intercept

Lab 1 prints the intercept \(\beta_0 = 1.3325\). Explain what this number is the fitted value of, and why.

Solution

The first column of \(A\) is the vector of ones, so the fitted value at \(x_1 = x_2 = 0\) is the row \((1, 0, 0)\) times \(\beta\), which is \(\beta_0\). The printed value is therefore the fitted response at zero regressors. It is an estimate of the generator's intercept of 1.5 from twelve observations, and the lab does not state a confidence interval for it.

Research

R1 · Uniqueness of the projection in any subspace

Let \(W\) be any subspace of \(\mathbb{R}^n\), and let \(p\) and \(p'\) both be orthogonal projections of \(y\) onto \(W\), in the sense of Definition 1.1. Prove that \(p = p'\). Does this argument prove that a projection exists?

Solution

Both \(p\) and \(p'\) lie in \(W\), so \(p' - p \in W\). Both \(y - p\) and \(y - p'\) are orthogonal to \(W\), so their difference \(p' - p\) is orthogonal to \(W\), and in particular to itself. Hence \(\|p' - p\|^2 = 0\) and \(p = p'\). The argument uses only the subspace property and the definition, so it proves uniqueness in any subspace. It does not prove existence. Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, existence uses a basis of \(W\), and Theorem 1.2 supplies the projection from one. Take an independent list \(w_1, \ldots, w_k\) in \(W\) of largest possible length. Its length is at most \(n\) by Theorem 3.7 of Lesson 1, since the standard basis of \(\mathbb{R}^n\) is a spanning list of \(n\) vectors. The list spans \(W\): if some \(w \in W\) lay outside its span, appending \(w\) would keep the list independent and make it longer, a contradiction. So \(w_1, \ldots, w_k\) is a basis of \(W\). If \(k \ge 1\), the matrix with columns \(w_1, \ldots, w_k\) has independent columns and column space \(W\), so Theorem 1.2 gives the orthogonal projection onto \(W\). If \(W = \{0\}\), then \(k = 0\), Theorem 1.2 does not apply, and the only vector of \(W\) is \(0\). The orthogonal projection is then \(0\): it lies in \(W\), and \(y - 0\) is orthogonal to \(W\) because \(W\) has no nonzero vector.

R2 · The coefficients are linear in the response

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.2, let \(L = (A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}\) and \(\hat\beta(y) = Ly\). Prove that \(\hat\beta(y + z) = \hat\beta(y) + \hat\beta(z)\) and \(\hat\beta(cy) = c\,\hat\beta(y)\) for scalars \(c\).

Solution

Matrix multiplication is linear: \(L(y + z) = Ly + Lz\) and \(L(cy) = cLy\), by the distributive and scalar rules for matrix-vector products. So \(\hat\beta(y + z) = \hat\beta(y) + \hat\beta(z)\) and \(\hat\beta(cy) = c\hat\beta(y)\). By Theorem 2.2(b), \(\hat\beta(y)\) is the least-squares solution for every \(y\), so the map \(y \mapsto \hat\beta(y)\) is linear.

R3 · The complementary projection

Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.2, let \(Q = I - P\). Prove that \(Q\) is symmetric with \(Q^2 = Q\), that \(Qy\) is orthogonal to \(W\), and that \(Qy = 0\) exactly when \(y \in W\).

Solution

Symmetry follows from that of \(P\). Also \(Q^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - 2P + P = Q\), using \(P^2 = P\). For orthogonality, \(A^{\top}Qy = A^{\top}y - A^{\top}A(A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}y = A^{\top}y - A^{\top}y = 0\), so \(Qy\) is orthogonal to every column of \(A\), hence to \(W\). If \(y \in W\), then \(Py = y\) by Problem P1, so \(Qy = 0\). If \(Qy = 0\), then \(y = Py \in W\).

    Definition 1.1. The projection lies in \(W\), and the error \(y - p\) is orthogonal to \(W\).

  2. For \(A = (1, 1)^{\top}\) and \(y = (2, 0)^{\top}\), what is the first coordinate of \(Py\)?

    \(P = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), so \(Py = (1, 1)^{\top}\) (Problem M1).

  3. For \(P = A(A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}\) with independent columns, which statement holds?

    Options

    Theorem 1.2(c). The identity holds only when \(W = \mathbb{R}^n\), and Misconception 3 shows a projection with no inverse.

  4. Fit \(y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x\) to \((0, 1)\), \((1, 2)\), \((2, 2)\) by least squares. What is \(\beta_0\)? Give four decimals.

    \(\beta_0 = 7/6 \approx 1.1667\) and \(\beta_1 = 1/2\) (Problem M2).

  5. The residual \(r = y - Py\) of a least-squares fit satisfies which identity?

    Options

    Theorem 2.2(c). The residual is orthogonal to every column, and it is zero only when \(y\) is in the column space.

  6. When the columns of \(A\) are linearly dependent, the matrix \(A^{\top}A\) is…

    Options

    Problem P3: a nonzero \(\gamma\) with \(A\gamma = 0\) also satisfies \(A^{\top}A\gamma = 0\).

  7. A projection satisfies \(\|y\|^2 = 25\) and \(\|y - Py\|^2 = 9\). What is \(\|Py\|^2\)?

    Remark 1.4: \(25 = \|Py\|^2 + 9\), so \(\|Py\|^2 = 16\).

  8. For \(A = (1, 0)^{\top}\), the projection matrix \(P\) is…

    Options

    Problem M3: \(P = AA^{\top}\) because \(A^{\top}A = 1\).

  9. For \(y = (3, 4)^{\top}\) and \(W\) the span of \((1, 0)^{\top}\), what is the second coordinate of the orthogonal projection of \(y\) onto \(W\)?

    The projection is \((3, 0)^{\top}\) (Problem M3), whose second coordinate is \(0\).
