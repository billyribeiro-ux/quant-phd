Measure-Theoretic Probability

Probability on a general space is defined through measures, and results such as the expectation of a payoff, a conditional expectation given partial information, or the expected final wealth of a fair game under a bounded stopping rule are stated and proved in that language. This module constructs it from the σ-algebra of events up. It covers measures and probability spaces, the Lebesgue integral and the monotone and dominated convergence theorems, product measures and Fubini's theorem, densities and the Radon–Nikodym theorem, conditional expectation as an L2 projection, filtrations and discrete-time martingales, stopping times, optional stopping, Doob's maximal inequality and martingale convergence. Each term is defined in the lesson that teaches it, listed in the lesson map below. You leave this module able to state each result with its hypotheses, to prove the ones that the unit proves, and to check the martingale claims of a model against exact computations.

  • Prerequisites: M4 and M5, and through them M1, M2 and P0.1. The prerequisite concepts are linked from the section below.
  • Project: Proof portfolio: martingales, graded by an autograder and a rubric
  • Data: none. Each proved result is proved on its lesson page. A theorem that a lesson states without proof is marked as stated, not proved, and is not graded T. Labs compute exact or numerical checks on finite or explicitly stated spaces, and the project's grader generates SIMULATED instances. No real market data is used.

108 h lessons · 42 h project (28% of the 150 h module)

Objectives

By the end of M6 you can:

  1. State and prove the optional stopping theorem for a bounded stopping time, and show with the doubling strategy why the bound is needed (Lesson 10).
  2. Compute Lebesgue integrals of simple functions, and apply the monotone and dominated convergence theorems to a sequence, checking each hypothesis (Lessons 3 and 4).
  3. Decide whether an interchange of an integral with a limit or a sum is justified, by checking the hypotheses of Fubini's theorem (Lesson 5).
  4. Construct a conditional expectation as an L2 projection on a finite space, and verify its defining property (Lesson 7).
  5. Verify the martingale property of a given process with respect to its filtration (Lesson 8).
  6. Bound a maximal function with Doob's inequality, and state the hypotheses it needs (Lesson 11).

Placement diagnostic

Nine questions. Seven test the prerequisites, from P0.1, M1, M2, M4 and M5. Two are skill checks, marked as such, on what rungs 1 and 2 of the project teach. The mastery bar is 80 percent, which for nine items means eight correct: seven of nine is 77.8 percent and eight of nine is 88.9 percent. To skip rungs 1 and 2, you must reach the bar and answer both skill checks. If you fall below the bar, complete the lessons in order and attempt the rungs.

  1. What does grade T mean in this program?

    Options

    T is the theorem grade (P0.1, evidence grade).

  2. Which statement is a proof by induction of a claim \(P(n)\) for all integers \(n \ge 1\) needs in addition to the base case \(P(1)\)?

    Options

    Mathematical induction needs the inductive step as well as the base case (M1, mathematical induction).

  3. What is \(\int_0^1 x \, dx\)?

    The antiderivative of \(x\) is \(x^2/2\), so the value is \(1/2 - 0 = 0.5\) (M2, definite integral).

  4. A fair six-sided die shows the number \(X\). What is \(\E[X]\)?

    Each face has probability \(1/6\), so \(\E[X] = (1 + 2 + \dots + 6)/6 = 21/6 = 3.5\) (M4, expectation).

  5. A random variable \(X\) is uniform on \(\{1, 2, 3, 4\}\), and \(A = \{X \in \{1, 2\}\}\). What is \(\E[X \mid A]\), the average of \(X\) over the cell \(A\)?

    Given \(A\), \(X\) is uniform on \(\{1, 2\}\), with mean \(1.5\) (M4, conditional expectation for a discrete partition).

  6. Which sequence is a Cauchy sequence of real numbers?

    Options

    The terms \(1/n\) get arbitrarily close to each other. The sequence \((-1)^n\) keeps alternating by 2, and \(n\) grows without bound (M5, Cauchy sequence).

  7. Which property of the real numbers makes every Cauchy sequence of reals converge?

    Options

    Completeness of the reals is the property that gives convergence of Cauchy sequences (M5, completeness of the reals).

  8. Skill check (rung 1): a fair walk starts at \(S_0 = 0\) and moves \(\pm 1\) with probability \(1/2\) each. Let \(\tau\) be the first time the walk reaches \(+1\), capped at \(2\), so \(\tau = \min(\text{first hit of } +1, 2)\). What is \(\E[S_\tau]\)?

    With probability \(1/2\), \(\tau = 1\) and \(S_\tau = 1\). Otherwise \(S_1 = -1\), and at time 2 the walk is at \(0\) or \(-2\) with probability \(1/2\) each. So \(\E[S_\tau] = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 (\tfrac12 \cdot 0 + \tfrac12 \cdot (-2)) = 0\), which equals \(S_0\), as optional stopping for a bounded stopping time requires (rung 1).

  9. Skill check (rung 2): a doubling strategy stakes 1 unit, then doubles the stake after each loss. After 3 losses in a row, what is the total staked?

    The stakes are \(1, 2, 4\), summing to \(2^3 - 1 = 7\). This is the geometric sum that the completion problem of rung 2 fills in.

Lessons

Twelve lessons of nine study hours each, which sum to 108 hours. Each lesson page declares its hours, and the lesson map below links every lesson. Lessons 1 and 2 construct the measure space. Lessons 3 and 4 develop the integral and its convergence theorems. Lessons 5 and 6 cover products and densities. Lessons 7 to 12 cover conditional expectation and the three martingale results. Every lesson has labs inline, a problem set with worked solutions, and a retrieval quiz that feeds your review deck.

L01 · 9 hSigma-algebras and Measurable EventsThe σ-algebra of events, its three axioms, the closure properties they imply, the σ-algebra generated by a finite partition, and why countable unions are required. L02 · 9 hMeasure and Probability SpacesMeasures and probability measures on a σ-algebra, the probability space, and the consequences of the axioms: monotonicity, additivity and continuity from below. L03 · 9 hThe Lebesgue IntegralThe integral of simple functions as a sum of values times measures, the supremum definition for non-negative functions, and a dyadic bracket of the integral of \(x^2\). L04 · 9 hMonotone and Dominated ConvergenceThe monotone convergence theorem with its proof, the dominated convergence theorem with its hypotheses, and a sequence whose integrals stay at one while its pointwise limit is zero. L05 · 9 hProduct Measures and Fubini's TheoremProduct measures, the Fubini interchange for finite products, and a function on the unit square whose iterated integrals disagree because its integrability hypothesis fails. L06 · 9 hDensities and the Radon–Nikodym TheoremAbsolute continuity, densities of one measure with respect to another, and the finite case of the Radon–Nikodym theorem checked exactly. L07 · 9 hConditional Expectation as an L2 ProjectionConditional expectation defined as a projection onto the functions measurable with respect to a partition, with its tower property and its finite-space form checked exactly. L08 · 9 hFiltrations and Discrete-Time MartingalesFiltrations, adapted processes and martingales, with a fair walk shown to be a martingale and a biased walk shown not to be one on a finite tree. L09 · 9 hStopping TimesStopping times, the event that a stopping time has occurred by time k, and the closure properties of stopping times. L10 · 9 hOptional Stopping and the Doubling StrategyThe bounded optional stopping theorem, and the doubling strategy with finite capital, where the hypothesis that fails in the unbounded case is named. L11 · 9 hDoob's Maximal InequalityDoob's maximal inequality for a non-negative submartingale, proved from the first time the process exceeds a level, and checked exactly on a grid of levels. L12 · 9 hMartingale ConvergenceThe martingale convergence theorem and its hypotheses, the L2 case proved on the page, and the dyadic martingale whose error is computed exactly.

The project ladder

The module ends in the proof portfolio: martingales. Rungs 1 and 2 scaffold the skills that the placement check tests. To skip them, a student must reach the placement bar and answer both skill checks.

  1. Worked example

    One fully solved bounded optional-stopping computation for a fair walk, with the reason for every step, including the adaptedness of the stopping time (Lessons 8 and 9) and the hypothesis that makes the identity hold.

  2. Completion problem

    The doubling wealth recursion with finite capital in integer cents, with the cap and ruin lines left blank. Graded instantly in your browser.

  3. Independent project

    Typeset proofs of optional stopping in the bounded case, Doob's maximal inequality and the martingale convergence theorem, with the engine martingale_checks.py that verifies each claim on generated instances. Graded by the autograder against those instances and by the rubric. Project page.

  4. Stretch

    An open-ended extension with no ceiling, for example the same checks for a martingale built from an arbitrary bounded predictable bet, suitable for your portfolio.

Data status

No real data is used in M6. The labs compute exact results on finite spaces, or numerical results from closed forms. The project's instances are generated from seeds and labelled SIMULATED on the project page. No lab reads a data file.
