Sigma-algebras and Measurable Events

  • Uses: quantifiers (M1), direct proof (M1), and finite sets, which this lesson uses as ordinary mathematics
  • Introduces: the σ-algebra of events
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or lab result
  • Dlore

A probability model assigns probabilities to events, and an event is a set of outcomes. For a finite experiment, any collection of sets of outcomes can be taken as the events. For an infinite experiment, the collection of events has to be specified with care, and §4 raises, without settling, whether the collection of all subsets is too large to use. This lesson defines that collection, the σ-algebra, and proves the properties that the rest of the unit uses: closure under complements, finite unions, and countable unions and intersections. It also shows that finite closure is not enough.

1Sets, events and the σ-algebra

Let \(\Omega\) be the set of outcomes of an experiment. A subset of \(\Omega\) is a set of outcomes, and \(A^c = \Omega \setminus A\) is the complement of \(A\). A sequence \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) is countable when it is indexed by the positive integers. Its union \(\bigcup_n A_n\) is the set of outcomes that lie in at least one \(A_n\), and its intersection \(\bigcap_n A_n\) is the set of outcomes that lie in every \(A_n\).

Definition 1.1 (σ-algebra, events)

Let \(\Omega\) be a set. A σ-algebra on \(\Omega\) is a collection \(\mathcal F\) of subsets of \(\Omega\) such that

  1. (S1) \(\Omega \in \mathcal F\);
  2. (S2) if \(A \in \mathcal F\), then \(A^c \in \mathcal F\);
  3. (S3) if \(A_1, A_2, \dots\) is a countable sequence of members of \(\mathcal F\), then \(\bigcup_n A_n \in \mathcal F\).

The members of \(\mathcal F\) are the events. Probability is defined only on events.

Example 1.2 (two σ-algebras and one that fails)

For any set \(\Omega\), the collection \(\{\emptyset, \Omega\}\) is a σ-algebra, and so is the collection of all subsets of \(\Omega\). For the first, (S1) holds, (S2) holds because \(\emptyset^c = \Omega\) and \(\Omega^c = \emptyset\), and (S3) holds because every countable union of \(\emptyset\) and \(\Omega\) is \(\emptyset\) or \(\Omega\). For the second, every subset is already a member, so there is nothing to check beyond the fact that unions and complements of subsets are subsets.

On \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3\}\), the collection \(\{\emptyset, \{1\}, \Omega\}\) satisfies (S1) and (S3) but fails (S2), because the complement of \(\{1\}\) is \(\{2, 3\}\), which the collection does not contain.

Proposition 1.3 (consequences of the axioms)

Assumptions.

  1. \(\Omega\) is a set and \(\mathcal F\) is a σ-algebra on \(\Omega\) (Definition 1.1).

Statement. Then (a) \(\emptyset \in \mathcal F\); (b) if \(A, B \in \mathcal F\), then \(A \cup B \in \mathcal F\) and \(A \cap B \in \mathcal F\); (c) if \(A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal F\), then \(\bigcap_n A_n \in \mathcal F\).

Proof

(a) By (S1) and (S2), \(\emptyset = \Omega^c \in \mathcal F\).

(b) Put \(A_1 = A\), \(A_2 = B\), and \(A_n = \emptyset\) for \(n \ge 3\). Each \(A_n\) lies in \(\mathcal F\) by (a), and \(\bigcup_n A_n = A \cup B\), so \(A \cup B \in \mathcal F\) by (S3). For the intersection, \(A^c\) and \(B^c\) lie in \(\mathcal F\) by (S2). Their union lies in \(\mathcal F\) by what was just shown, and its complement lies in \(\mathcal F\) by (S2). An outcome lies outside \(A^c \cup B^c\) exactly when it lies in \(A\) and in \(B\), so \((A^c \cup B^c)^c = A \cap B\), and therefore \(A \cap B \in \mathcal F\).

(c) Put \(C_n = A_n^c\). Each \(C_n\) lies in \(\mathcal F\) by (S2), so \(\bigcup_n C_n \in \mathcal F\) by (S3). An outcome lies outside every \(C_n\) exactly when it lies in every \(A_n\), so \(\bigl(\bigcup_n C_n\bigr)^c = \bigcap_n A_n\). By (S2), \(\bigcap_n A_n \in \mathcal F\). \(\blacksquare\)

The axioms state only countable unions, in (S3). Countable intersections follow from (S2) and (S3) together, as Proposition 1.3(c) shows, and later lessons use both.

2The σ-algebra generated by a finite partition

A finite family of disjoint sets that covers \(\Omega\) is the simplest way to specify events. Such a family is called a partition, and its members are its blocks. Formally, \(B_1, \dots, B_k\) is a partition of \(\Omega\) when every block is non-empty, the blocks are pairwise disjoint, and \(B_1 \cup \dots \cup B_k = \Omega\).

Proposition 2.1 (the σ-algebra of a partition)

Assumptions.

  1. \(\Omega\) is a set, and \(B_1, \dots, B_k\) with \(k \ge 1\) is a partition of \(\Omega\) in the sense above.
  2. For each subset \(J\) of \(\{1, \dots, k\}\), write \(U_J = \bigcup_{j \in J} B_j\), with \(U_\emptyset = \emptyset\), and let \(\mathcal F_P\) be the collection of all \(U_J\).

Statement. Then (a) \(\mathcal F_P\) is a σ-algebra on \(\Omega\); (b) \(\mathcal F_P\) has exactly \(2^k\) members; (c) every σ-algebra on \(\Omega\) that contains \(B_1, \dots, B_k\) contains \(\mathcal F_P\). So \(\mathcal F_P\) is the smallest σ-algebra containing the blocks. Its members are the events generated by the partition.

Proof

Every outcome lies in exactly one block. So for \(x \in \Omega\), \(x\) lies in \(U_J\) exactly when the block containing \(x\) has its index in \(J\). Call that index \(i(x)\). Then \(x \in U_J\) if and only if \(i(x) \in J\). This rule is used in parts (a) and (b) below.

(a) (S1): \(U_{\{1, \dots, k\}} = \Omega\). (S2): \(x \in U_J^c\) if and only if \(i(x) \notin J\), if and only if \(x \in U_{J^c}\), where \(J^c\) is the complement of \(J\) in \(\{1, \dots, k\}\). So \(U_J^c = U_{J^c}\). (S3): let \(U_{J_1}, U_{J_2}, \dots\) be a countable sequence in \(\mathcal F_P\), and let \(J = \bigcup_m J_m\), a subset of \(\{1, \dots, k\}\). Then \(x \in \bigcup_m U_{J_m}\) if and only if \(i(x) \in J_m\) for some \(m\), if and only if \(i(x) \in J\), so \(\bigcup_m U_{J_m} = U_J \in \mathcal F_P\).

(b) The map \(J \mapsto U_J\) is one-to-one. If \(J \ne J'\), choose \(j\) in one of them but not the other. The block \(B_j\) is non-empty, so pick \(x \in B_j\). Then \(i(x) = j\), so \(x\) lies in exactly one of \(U_J\) and \(U_{J'}\). Hence the \(2^k\) subsets \(J\) give \(2^k\) distinct members of \(\mathcal F_P\).

(c) Let \(\mathcal G\) be a σ-algebra containing every \(B_j\). By Proposition 1.3(a), \(\emptyset \in \mathcal G\). Each \(U_J\) is the countable union of the sequence whose \(j\)-th term is \(B_j\) for \(j \in J\) and \(\emptyset\) otherwise, and each term lies in \(\mathcal G\). By (S3), \(U_J \in \mathcal G\). So \(\mathcal F_P \subseteq \mathcal G\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (a four-point set with two blocks)

Let \(\Omega = \{0, 1, 2, 3\}\) and blocks \(\{0, 1\}\) and \(\{2, 3\}\). By Proposition 2.1 the events are \(\emptyset\), \(\{0, 1\}\), \(\{2, 3\}\) and \(\Omega\), which is \(2^2 = 4\) events. The collection of all subsets of \(\Omega\) has \(2^4 = 16\) members, so this σ-algebra is much smaller. Taking the four singletons as blocks instead gives all 16 subsets.

Lab 1 generates these collections for three partitions and runs the closure checks of Definition 1.1 by enumeration. It also tests a candidate family that is not a σ-algebra.

Lab 1 · Sigma-algebras of finite partitions

Predicted before running. By Proposition 2.1(b), two blocks give \(2^2 = 4\) events on the four-point set, four singleton blocks give \(2^4 = 16\), and three blocks on the six-point set give \(2^3 = 8\). Each of the three families should pass every closure check. The candidate \(\{\emptyset, \{0, 1\}, \Omega\}\) should pass the checks for (S1) and union closure and fail complement closure, because the complement of \(\{0, 1\}\) is \(\{2, 3\}\), which the candidate omits. Overlapping blocks should be rejected.

"""M6 lab L01: the sigma-algebra generated by a finite partition, checked by exhaustive enumeration.

Standard library only. Nothing is random and nothing is read from a file, so every run prints the same lines.
Each collection is a finite family of subsets of a finite set, and each check runs over every member of that
family, so each count and each True or False is exact for the set named on its line. A finite check says nothing
about an infinite sample space; the page reads the output that way.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections.abc import Sequence

Subset = frozenset[int]


def is_partition(omega: Subset, blocks: Sequence[Subset]) -> bool:
    """True when the blocks are non-empty, pairwise disjoint, and their union is omega."""
    if any(not block for block in blocks):
        return False
    seen: set[int] = set()
    for block in blocks:
        if seen & block:
            return False
        seen |= block
    return seen == set(omega)


def generated_by_partition(omega: Subset, blocks: Sequence[Subset]) -> set[Subset]:
    """All unions of subfamilies of the blocks: the smallest sigma-algebra that contains every block.

    A subfamily is a choice of blocks, so there are 2 to the power len(blocks) of them, and distinct
    subfamilies give distinct unions because the blocks are non-empty and disjoint. Raises ValueError
    when the blocks do not partition omega.
    """
    if not is_partition(omega, blocks):
        raise ValueError("blocks must be non-empty, pairwise disjoint, and cover omega")
    family: set[Subset] = set()
    for mask in range(2 ** len(blocks)):
        union: set[int] = set()
        for j, block in enumerate(blocks):
            if (mask >> j) & 1:
                union |= block
        family.add(frozenset(union))
    return family


def closure_checks(omega: Subset, family: set[Subset]) -> tuple[bool, bool, bool]:
    """Return (contains the empty set and omega, closed under complement, closed under pairwise union).

    Every member is tested for complement closure, and every pair of members for union closure. Closure under
    pairwise union implies closure under every finite union, by induction on the number of sets.
    """
    whole = frozenset(omega)
    contains_ends = frozenset() in family and whole in family
    complement_closed = all(whole - a in family for a in family)
    union_closed = all((a | b) in family for a in family for b in family)
    return contains_ends, complement_closed, union_closed


def as_set_text(items: Subset) -> str:
    """Write a finite set in the form {0, 1}, with its members in increasing order."""
    return "{" + ", ".join(str(x) for x in sorted(items)) + "}"


def main() -> None:
    four = frozenset({0, 1, 2, 3})
    six = frozenset({0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5})
    cases = [
        ("4-point set, blocks {0,1} and {2,3}", four, [frozenset({0, 1}), frozenset({2, 3})]),
        ("4-point set, four singleton blocks", four, [frozenset({i}) for i in range(4)]),
        (
            "6-point set, blocks {0,1}, {2,3}, {4,5}",
            six,
            [frozenset({0, 1}), frozenset({2, 3}), frozenset({4, 5})],
        ),
    ]
    for label, omega, blocks in cases:
        family = generated_by_partition(omega, blocks)
        contains_ends, complement_closed, union_closed = closure_checks(omega, family)
        k = len(blocks)
        print(f"{label}: members {len(family)} (2^{k} = {2**k})")
        print(
            f"  contains empty set and whole set: {contains_ends}; "
            f"complement closed: {complement_closed}; union closed: {union_closed}"
        )

    candidate = {frozenset(), frozenset({0, 1}), four}
    contains_ends, complement_closed, union_closed = closure_checks(four, candidate)
    print("candidate {empty set, {0,1}, whole set} on the 4-point set")
    print(
        f"  contains empty set and whole set: {contains_ends}; "
        f"complement closed: {complement_closed}; union closed: {union_closed}"
    )
    missing = four - frozenset({0, 1})
    print(f"  complement of {{0, 1}} is {as_set_text(missing)}; in the candidate: {missing in candidate}")

    try:
        generated_by_partition(four, [frozenset({0, 1}), frozenset({1, 2, 3})])
    except ValueError as error:
        print(f"overlapping blocks rejected: {error}")

3Why countable unions are required

Finite closure looks like enough. It is not. The next example is a family that is closed under complements and under every finite union, and is not closed under countable unions.

Example 3.1 (finite and cofinite sets)

Let \(\mathcal C\) be the collection of subsets of \(\N = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}\) that are finite or whose complement is finite (the cofinite sets). Then \(\mathcal C\) is closed under complements and finite unions, but not under countable unions.

Proof. A set is in \(\mathcal C\) if and only if its complement is in \(\mathcal C\), so complements are closed. For finite unions: a union of finite sets is finite. If \(A\) is finite and \(B\) is cofinite, the complement of \(A \cup B\) is \(A^c \cap B^c\), which is contained in the finite set \(B^c\), and a subset of a finite set is finite, so \(A \cup B\) is cofinite. If both are cofinite, the complement of their union is \(A^c \cap B^c\), the intersection of two finite sets, which is finite. So finite unions stay in \(\mathcal C\). For the counterexample, put \(A_n = \{2n\}\), each finite and so in \(\mathcal C\). Their union is the set of even positive integers, which is infinite, and its complement, the set of odd positive integers, is also infinite. So the union is neither finite nor cofinite, and it is not in \(\mathcal C\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 3.1 shows that (S3) cannot be weakened to finite unions. A probability model that assigns \(\Prob\bigl(\bigcup_n A_n\bigr)\) to a countable union needs that union to be an event. The axiom asks for exactly that.

4Where the canon breaks

The definitions above are short. Four points mark where the simple picture fails.

  • Finite closure is not enough. Example 3.1 is closed under complements and every finite union, and a countable union leaves it. Later lessons use countable unions of events, for example continuity from below in Lesson 2, Proposition 1.3(d), so (S3) is needed there.
  • Proposition 2.1 covers finitely many blocks. The page does not treat a partition with infinitely many blocks, and no result about that case is used below.
  • The axioms do not force every subset to be an event. The power set, the collection of all subsets of \(\Omega\), is a σ-algebra on any \(\Omega\) (Example 1.2), so a model may take it as its events, and Example 2.2 gives a σ-algebra that is smaller. The page does not prove that a smaller choice is needed for an uncountable \(\Omega\). That is raised here as a question, not stated as a result.
  • A finite check is evidence about a finite family. Lab 1 runs every check on finite families, and its counts are exact for them. A count on a finite family says nothing about the infinite families of the later sections.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Any collection of subsets that contains \(\Omega\) is a σ-algebra.”

    Definition 1.1 also requires (S2) and (S3). Example 1.2 gives the collection \(\{\emptyset, \{1\}, \Omega\}\) on \(\{1, 2, 3\}\), which contains \(\Omega\) and fails (S2), since the complement of \(\{1\}\) is \(\{2, 3\}\) and is missing.

  2. “Closure under finite unions gives closure under countable unions.”

    Example 3.1 is closed under complements and every finite union. The sets \(\{2n\}\) are in it, but their countable union, the even positive integers, is neither finite nor cofinite. Lab 1 does not test countable unions, because its families are finite; the example is the argument, and it does not depend on the lab.

  3. “Every subset of \(\Omega\) is an event, whatever \(\Omega\) and the collection are.”

    The events are the members of the chosen σ-algebra \(\mathcal F\). Proposition 2.1(b) gives a σ-algebra on the four-point set with blocks \(\{0, 1\}\) and \(\{2, 3\}\) that has 4 members, not the 16 subsets of that set. Lab 1 prints 4 for that partition.

Mechanics

M1 · The events of a three-block partition

Let \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}\) with blocks \(\{1\}\), \(\{2, 3\}\) and \(\{4, 5\}\). List the σ-algebra generated by the blocks and state its size.

Solution

Proposition 2.1 gives the unions of subfamilies of the three blocks. They are \(\emptyset\), \(\{1\}\), \(\{2, 3\}\), \(\{4, 5\}\), \(\{1, 2, 3\}\), \(\{1, 4, 5\}\), \(\{2, 3, 4, 5\}\) and \(\Omega\). There are \(2^3 = 8\) of them, matching Proposition 2.1(b).

M2 · Repairing a family

On \(\Omega = \{1, 2, 3\}\), the family \(\{\emptyset, \{1\}, \Omega\}\) fails (S2). Find the smallest set that must be added to make it a σ-algebra, and list the resulting family.

Solution

The missing set is the complement of \(\{1\}\), which is \(\{2, 3\}\). Adding it gives \(\{\emptyset, \{1\}, \{2, 3\}, \Omega\}\). This is the σ-algebra generated by the partition \(\{1\}, \{2, 3\}\) of Proposition 2.1, and it has \(2^2 = 4\) members. Any σ-algebra containing \(\{1\}\) must contain \(\{2, 3\}\), so no smaller repair works.

M3 · Counting events for five blocks

Let \(\Omega\) have seven points, partitioned into the block \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) and the four singletons \(\{4\}\), \(\{5\}\), \(\{6\}\), \(\{7\}\). How many events does the generated σ-algebra have?

Solution

There are five blocks, so Proposition 2.1(b) gives \(2^5 = 32\) events. The count does not depend on the sizes of the blocks, only on their number, because distinct subfamilies give distinct unions.

Proof

P1 · Finite unions from (S3)

Let \(\mathcal F\) be a σ-algebra and \(A_1, \dots, A_m \in \mathcal F\). Prove that \(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_m \in \mathcal F\).

Solution

Put \(A_n = \emptyset\) for \(n > m\). Each term lies in \(\mathcal F\), because \(\emptyset \in \mathcal F\) by Proposition 1.3(a). The countable union of this sequence is \(A_1 \cup \dots \cup A_m\), so (S3) gives membership in \(\mathcal F\).

P2 · Injectivity in Proposition 2.1(b)

Let \(J \ne J'\) be subsets of \(\{1, \dots, k\}\). Prove that \(U_J \ne U_{J'}\).

Solution

Since \(J \ne J'\), there is an index \(j\) in exactly one of them, say \(j \in J\) and \(j \notin J'\). The block \(B_j\) is non-empty, so choose \(x \in B_j\). Then \(i(x) = j\), so \(x \in U_J\) and \(x \notin U_{J'}\), because \(i(x) \notin J'\). Hence \(U_J\) and \(U_{J'}\) differ, and the map \(J \mapsto U_J\) is one-to-one.

P3 · The complement of a block union

For a partition \(B_1, \dots, B_k\) of \(\Omega\) and a subset \(J\) of \(\{1, \dots, k\}\), prove that \(\bigl(\bigcup_{j \in J} B_j\bigr)^c = \bigcup_{j \notin J} B_j\).

Solution

Let \(x \in \Omega\). Since the blocks partition \(\Omega\), \(x\) lies in exactly one block \(B_{i(x)}\). If \(i(x) \in J\), then \(x\) lies in the union over \(J\), and it lies in no block with index outside \(J\), so it is not in the right-hand side either. If \(i(x) \notin J\), then \(x\) is outside the union over \(J\) and lies in the union over the complement of \(J\). So \(x\) is in the left-hand side exactly when it is in the right-hand side, which proves the identity.

Empirical

E1 · Where 16 comes from

Lab 1 prints 16 for the partition of a four-point set into four singletons. Derive that number by hand and check it against the output.

Solution

Four singleton blocks give \(2^4 = 16\) unions, one for each subset of the four blocks. Each subset \(J\) of the four blocks gives the set of points whose block lies in \(J\). For singleton blocks that set is \(J\) itself, so the family is the whole power set of the four-point set, which has 16 members. The printed value of 16 agrees with this count. The count is exact.

E2 · What the candidate line establishes

Lab 1 reports that the candidate \(\{\emptyset, \{0, 1\}, \Omega\}\) is not complement closed and names the missing set. What does that line establish, and what would it not establish about a different candidate?

Solution

The line establishes that this candidate is not a σ-algebra, because the complement of \(\{0, 1\}\), which is \(\{2, 3\}\), is missing. It does not establish anything about another family. A different candidate needs its own check, and a family that passes every check on this four-point set is still only tested on that set.

E3 · Changing the partition size without running Lab 1

Suppose Lab 1 were run on a five-point set partitioned into its five singletons. State the number of events it would print and the reason, without running the code.

Solution

Five singleton blocks give \(2^5 = 32\) events, which is the whole power set of the five-point set. The line would print 32. The reason is Proposition 2.1(b) with \(k = 5\), not the lab, which only confirms the count.

Research

R1 · Why a countable union must be an event

A model defines \(\Prob(A)\) only for events and wants \(\Prob\bigl(\bigcup_n A_n\bigr)\) for a countable sequence of events \(A_n\). Using Example 3.1, explain what goes wrong if the events are only finitely closed.

Solution

In Example 3.1 the sets \(\{2n\}\) are events, but their union is not in the family. The model then has no value for the probability of that union, although the union is a natural quantity to ask about. Requiring (S3) makes every countable union an event, so the probability of the union has a place to be defined.

R2 · Stating the question for an uncountable sample space

The canon-breaks section says that the power set may be too large to use as the events on an uncountable \(\Omega\). State precisely what a proof of that claim would have to show, and say what this unit does not claim.

Solution

A proof would have to name a property that the model requires of its probability, and show that no probability on the power set of some uncountable \(\Omega\) can have that property. The unit does not carry out this proof and does not claim the result. Its proved claims are Proposition 1.3, Proposition 2.1 and Example 3.1; Lab 1 checks finite families only.

R3 · When every subset is an event

A modeller says that every subset of the outcome space is an event, so the axioms impose no restriction. State when this is true, and what Proposition 2.1 says about the number of events when the outcome space is finite and generated by a partition.

Solution

It is true when the chosen σ-algebra is the power set, which satisfies (S1) to (S3) by Example 1.2. The restriction is then absent by construction, not by the axioms. Proposition 2.1 shows that a partition with \(k\) blocks generates only \(2^k\) events, which is all subsets only when \(k\) equals the number of outcomes, as with singleton blocks.

    Each \(\{2n\}\) is one even integer, and the union collects all of them. The union is neither finite nor cofinite.
