Product measures and Fubini's theorem

An integral over a product of two sets can often be computed one variable at a time. That is the convenient case, and it is not automatic. Fubini's theorem states when the two iterated integrals of a function on a product agree with each other and with the double integral. This lesson proves the finite case from the distributive law. The general statement is listed as Assumption F and is not proved here. Section 3 gives a function on the unit square whose two iterated integrals are plus and minus \(\pi/4\). That function is not integrable, and Lab 1 computes both orders in closed form and by quadrature.

1Product weights and the finite case

Let \(X\) and \(Y\) be finite sets. Let \(\mu_1\) be a nonnegative weight on \(X\) and \(\mu_2\) a nonnegative weight on \(Y\). The measure of a set \(A \subseteq X\) is \(\mu_1(A) = \sum_{x \in A} \mu_1(x)\), and similarly for \(\mu_2\). On the product \(X \times Y\), the product weight is \(\mu(x,y) = \mu_1(x)\,\mu_2(y)\). A rectangle \(A \times B\) then has measure \(\mu_1(A)\,\mu_2(B)\). On a finite set, take every subset of \(X \times Y\) as measurable, so no further sets are needed.

For a real function \(f\) on \(X \times Y\), the double integral is the sum \(\sum_{(x,y)} f(x,y)\,\mu(x,y)\). The two iterated integrals are

\[ I_{1} = \sum_{x \in X} \mu_1(x) \Big( \sum_{y \in Y} f(x,y)\,\mu_2(y) \Big), \qquad I_{2} = \sum_{y \in Y} \mu_2(y) \Big( \sum_{x \in X} f(x,y)\,\mu_1(x) \Big). \]
Definition 1.1 (Fubini's theorem)

Fubini's theorem is the statement that, for a function \(f\) on a product of two measure spaces, the integral of \(f\) against the product measure equals each of the two iterated integrals, in either order, provided a stated integrability hypothesis holds. Theorem 1.2 proves it for finite sets. Assumption F in §2 gives the general statement.

Theorem 1.2 (Fubini for finite sets)

Assumptions.

  1. \(X\) and \(Y\) are finite sets.
  2. \(\mu_1\) and \(\mu_2\) are nonnegative weights on \(X\) and \(Y\), and \(\mu(x,y) = \mu_1(x)\mu_2(y)\).
  3. \(f\) is a real function on \(X \times Y\).

Statement. Under Assumptions 1 to 3, \(I_{1} = \sum_{(x,y)} f(x,y)\,\mu(x,y) = I_{2}\).

Proof

Write \(D = \sum_{(x,y) \in X \times Y} f(x,y)\,\mu_1(x)\,\mu_2(y)\). Because \(X \times Y\) is finite, the terms of \(D\) may be grouped in any way. Group them by \(x\). For fixed \(x\), the distributive law gives \(\mu_1(x)\sum_{y} f(x,y)\mu_2(y) = \sum_{y} f(x,y)\,\mu_1(x)\mu_2(y)\), so \(D = \sum_{x} \mu_1(x) \sum_{y} f(x,y)\mu_2(y) = I_{1}\). Group the same terms by \(y\). For fixed \(y\), the distributive law gives \(\mu_2(y)\sum_{x} f(x,y)\mu_1(x) = \sum_{x} f(x,y)\mu_1(x)\mu_2(y)\), so \(D = \sum_{y} \mu_2(y) \sum_{x} f(x,y)\mu_1(x) = I_{2}\). Each step uses only the distributive law and the associativity of finite sums, so \(I_{1} = D = I_{2}\).

2The general statement, as an assumption

A measure space \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mu)\) is \(\sigma\)-finite when \(\Omega\) is a countable union of sets of finite measure. Each space in this lesson is either finite or the unit square with area (Lebesgue measure), and each is \(\sigma\)-finite, because each is a countable union of sets of finite measure. The product \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal F_1 \otimes \mathcal F_2\) is the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra on \(\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2\) that contains every rectangle \(A \times B\) with \(A \in \mathcal F_1\) and \(B \in \mathcal F_2\). A function is measurable for a \(\sigma\)-algebra when its preimages of the open sets are in that \(\sigma\)-algebra. The statements below are not proved on this page, and no grade is given to them.

Assumption F (Fubini, general form; not proved here)
  1. \((\Omega_1, \mathcal F_1, \mu_1)\) and \((\Omega_2, \mathcal F_2, \mu_2)\) are \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces, and \(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2\) is the product measure on \(\mathcal F_1 \otimes \mathcal F_2\).
  2. \(f\) is measurable for \(\mathcal F_1 \otimes \mathcal F_2\).
  3. \(\int |f|\, d(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2) < \infty\).

Conclusion. Both iterated integrals of \(f\) exist, and each equals \(\int f\, d(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2)\).

Assumption T (Tonelli, nonnegative case; not proved here)

Under Assumptions 1 and 2 of Assumption F, let \(f \ge 0\) be measurable for \(\mathcal F_1 \otimes \mathcal F_2\). Then the two iterated integrals of \(f\) are equal as values in \([0, \infty]\). No integrability hypothesis is needed. Assumption T is not used in any proof on this page.

Remark 2.1 (where the hypothesis matters)

Assumption F requires \(\int |f| < \infty\), which is stronger than the existence of each iterated integral. Section 3 gives a function for which both iterated integrals exist as finite numbers, which differ. For that function the integrability hypothesis fails, so Assumption F gives no equality.

3A function that is not integrable

Let \(f(x,y) = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)^2\) on the unit square \([0,1]^2\), except at the single point \((0,0)\), where \(f\) is not defined. A single point does not change an integral, so the value at \((0,0)\) is irrelevant to every statement below.

Proposition 3.1 (the two iterated integrals of f)

Assumptions.

  1. \(f\) is as defined above, and \(x\) and \(y\) range over \((0,1]\) in the two formulas.
  2. For each fixed \(x > 0\), the function \(y \mapsto f(x,y)\) is continuous on \([0,1]\), because its denominator is at least \(x^4 > 0\). For each fixed \(y > 0\), the function \(x \mapsto f(x,y)\) is continuous on \([0,1]\) by the same reasoning.
  3. The fundamental theorem of calculus holds: if \(G' = g\) on \([a,b]\) with \(g\) continuous, then \(\int_a^b g = G(b) - G(a)\). This is standard calculus and is used without proof.

Statement. For \(x \in (0,1]\), \(\int_0^1 f(x,y)\,dy = 1/(1+x^2)\). For \(y \in (0,1]\), \(\int_0^1 f(x,y)\,dx = -1/(1+y^2)\). Hence \(\int_0^1 \big(\int_0^1 f\,dy\big)dx = \pi/4\) and \(\int_0^1 \big(\int_0^1 f\,dx\big)dy = -\pi/4\).

Proof

Let \(G(y) = y/(x^2 + y^2)\) for fixed \(x > 0\). By the quotient rule, \(G'(y) = \big((x^2+y^2) - 2y^2\big)/(x^2+y^2)^2 = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2 = f(x,y)\). By Assumption 3, \(\int_0^1 f(x,y)\,dy = G(1) - G(0) = 1/(x^2+1) - 0 = 1/(1+x^2)\), which uses Assumption 2 for continuity. Next let \(H(x) = -x/(x^2+y^2)\) for fixed \(y > 0\). By the quotient rule, \(H'(x) = -\big((x^2+y^2) - 2x^2\big)/(x^2+y^2)^2 = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2 = f(x,y)\). So \(\int_0^1 f(x,y)\,dx = H(1) - H(0) = -1/(1+y^2)\). Integrating the first formula over \(x\) gives \(\int_0^1 dx/(1+x^2) = \arctan 1 - \arctan 0 = \pi/4\), using the antiderivative \(\arctan\) of \(1/(1+x^2)\), a standard fact. Integrating the second over \(y\) gives \(-\pi/4\) by the same antiderivative.

Proposition 3.2 (f is not integrable on the square)

Assumptions.

  1. \(f\) is as defined in §3.
  2. For \(a > 0\) and \(0 \le y \le a < x\), \(f(x,y) \ge 0\), because \(x^2 > y^2\).
  3. Assumption C: for a function continuous on a closed rectangle, the integral over the rectangle equals either iterated integral. This is standard calculus and is used without proof.
  4. The integral is additive over disjoint sets and monotone (a larger nonnegative function has a larger integral). These are basic properties of the integral, used without proof.

Statement. \(\int_{[0,1]^2} f^{+} = \infty\) and \(\int_{[0,1]^2} f^{-} = \infty\). Hence \(\int_{[0,1]^2} |f| = \infty\), and \(f\) is not integrable.

Proof

For \(k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots\), let \(a_k = 2^{-(k+1)}\) and \(Q_k = (a_k, 2a_k] \times [0, a_k]\). Then \(2a_{k+1} = a_k\), so the \(x\)-interval of \(Q_{k+1}\) ends where the \(x\)-interval of \(Q_k\) begins, and the blocks are pairwise disjoint. Each \(Q_k\) lies in \([0,1]^2\), since \(2a_0 = 1\). On \(Q_k\) we have \(x > a_k \ge y\), so \(f \ge 0\) on \(Q_k\) by Assumption 2. Let \(c_k = \int_{Q_k} f\). By Assumption 3 on the closed rectangle \([a_k, 2a_k] \times [0, a_k]\), the inner integral is \(\int_0^{a_k} f(x,y)\,dy = G(a_k) - G(0) = a_k/(x^2 + a_k^2)\), with \(G\) as in the proof of Proposition 3.1. Then \(c_k = \int_{a_k}^{2a_k} a_k/(x^2 + a_k^2)\,dx\). Substitute \(x = a_k t\): \(c_k = \int_1^2 dt/(1 + t^2) = \arctan 2 - \arctan 1\). This does not depend on \(k\), so \(c_k = c = \arctan 2 - \pi/4\). Since \(f^{+} \ge f\,\indic_{Q_0 \cup \dots \cup Q_{N-1}}\) on the square, Assumption 4 gives \(\int_{[0,1]^2} f^{+} \ge N c\) for every \(N\). Since \(c > 0\), the integral is infinite. Swapping \(x\) and \(y\) turns \(f\) into \(-f\) and maps the square to itself, so the same argument with the blocks reflected gives \(\int f^{-} = \infty\).

Remark 3.3 (what the lab checks and what it does not)

Lab 1 computes the two iterated integrals numerically, checks block 0 by quadrature, and prints partial sums of the closed-form block value. The quadrature agrees with \(\pm\pi/4\) to about \(10^{-7}\). That agreement tests each iterated integral. It does not test integrability, which is what Proposition 3.2 settles.

Lab 1 · The two iterated integrals of f, in closed form and by quadrature

The inputs are a fixed formula, not data, and no random draws are used. Part A prints the two iterated integrals in closed form: \(+\pi/4\) for the \(y\)-integral first and \(-\pi/4\) for the \(x\)-integral first (Proposition 3.1). Part B prints both by quadrature, and the error against the closed form. Part C prints the closed-form integral of \(f\) over a block \(Q_k\) of Proposition 3.2, checks block 0 by quadrature, and prints partial sums of that closed-form value over six blocks. Before running, the expected result is that both quadrature values agree with the closed forms to about \(10^{-7}\), that block 0 agrees with its closed form, and that the partial sums grow in proportion to the number of blocks.

"""M6 L05 lab 1: the two iterated integrals of f(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2) / (x^2 + y^2)^2 on the unit square.

Inputs are a fixed formula, not data, and no random draws are used, so every run prints the same lines.
Part A: the iterated integrals in closed form, from the antiderivatives in y and in x.
Part B: the same two iterated integrals by quadrature (a midpoint rule on the outer variable, and a composite Simpson
rule on the inner variable after the substitution y = s^2 (first order) or x = s^2 (second order)), with the error
against the closed form.
Part C: the closed-form integral of f over a dyadic block Q_k that lies inside the set where f >= 0, and the partial
sums over six blocks. The closed form gives the same value for every block (the scaling in Proposition 3.2), so the
partial sums grow in proportion to the number of blocks. Block 0 alone is checked by quadrature.

The closed forms are exact up to floating-point rounding. The quadrature values are numerical approximations, and
their error is printed. The lab does not decide integrability by quadrature; Proposition 3.2 proves it.
"""

import math
from collections.abc import Callable


def f(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """The integrand (x^2 - y^2) / (x^2 + y^2)^2, for (x, y) other than (0, 0)."""
    denom = x * x + y * y
    return (x * x - y * y) / (denom * denom)


def simpson(g: Callable[[float], float], a: float, b: float, n: int) -> float:
    """Composite Simpson rule on [a, b] with n subintervals; n must be even."""
    h = (b - a) / n
    total = g(a) + g(b)
    for i in range(1, n):
        total += (4.0 if i % 2 == 1 else 2.0) * g(a + i * h)
    return total * h / 3.0


def inner_over_y(x: float, m: int) -> float:
    """The integral over y in [0, 1] of f(x, y) for a fixed x > 0, by Simpson on y = s^2, s in [0, 1]."""
    return simpson(lambda s: f(x, s * s) * 2.0 * s, 0.0, 1.0, m)


def inner_over_x(y: float, m: int) -> float:
    """The integral over x in [0, 1] of f(x, y) for a fixed y > 0, by Simpson on x = s^2, s in [0, 1]."""
    return simpson(lambda s: f(s * s, y) * 2.0 * s, 0.0, 1.0, m)


def block_integral_closed(a: float) -> float:
    """The integral of f over (a, 2a] x [0, a] for a > 0, by the substitution x = a t (Proposition 3.2).

    The value arctan 2 - arctan 1 does not depend on a > 0. For a <= 0 no block is defined, and the value is 0.
    """
    return math.atan(2.0) - math.atan(1.0) if a > 0 else 0.0


def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A, B and C. Every line is a fixed closed form or a deterministic quadrature."""
    quarter_pi = math.pi / 4.0
    print("Part A: the two iterated integrals in closed form")
    print(f"dy_first_closed {quarter_pi:.10f}")
    print(f"dx_first_closed {-quarter_pi:.10f}")

    print("Part B: the same two iterated integrals by quadrature")
    outer_n = 400
    h = 1.0 / outer_n
    dy_first = sum(inner_over_y((i + 0.5) * h, 4000) for i in range(outer_n)) * h
    print(f"dy_first_quadrature {dy_first:.10f} error {dy_first - quarter_pi:+.2e}")
    dx_first = sum(inner_over_x((j + 0.5) * h, 4000) for j in range(outer_n)) * h
    print(f"dx_first_quadrature {dx_first:.10f} error {dx_first + quarter_pi:+.2e}")

    print("Part C: integral of the positive part of f over dyadic blocks where f >= 0")
    c = block_integral_closed(0.5)
    block_check = simpson(lambda x: simpson(lambda y: f(x, y), 0.0, 0.5, 200), 0.5, 1.0, 200)
    print(f"block_integral_closed {c:.10f}")
    print(f"block_0_quadrature {block_check:.10f} error {block_check - c:+.2e}")
    total = 0.0
    for k in range(6):
        a = 0.5 ** (k + 1)
        total += block_integral_closed(a)
        print(f"blocks_0_to_{k} partial_sum {total:.10f} block_count {k + 1}")
    print(f"partial_sums_equal_count_times_block {abs(total - 6 * c) < 1e-12}")

Part A gives the two closed forms, and the two orders disagree in sign: \(+\pi/4\) and \(-\pi/4\) to ten decimal places. Part B's quadrature values are \(0.7853982936\) and \(-0.7853982936\). Their errors against the closed forms are \(+1.30\times10^{-7}\) and \(-1.30\times10^{-7}\). 4Where the canon breaks

  • Both iterated integrals existing. For the function of §3, both iterated integrals exist and are finite (\(\pm\pi/4\)), yet they differ. Assumption F requires \(\int |f| < \infty\), which fails (Proposition 3.2). Existence of both iterated integrals is therefore not enough to justify an interchange.
  • Nonnegative integrands. For \(f \ge 0\), Assumption T gives equality of the iterated integrals with no integrability hypothesis. The equality can then be \(+\infty\). Assumption T is not proved on this page.
  • Finite sums. Theorem 1.2 uses no limit, because a finite sum can be regrouped without one. Its statement and proof concern finite sets.
  • Non-\(\sigma\)-finite spaces. Assumption F requires \(\sigma\)-finite measures. The spaces this lesson uses are finite sets and the unit square with its area, and both are \(\sigma\)-finite. This lesson gives no example of a space that is not \(\sigma\)-finite.

5Misconceptions

  1. “If both iterated integrals exist, they must be equal.”

    For the function of §3, the \(y\)-first integral is \(+\pi/4\) and the \(x\)-first integral is \(-\pi/4\) (Proposition 3.1). Both exist and are finite, and they differ. Part A of Lab 1 prints both values.

  2. “Fubini's theorem needs only the existence of each iterated integral.”

    Assumption F requires \(\int |f| < \infty\). For the function of §3 that integral is infinite (Proposition 3.2), so the theorem gives no equality, and the iterated integrals differ as shown in the previous item.

  3. “The quadrature in Lab 1 agrees with \(\pm\pi/4\), so the interchange is justified.”

    Part B shows agreement of each iterated integral with its closed form to about \(10^{-7}\). That is a statement about two one-dimensional integrals. It is not a test of \(\int |f| < \infty\). Proposition 3.2 gives that test, and it fails. Remark 3.3 states this point.

Mechanics

M1 · A finite double sum in both orders

Let \(X = \{a, b\}\) and \(Y = \{c, d\}\), with \(\mu_1(a) = \mu_1(b) = 1/2\) and \(\mu_2(c) = 1/3\), \(\mu_2(d) = 2/3\). Let \(f(a,c) = 1\), \(f(a,d) = 2\), \(f(b,c) = 3\), \(f(b,d) = 0\). Compute \(I_1\) and \(I_2\).

Solution

For \(x = a\), the inner sum is \(1 \cdot \tfrac13 + 2 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac53\). For \(x = b\), it is \(3 \cdot \tfrac13 + 0 = 1\). So \(I_1 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac53 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = \tfrac43\). For \(y = c\), the inner sum is \(1 \cdot \tfrac12 + 3 \cdot \tfrac12 = 2\). For \(y = d\), it is \(2 \cdot \tfrac12 + 0 = 1\). So \(I_2 = \tfrac13 \cdot 2 + \tfrac23 \cdot 1 = \tfrac43\). Both equal \(\tfrac43\), as Theorem 1.2 requires.

M2 · A product of one-variable integrals

Compute \(\int_0^1 \int_0^2 x y^2\,dy\,dx\) and the same integral with the order reversed.

Solution

In the first order, \(\int_0^2 x y^2\,dy = x \cdot 8/3\), then \(\int_0^1 (8/3) x\,dx = (8/3)(1/2) = 4/3\). In the second order, \(\int_0^1 x y^2\,dx = y^2/2\), then \(\int_0^2 (y^2/2)\,dy = (1/2)(8/3) = 4/3\). The two orders agree. This is the continuous case of Assumption C (stated in §3, not proved here), since \(xy^2\) is continuous on the closed rectangle \([0,1] \times [0,2]\).

M3 · The inner integral at x = 1/2

Using Proposition 3.1, find \(\int_0^1 f(1/2, y)\,dy\) as an exact fraction.

Solution

Proposition 3.1 gives \(1/(1 + x^2)\). At \(x = 1/2\), \(1 + 1/4 = 5/4\), so the value is \(4/5\). Directly: \(G(y) = y/(1/4 + y^2)\) has \(G(1) = 1/(5/4) = 4/5\) and \(G(0) = 0\), so the integral is \(4/5\).

Proof

P1 · Theorem 1.2 when X has one point

Prove Theorem 1.2 in the case \(X = \{x_0\}\) and state which step uses the distributive law.

Solution

With one point, \(I_1 = \mu_1(x_0)\sum_{y} f(x_0,y)\mu_2(y)\). The double sum is \(\sum_{y} f(x_0,y)\mu_1(x_0)\mu_2(y)\), which equals \(I_1\) by the distributive law applied to the finite sum over \(y\). Also \(I_2 = \sum_{y} \mu_2(y) f(x_0,y)\mu_1(x_0)\), which is the same sum, by commutativity of multiplication. So \(I_1 = I_2 = D\). The distributive law is the step that moves \(\mu_1(x_0)\) inside the sum over \(y\).

P2 · The antiderivative in y

Verify that \(G(y) = y/(x^2 + y^2)\) satisfies \(G'(y) = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2\), for fixed \(x > 0\).

Solution

By the quotient rule, \(G'(y) = \big[(x^2 + y^2)\cdot 1 - y \cdot 2y\big]/(x^2+y^2)^2 = (x^2 + y^2 - 2y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2 = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2\). This is \(f(x,y)\), as used in the proof of Proposition 3.1.

P3 · The blocks are disjoint

With \(a_k = 2^{-(k+1)}\) and \(Q_k = (a_k, 2a_k] \times [0, a_k]\), show that \(Q_k\) and \(Q_{k+1}\) are disjoint.

Solution

The \(x\)-interval of \(Q_{k+1}\) is \((a_{k+1}, 2a_{k+1}] = (a_k/2, a_k]\), because \(a_{k+1} = a_k/2\). The \(x\)-interval of \(Q_k\) is \((a_k, 2a_k]\). These two intervals share no point, since the first ends at \(a_k\), which the second excludes. Two sets whose \(x\)-projections are disjoint are disjoint, so \(Q_k\) and \(Q_{k+1}\) are disjoint. The same holds for every pair \(k < k'\), because the intervals \((a_k, 2a_k]\) are ordered and disjoint.

Empirical

E1 · The size of the quadrature error

From Part B of Lab 1, compute the difference between the \(y\)-first quadrature value and \(\pi/4\), and compare it with the printed error.

Solution

Part B prints \(0.7853982936\) for the quadrature and \(0.7853981634\) for the closed form (Part A). The difference is \(0.7853982936 - 0.7853981634 = 0.0000001302 = 1.302\times10^{-7}\). The printed error is \(+1.30\times10^{-7}\), which agrees with this difference to the three digits printed.

E2 · Blocks needed to pass 3

Using the printed block integral \(0.3217505544\), find the smallest number of blocks \(N\) for which \(N \times 0.3217505544\) exceeds 3.

Solution

Nine blocks give \(9 \times 0.3217505544 = 2.8957549896\), which is below 3. Ten blocks give \(3.217505544\), which is above 3. So \(N = 10\). The value is computed from the printed block integral; it is not a printed partial sum, because Part C prints only six partial sums.

E3 · The sign of the second order

Read Part B of Lab 1. Which iterated integral has the negative value, and by how much does its quadrature error differ in sign from the other?

Solution

The \(x\)-first quadrature prints \(-0.7853982936\), which is the negative value. Its error is \(-1.30\times10^{-7}\), the negative of the error of the \(y\)-first quadrature, \(+1.30\times10^{-7}\). The two errors have equal size and opposite sign. This is consistent with the closed forms \(\pm\pi/4\).

Research

R1 · A nonnegative example with infinite integrals

Let \(g(x,y) = 1/(xy)\) on \((0,1]^2\). Show that both iterated integrals are \(+\infty\), and that \(\int g = \infty\), so Assumption F does not apply. Then state what Assumption T says about \(g\), and note that your argument above does not use it.

Solution

For fixed \(x > 0\), \(\int_0^1 dy/(xy)\) diverges, since \(\int_\epsilon^1 dy/y = \ln(1/\epsilon) \to \infty\) as \(\epsilon \to 0\). So the inner integral is \(+\infty\) for every \(x\), and the outer integral of \(+\infty\) is \(+\infty\). The same holds with the order reversed, so both iterated integrals equal \(+\infty\). For the double integral, \(\int_{[\epsilon,1]^2} g = \big(\ln(1/\epsilon)\big)^2\) for \(0 < \epsilon < 1\). These sets increase to \((0,1]^2\) as \(\epsilon \to 0\), so by monotone convergence (L04) \(\int g = \lim_{\epsilon \to 0} \big(\ln(1/\epsilon)\big)^2 = +\infty\). Assumption F needs \(\int |g| < \infty\), which fails. Assumption T, for nonnegative \(g\), gives that the two iterated integrals are equal as values in \([0,\infty]\); here both are \(+\infty\). This solution does not use Assumption T.

R2 · Symmetric truncation

Let \(S_\epsilon = [0,1]^2\) minus the open disk of radius \(\epsilon\) about the origin. Show that \(\int_{S_\epsilon} f = 0\) for every \(\epsilon \in (0,1)\), and explain why this does not make \(f\) integrable on the square.

Solution

On \(S_\epsilon\), \(f\) is bounded and continuous, so its integral exists. The swap \(\sigma(x,y) = (y,x)\) maps \(S_\epsilon\) onto itself, and \(f(\sigma(x,y)) = -f(x,y)\). Invariance of the integral under this measure-preserving map gives \(\int_{S_\epsilon} f = \int_{S_\epsilon} f\circ\sigma = -\int_{S_\epsilon} f\), so \(\int_{S_\epsilon} f = 0\). The value \(0\) is the limit of the truncated integrals, but Proposition 3.2 shows that \(\int |f| = \infty\) on the full square. A limit of integrals over truncations does not make \(f\) integrable, because the integrability in Assumption F is of \(|f|\) on the whole space.

R3 · Where Assumption F gives equality

Under Assumption F, suppose one iterated integral of \(|f|\) is finite. Does the theorem then conclude that the double integral of \(f\) is finite? Explain using only Assumption F as stated.

Solution

No. Assumption F requires \(\int |f|\,d(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2) < \infty\) for the product measure, which is the double integral of \(|f|\), not one iterated integral. The theorem's conclusion covers the iterated integrals of \(f\) and the double integral of \(f\). A finite iterated integral of \(|f|\) is not one of the hypotheses. To use Assumption F, the double integral of \(|f|\) must be checked directly, as Proposition 3.2 does for the function of §3.
