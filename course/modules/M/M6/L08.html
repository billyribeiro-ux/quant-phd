A filtration is the record of what is known at each time, and a martingale is a process whose best forecast of its next value, given that record, is its current value. It is the formal version of a fair game. This lesson defines both on a finite tree. It proves that the fair \(\pm 1\) walk is a martingale on its natural filtration, and that the biased walk is not, because given the record each step adds \(2p - 1\) on average, where \(p\) is defined in §2 (Proposition 2.3). Lab 1 checks both exactly on all 1024 paths of a ten-step tree. The stopping times of L09 and the bounded optional stopping theorem of L10 build on these.

1Information over time

Let \(\Omega\) be a finite set of paths, with weights \(p(\omega) > 0\) that sum to 1. A process is a finite sequence \((X_0, X_1, \dots, X_N)\) of random variables on \(\Omega\). For a fixed \(k\), the record of the first \(k\) values \((X_0, \dots, X_k)\) partitions \(\Omega\) into cells, one for each possible record. A cell of this partition is an event that is known once the record is known. The partition is the information at time \(k\).

Definition 1.1 (filtration)

A filtration on \(\Omega\) is a family \(\mathcal F_0 \subseteq \mathcal F_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \mathcal F_N\) of sub-\(\sigma\)-algebras of the power set of \(\Omega\). Here each \(\mathcal F_k\) is generated by a partition of \(\Omega\) into cells, as in L07. The filtration is increasing: information is never lost as time passes. A process \((X_k)\) is adapted to the filtration when each \(X_k\) is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable, which means that \(X_k\) is constant on each cell of the partition at time \(k\), so the value of \(X_k\) is known at time \(k\).

Definition 1.2 (natural filtration)

For a process \((X_k)\), the natural filtration has \(\mathcal F_k\) generated by the record \((X_0, \dots, X_k)\). Its cells at time \(k\) are the sets of paths that share the same record up to time \(k\).

2The walk, its filtration, and martingales

Fix \(N \ge 1\). Let \(\Omega\) be the set of paths \(\omega = (x_1, \dots, x_N)\) with each \(x_i \in \{+1, -1\}\), and let the weight of a path be \(\prod_{i} q(x_i)\), where \(q(+1) = p\) and \(q(-1) = 1 - p\) for a fixed \(p \in (0,1)\). Let \(X_i(\omega) = x_i\) be the step, and \(S_0 = 0\), \(S_k = X_1 + \cdots + X_k\). The fair walk has \(p = 1/2\). The biased walk has \(p \ne 1/2\). The walk and its natural filtration \(\mathcal F_k\) are the objects of this lesson. The level-\(k\) cells are the \(2^k\) sets of paths with a common prefix of length \(k\).

Proposition 2.1 (the natural filtration of the walk)

Assumptions.

  1. \(\Omega\), \(p\), the weights and the steps \(X_i\) are as defined above.
  2. \(\mathcal F_k\) is the natural filtration of the walk, generated by the prefix of length \(k\).

Statement. (a) \(\mathcal F_0\) is the trivial family \(\{\emptyset, \Omega\}\), and \(\mathcal F_N\) contains every subset of \(\Omega\). (b) \(\mathcal F_k \subseteq \mathcal F_{k+1}\). (c) \(S_k\) is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable, so the walk is adapted.

Proof

(a) The prefix of length 0 is empty, so there is one cell, \(\Omega\). The prefix of length \(N\) is the whole path, so the cells at time \(N\) are single paths, and every subset is a union of them. (b) Each level-\(k\) cell is the disjoint union of the two level-\((k+1)\) cells with the same prefix followed by \(+1\) and \(-1\). So each generator of \(\mathcal F_k\) is in \(\mathcal F_{k+1}\), and hence \(\mathcal F_k \subseteq \mathcal F_{k+1}\). (c) \(S_k\) depends only on the first \(k\) steps, so it is constant on each level-\(k\) cell. By the characterization of L07 (Proposition 1.1), it is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable.

Definition 2.2 (martingale)

An adapted process \((M_k)_{k=0}^N\) on a filtration \((\mathcal F_k)\) is a martingale when \(\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k] = M_k\) for every \(k = 0, \dots, N-1\). The conditional expectation is the L2 conditional expectation of L07, which on a partition-generated \(\mathcal F_k\) is the cell average of Theorem 2.2 in L07.

Proposition 2.2 (the martingale test on cells)

Assumptions.

  1. \((M_k)\) is adapted to the natural filtration of the walk, as in Proposition 2.1.
  2. Each cell at time \(k\) has positive probability.

Statement. \((M_k)\) is a martingale if and only if, for every \(k\) and every level-\(k\) cell \(B\), the average \(\E[M_{k+1}\indic_B]/\Prob(B)\) equals the value of \(M_k\) on \(B\).

Proof

By Theorem 2.2 in L07, \(\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k]\) is the function whose value on each level-\(k\) cell \(B\) is \(\E[M_{k+1}\indic_B]/\Prob(B)\). Since \(M_k\) is adapted, it has a single value on each such cell. Two functions that are constant on each cell are equal if and only if they agree on each cell. So \(\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k] = M_k\) if and only if the cell test holds for every \(B\) at time \(k\).

Proposition 2.3 (the walk's increment on each cell)

Assumptions.

  1. The walk, its weights and its natural filtration are as defined above, with \(p \in (0,1)\).
  2. The weight of a path is the product of its step weights \(q(x_i)\).

Statement. For every \(k < N\) and every level-\(k\) cell \(B\) with prefix \(h\), the average of \(S_{k+1}\) over \(B\) is \(S_k(h) + (2p - 1)\), where \(S_k(h)\) is the common value of \(S_k\) on \(B\). Hence the walk is a martingale if and only if \(p = 1/2\).

Proof

Let \(B\) be the cell with prefix \(h = (h_1, \dots, h_k)\). The weight of a path in \(B\) factors as \(\prod_{i \le k} q(h_i)\) times \(\prod_{i > k} q(x_i)\). The sum of the second factor over the tail \((x_{k+1}, \dots, x_N)\) is 1, because \(\sum_{x} q(x) = p + (1 - p) = 1\) at each step. So \(\Prob(B) = \prod_{i \le k} q(h_i)\), and for \(s \in \{+1,-1\}\), \(\Prob(B \cap \{X_{k+1} = s\}) = \Prob(B)\,q(s)\), because the sum over the later steps is again 1. Therefore the average of \(X_{k+1}\) over \(B\) is \(q(+1)\cdot(+1) + q(-1)\cdot(-1) = p - (1 - p) = 2p - 1\). Since \(S_{k+1} = S_k + X_{k+1}\) and \(S_k\) is constant on \(B\), the average of \(S_{k+1}\) over \(B\) is \(S_k(h) + (2p - 1)\). By Proposition 2.2, the walk is a martingale if and only if this equals \(S_k(h)\) on every cell, which holds if and only if \(2p - 1 = 0\), that is \(p = 1/2\). The cell \(B\) with \(k = 0\) has \(\Prob(B) = 1\), so the failure for \(p \ne 1/2\) occurs on a cell of positive probability.

Remark 2.4 (what the fair walk satisfies)

For \(p = 1/2\), Proposition 2.3 gives a cell average of \(S_k(h)\) on every cell at every level. This is the statement that \(\E[S_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k] = S_k\), which makes the fair walk a martingale on its natural filtration. Lab 1 checks the cell test on all 1023 cells of the ten-step tree.

Lab 1 · The fair and biased walks on a ten-step tree

The inputs are a fixed finite tree of \(2^{10} = 1024\) paths, not data, and no random draws are used. The walk has steps \(\pm 1\). Part A checks that the natural filtration is nested and that the fair measure has total mass 1. For each walk, Part B checks the cell test of Proposition 2.2 at every level \(k = 0, \dots, 9\) and every cell, with exact fractions, and prints the increment on the first cell. Before running, the expected result is that the fair walk passes every cell at every level, that the biased walk with \(p = 1/3\) fails every cell at every level, with the first cell's increment equal to \(2p - 1 = -1/3\) at each level, and that the expected final value is \(N(2p - 1)\) in each case.

"""M6 L08 lab 1: the martingale property of a +1/-1 walk on its natural filtration, checked on all 2^10 paths.

Inputs are a fixed finite probability space, not data. The walk is S_0 = 0, S_k = X_1 + ... + X_k, where each step
X_i is +1 with probability p and -1 with probability 1 - p, independently. Two walks are tested on the same tree:
the fair walk with p = 1/2 and a biased walk with p = 1/3. No random draws are used.

The filtration F_k is generated by the first k steps. Its cells at level k are the sets of paths that share a
prefix of length k, so there are 2^k cells at level k. For each cell A at level k the lab computes the cell average
of S_{k+1}, E[S_{k+1} 1_A] / P(A), and compares it with S_k on the same cell. A walk is a martingale on this tree
when the two agree on every cell at every level k = 0, ..., 9.

Every probability and value is a Fraction, so every comparison is exact.
"""

from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product

N = 10
FAIR = Fraction(1, 2)
BIASED = Fraction(1, 3)


def path_probability(path: tuple[int, ...], p: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """The probability of a path of +1/-1 steps when each step is +1 with probability p, independently."""
    out = Fraction(1)
    for step in path:
        out *= p if step == 1 else 1 - p
    return out


def partial_sum(path: tuple[int, ...], k: int) -> int:
    """S_k along a path: the sum of its first k steps, with S_0 = 0."""
    return sum(path[:k])


def level_check(paths: list[tuple[int, ...]], p: Fraction, k: int) -> tuple[int, int, Fraction]:
    """At level k, count the cells and the paths where the cell average of S_{k+1} differs from S_k.

    Returns (cells that agree on every path in them, paths whose cell average differs from S_k, the common
    increment E[S_{k+1} - S_k | A] of the first cell). The increment is printed only as a check of the formula 2p - 1.
    """
    cells: dict[tuple[int, ...], list[tuple[int, ...]]] = {}
    for path in paths:
        cells.setdefault(path[:k], []).append(path)
    agreeing = 0
    mismatched = 0
    first_increment = Fraction(0)
    for index, (prefix, members) in enumerate(cells.items()):
        mass = sum((path_probability(q, p) for q in members), Fraction(0))
        cell_average = sum((path_probability(q, p) * partial_sum(q, k + 1) for q in members), Fraction(0)) / mass
        level_value = partial_sum(prefix, k)
        differing = sum(1 for q in members if cell_average != level_value)
        mismatched += differing
        if differing == 0:
            agreeing += 1
        if index == 0:
            first_increment = cell_average - level_value
    return agreeing, mismatched, first_increment


def nested(paths: list[tuple[int, ...]]) -> bool:
    """Each cell at level k is the union of exactly two cells at level k + 1, so the filtration increases."""
    for k in range(N):
        coarse = {path[:k] for path in paths}
        fine = {path[: k + 1] for path in paths}
        for prefix in coarse:
            children = {(*prefix, 1), (*prefix, -1)}
            if not children <= fine:
                return False
    return True


def main() -> None:
    """Print the filtration check, then the level-by-level martingale check for the fair and the biased walk."""
    paths = list(product((1, -1), repeat=N))
    print("Part A: the natural filtration is nested, and the fair measure has total mass 1")
    print(f"paths {len(paths)} steps {N}")
    print(f"filtration_nested {nested(paths)}")
    total_mass = sum((path_probability(q, FAIR) for q in paths), Fraction(0))
    print(f"total_mass_fair {total_mass}")

    print("Part B: the cell test of Proposition 2.2 at every level, for the fair and the biased walk")
    for label, p in (("fair", FAIR), ("biased", BIASED)):
        print(f"Walk {label} with p = {p}")
        cells_agreeing = 0
        cells_total = 0
        mismatched_total = 0
        for k in range(N):
            agreeing, mismatched, increment = level_check(paths, p, k)
            cells_total += 2**k
            cells_agreeing += agreeing
            mismatched_total += mismatched
            print(f"level {k} cells {2**k} agreeing {agreeing} mismatched_paths {mismatched} increment {increment}")
        print(f"cells_agreeing {cells_agreeing} of {cells_total}")
        print(f"mismatched_paths_all_levels {mismatched_total}")
        expected_mean = sum((path_probability(q, p) * partial_sum(q, N) for q in paths), Fraction(0))
        print(f"expected_S_N {expected_mean} equals 2p-1 times N: {expected_mean == N * (2 * p - 1)}")

3Where the canon breaks

  • Zero mean without the history. A process can have zero expected increment and still fail to be a martingale, because the martingale property is a condition on the conditional mean given the record (Misconception 1). The unconditional mean is a consequence of the martingale property, not its definition.
  • A filtration can hide the future. The natural filtration at time \(k\) reveals only the first \(k\) steps. The cell test is computed from the cells of that filtration, and for the biased walk it fails on every cell of the ten-step tree (Lab 1).
  • Bias. The biased walk has increments of conditional mean \(2p - 1\). Proposition 2.3 shows that the drift is exactly this constant on every cell, and it is zero only for \(p = 1/2\).
  • Infinite horizons. The tree here has \(N = 10\) steps. The lesson does not treat martingales with infinitely many steps, where the cell test and the finite sums are no longer the whole argument.

4Misconceptions

  1. “A process with zero expected increments is a martingale.”

    Let \(M_0 = 0\), \(M_1 = X_1\) with \(X_1\) fair, and \(M_2 = 2X_1\), so the second increment \(X_1\) repeats the first. Then \(\E[M_2 - M_1] = \E[X_1] = 0\), but \(\E[M_2 \mid \mathcal F_1] = 2X_1 \ne X_1 = M_1\). The martingale property is a condition on the conditional mean given the record, and Proposition 2.2 tests it cell by cell.

  2. “The natural filtration reveals the whole path from time 0.”

    At time \(k\) the natural filtration reveals only the first \(k\) steps. At time 0 it is the trivial family \(\{\emptyset, \Omega\}\) (Proposition 2.1(a)), so nothing is known about the walk before the first step.

  3. “Subtracting the mean path \(k(2p-1)\) from a biased walk gives a martingale only when \(p = 1/2\).”

    The compensated walk \(M_k = S_k - k(2p - 1)\) is a martingale for every \(p\). On each cell, the average of \(M_{k+1}\) is \(S_k(h) + (2p-1) - (k+1)(2p-1) = S_k(h) - k(2p-1) = M_k(h)\), as Problem P2 shows. The lab does not print this check; the claim rests on Proposition 2.3 and Problem P2.

5Problem set

Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.

Mechanics

M1 · The fair walk after two steps

For the fair walk with \(N = 2\), compute \(\E[S_2 \mid \mathcal F_1]\) on the cell where \(X_1 = +1\), and on the cell where \(X_1 = -1\).

Solution

On the cell \(X_1 = +1\), \(S_1 = 1\), and \(S_2 = 1 + X_2\). With \(p = 1/2\), the cell average of \(X_2\) is \(\tfrac12(+1) + \tfrac12(-1) = 0\). So \(\E[S_2 \mid \mathcal F_1] = 1 = S_1\) there. On the cell \(X_1 = -1\), \(S_1 = -1\) and the same argument gives \(\E[S_2 \mid \mathcal F_1] = -1 = S_1\). Both cells pass the test.

M2 · The biased walk's expected final value

For the biased walk with \(p = 1/3\) and \(N = 2\), compute \(\E[S_2]\) from the four path weights.

Solution

The paths \((+,+)\), \((+,-)\), \((-,+)\), \((-,-)\) have weights \(1/9\), \(2/9\), \(2/9\), \(4/9\), and final values \(2\), \(0\), \(0\), \(-2\). So \(\E[S_2] = 2/9 + 0 + 0 - 8/9 = -6/9 = -2/3\). This is \(N(2p - 1) = 2 \cdot (-1/3)\), as Proposition 2.3 predicts through the increments.

M3 · The biased walk's cell average

For the biased walk with \(p = 1/3\) and \(N = 2\), compute \(\E[S_2 \mid \mathcal F_1]\) on the cell \(X_1 = +1\).

Solution

On this cell \(S_1 = 1\). Given \(X_1 = +1\), \(X_2 = +1\) has conditional probability \(p = 1/3\), giving \(S_2 = 2\), and \(X_2 = -1\) has probability \(2/3\), giving \(S_2 = 0\). The average is \(\tfrac13 \cdot 2 + \tfrac23 \cdot 0 = 2/3\). This equals \(S_1 + (2p - 1) = 1 - 1/3 = 2/3\), as Proposition 2.3 states, and it is not \(S_1 = 1\).

Proof

P1 · The cell average of the increment for general p

Prove the claim of Proposition 2.3 that the average of \(X_{k+1}\) over a level-\(k\) cell is \(2p - 1\), using only the factorization of path weights.

Solution

A path in the cell \(B\) with prefix \(h\) and step \(x_{k+1} = s\) has weight \(\prod_{i \le k} q(h_i) \cdot q(s) \cdot \prod_{i \ge k+2} q(x_i)\). Summing over the free steps \(x_{k+2}, \dots, x_N\) gives 1, since \(q(+1) + q(-1) = 1\) at each step. So the total weight of paths in \(B\) with \(X_{k+1} = s\) is \(\Prob(B)\,q(s)\). The average of \(X_{k+1}\) over \(B\) is therefore \(\sum_s s\,q(s) = p - (1-p) = 2p - 1\).

P2 · The compensated walk

Show that \(M_k = S_k - k(2p-1)\) is a martingale on the natural filtration for every \(p \in (0,1)\).

Solution

\(M_k\) is adapted, since \(S_k\) is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable and \(k(2p-1)\) is a constant. On a level-\(k\) cell \(B\) with prefix \(h\), the average of \(M_{k+1} = S_{k+1} - (k+1)(2p-1)\) is, by Proposition 2.3, \(S_k(h) + (2p-1) - (k+1)(2p-1) = S_k(h) - k(2p-1) = M_k(h)\). By Proposition 2.2, \(M\) is a martingale.

P3 · Constant expectation of a martingale

Let \((M_k)\) be a martingale on the natural filtration of the walk. Show that \(\E[M_k] = \E[M_0]\) for every \(k\).

Solution

The filtration \(\mathcal F_0\) is trivial (Proposition 2.1(a)), so \(\E[M_0 \mid \mathcal F_0] = \E[M_0]\). For each \(k\), \(\E[M_{k+1}] = \E\big[\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k]\big]\) by Proposition 2.4 of L07 with the trivial partition. Since \(M\) is a martingale, \(\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k] = M_k\), so \(\E[M_{k+1}] = \E[M_k]\). Induction on \(k\) gives \(\E[M_k] = \E[M_0]\).

Empirical

E1 · The fair walk's counts

From Part B of Lab 1, state how many cells pass the test for the fair walk, and how many paths are mismatched in total.

Solution

The lab prints cells_agreeing 1023 of 1023, and mismatched_paths_all_levels 0. The count 1023 is \(1 + 2 + \cdots + 512 = 2^{10} - 1\), the total number of cells at levels 0 to 9. The mismatched total is 0, so every path agrees with the test at every level.

E2 · The biased walk's final value

From Part B of Lab 1, check the expected final value for the biased walk against \(N(2p-1)\).

Solution

The lab prints expected_S_N \(-10/3\). With \(N = 10\) and \(p = 1/3\), \(N(2p-1) = 10 \cdot (-1/3) = -10/3\). The printed flag is True. The value is also \(N\) times the printed first-cell increment \(-1/3\).

E3 · Why 1024 mismatches at each level

The biased walk reports 1024 mismatched paths at every level. Explain why the count is 1024 at each level rather than the number of cells.

Solution

The lab counts mismatched paths, not cells. Each path is in one cell at each level. At each level, every cell fails, and every path in a failing cell is a mismatched path. Since all \(2^{10} = 1024\) paths lie in some cell at each level, each level has 1024 mismatched paths. The total over the ten levels 0 to 9 is 10240.

Research

R1 · The quadratic martingale

For the fair walk, show that \(S_k^2 - k\) is a martingale on the natural filtration.

Solution

On a level-\(k\) cell with prefix \(h\), \(S_{k+1}^2 = (S_k + X_{k+1})^2 = S_k^2 + 2S_kX_{k+1} + 1\), because \(X_{k+1}^2 = 1\). The cell average of \(X_{k+1}\) is 0 when \(p = 1/2\) (Proposition 2.3), and \(S_k\) is constant on the cell. So the average of \(S_{k+1}^2\) is \(S_k(h)^2 + 1\). Then the average of \(S_{k+1}^2 - (k+1)\) is \(S_k(h)^2 - k\), which is the value of \(S_k^2 - k\) on the cell. Proposition 2.2 gives the martingale property.

R2 · The second moment of the fair walk

Using R1 and Problem P3, compute \(\E[S_N^2]\) for the fair walk.

Solution

By R1, \(M_k = S_k^2 - k\) is a martingale. By P3, \(\E[M_N] = \E[M_0]\). Here \(M_0 = S_0^2 - 0 = 0\), so \(\E[S_N^2] - N = 0\), and \(\E[S_N^2] = N\).

R3 · The natural filtration of a constant process

Let \(C_k = 5\) for all \(k\). Find the natural filtration of \((C_k)\) and show that \((C_k)\) is a martingale on it.

Solution

The record of a constant process reveals nothing, so every \(\mathcal F_k\) is the trivial family \(\{\emptyset, \Omega\}\). Each \(C_k\) is constant, hence adapted. The conditional expectation of \(C_{k+1} = 5\) on the trivial family is the constant 5, which is \(C_k\). So the constant process is a martingale on its natural filtration.

    The conditional mean of the next value given the record equals the current value.

  4. Why is zero expected increment not enough for the martingale property?

    Options

    The example \(M_2 = 2X_1\) has zero unconditional increment mean and a conditional mean that differs from \(M_1\).

  5. For the biased walk with \(p = 1/3\), what is \(\E[S_2 \mid \mathcal F_1]\) on the cell \(X_1 = +1\), to four decimal places?

    \(S_1 + (2p-1) = 1 - 1/3 = 2/3 = 0.6667\) (Problem M3).

  6. For the biased walk with \(p = 1/3\) and \(N = 2\), what is \(\E[S_2]\), to four decimal places?

    \(\E[S_2] = 2(2p-1) = -2/3 = -0.6667\) (Problem M2).

  7. In Lab 1, how many cells does the fair walk pass, out of how many?

    Options

    Levels 0 to 9 have \(1 + 2 + \cdots + 512 = 1023\) cells, and Part B prints cells_agreeing 1023 of 1023 (Problem E1).

  8. For the walk with \(p \ne 1/2\), what is the average of \(X_{k+1}\) on any level-\(k\) cell?

    Options

    On each cell the average of the next step is \(q(+1) - q(-1) = 2p - 1\), by the factorization in the proof of Proposition 2.3.

  9. Which process is a martingale for every \(p\) on the walk's natural filtration?

    Options

    Subtracting the mean path \(k(2p-1)\) removes the drift on each cell.

  10. At time 0, what does the natural filtration of the walk contain?

    Options

    No step has been observed at time 0, so the only cell is \(\Omega\).
