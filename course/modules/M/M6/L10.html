Optional stopping and the doubling strategy

  • Uses: filtration, martingale, stopping time, expectation
  • Introduces: optional stopping theorem, doubling strategy
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

A fair game has zero expected gain at every step, and a martingale is its formal model. A player may try to beat the game by choosing when to quit. The doubling strategy of Definition 10.5 is the classic attempt. This lesson proves an optional stopping theorem for bounded stopping times (Theorem 10.3, §1), applies it to the doubling strategy with finite capital (§2), and shows that the no-capital version breaks it (§3). The conclusion depends on a boundedness hypothesis that the proof uses.

1The stopped martingale

Let \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\) be a probability space with a filtration \((\mathcal F_k)\), and let \(M=(M_k)_{k\ge 0}\) be a martingale: \(M\) is adapted, each \(M_k\) is integrable, and \(\E[M_{k+1}\mid\mathcal F_k]=M_k\). The identity is the one of L08, Definition 2.2. L08 states it over a finite horizon, and this lesson applies it for every \(k\ge 0\). Integrability is stated here as a hypothesis, because L08's finite sample space made every variable integrable without saying so. The martingale property gives \(\E[M_{k+1}\indic_A]=\E[M_k\indic_A]\) for every event \(A\in\mathcal F_k\). This follows from \(\E[M_{k+1}\mid\mathcal F_k]=M_k\) by the defining property of conditional expectation. L07 proves that property on a finite probability space, and on a general space it is Assumption CE of L07, stated there and not proved. Lemma 10.2, Theorem 10.3, the martingale claim of Lemma 10.6 and Proposition 10.9(c) are therefore stated assuming Assumption CE. Let \(\tau\) be a stopping time (L09).

Definition 10.1 (the stopped process and the value at a time)

Write \(\tau\wedge k=\min(\tau,k)\). The process \(M_{\tau\wedge k}\), for \(k\ge 0\), is the stopped process: it follows \(M\) until \(\tau\) and is then frozen at \(M_\tau\). The value at the stopping time is \(M_\tau(\omega)=M_{\tau(\omega)}(\omega)\) on the outcomes where \(\tau(\omega)<\infty\).

Lemma 10.2 (increments of the stopped martingale)
  1. \(M\) is a martingale on the filtration \((\mathcal F_k)\), and \(\tau\) is a stopping time for the same filtration (L09, Definition 9.1).
  2. Assumption CE holds (stated in §1, not proved here).

Assuming Assumption CE, for every whole number \(k\ge 0\), \(M_{\tau\wedge k}\) is integrable, and \(\E[M_{\tau\wedge(k+1)}]=\E[M_{\tau\wedge k}]\).

Proof

Integrability: \(\tau\wedge k\) takes values in \(\{0,\ldots,k\}\), so \(|M_{\tau\wedge k}|\le |M_0|+\cdots+|M_k|\), which is integrable. For the identity, compare the two stopped values. On \(\{\tau\le k\}\), \(\tau\wedge(k+1)=\tau\wedge k=\tau\), so the difference is zero. On \(\{\tau>k\}\), \(\tau\wedge(k+1)=k+1\) and \(\tau\wedge k=k\), so the difference is \(M_{k+1}-M_k\). Hence \[ M_{\tau\wedge(k+1)}-M_{\tau\wedge k}=\indic_{\{\tau>k\}}\,(M_{k+1}-M_k). \] By Lemma 9.7 of L09, \(\{\tau>k\}\in\mathcal F_k\). Taking expectations and using the martingale property with \(A=\{\tau>k\}\) gives \(\E[\indic_{\{\tau>k\}}(M_{k+1}-M_k)]=\E[M_{k+1}\indic_{\{\tau>k\}}]-\E[M_k\indic_{\{\tau>k\}}]=0\). So \(\E[M_{\tau\wedge(k+1)}]=\E[M_{\tau\wedge k}]\).

Theorem 10.3 (optional stopping, bounded case)
  1. \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\) is a probability space, and \((\mathcal F_k)_{k\ge 0}\) is a filtration on it.
  2. \(M=(M_k)_{k\ge 0}\) is a martingale on \((\mathcal F_k)\).
  3. \(\tau\) is a stopping time on \((\mathcal F_k)\), and there is a fixed whole number \(N\ge 0\) with \(\tau(\omega)\le N\) for every outcome \(\omega\).
  4. Assumption CE holds (stated in §1, not proved here).

Assuming Assumption CE, \(M_\tau\) is integrable and \(\E[M_\tau]=\E[M_0]\). This statement is the optional stopping theorem for bounded stopping times.

Proof

Since \(\tau\le N\) everywhere, \(\tau\wedge N=\tau\), so \(M_\tau=M_{\tau\wedge N}\). Integrability follows from Lemma 10.2 with \(k=N\). Write the telescoping sum \[ M_{\tau\wedge N}=M_0+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\bigl(M_{\tau\wedge(k+1)}-M_{\tau\wedge k}\bigr). \] Each summand has expectation zero, by the proof of Lemma 10.2. Taking expectations of the whole sum gives \(\E[M_\tau]=\E[M_{\tau\wedge N}]=\E[M_0]\).

Remark 10.4 (which hypothesis does what)

The martingale property enters each summand in the proof through \(\E[\indic_{\{\tau>k\}}(M_{k+1}-M_k)]=0\). The stopping-time property enters through \(\{\tau>k\}\in\mathcal F_k\), which lets the indicator be chosen before the next step. The bound \(\tau\le N\) enters once, to replace \(M_\tau\) by the finite sum \(M_{\tau\wedge N}\). Section 3 removes the bound and shows the conclusion can fail.

2The doubling strategy with finite capital

Definition 10.5 (the doubling strategy with capital K)

Let \(K\ge 1\) be an integer number of cents, the capital. Each bet is a fair coin: \(\varepsilon_k=+1\) (a win) or \(-1\) (a loss), independent over \(k\), each with probability \(1/2\). Bet \(k\) stakes \(2^{k-1}\) cents. The doubling strategy places bet \(k\) only if no earlier bet has won and the stake \(2^{k-1}\) does not exceed the wealth on hand. Its wealth after the bets placed is \(K\) plus the net gain \(D_\tau\), where \(D_k=\sum_{j\le k}2^{j-1}\varepsilon_j\) and \(\tau\) is the number of bets placed. Ruin means that the strategy stops without a win.

Lemma 10.6 (the number of bets)
  1. \(K\ge 1\) is an integer number of cents, and \(m\) is the whole number with \(2^m\le K+1<2^{m+1}\).
  2. The bets \(\varepsilon_k\) are independent fair coins, \(\mathcal F_k=\sigma(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_k)\), and \(D\) and \(\tau\) are as in Definition 10.5.

Let \(w\) be the index of the first win, with \(w=\infty\) if no bet wins. Then the number of bets placed is \(\tau=\min(w,m)\). Moreover \(\tau\) is a stopping time and \(\tau\le m\). Assuming Assumption CE, \(D\) is a martingale.

Proof

Suppose bets \(1,\ldots,k-1\) have all lost. Then the wealth on hand before bet \(k\) is \(K-(2^{k-1}-1)=K+1-2^{k-1}\), since the losses sum to \(2^{k-1}-1\). Bet \(k\) is affordable exactly when \(2^{k-1}\le K+1-2^{k-1}\), that is \(2^k\le K+1\), which holds exactly when \(k\le m\). So bets \(1,\ldots,m\) are placed while no win has occurred, and bet \(m+1\) is not placed after \(m\) losses. If \(w\le m\), bets \(1,\ldots,w\) are placed and the count is \(w=\min(w,m)\). If \(w>m\), bets \(1,\ldots,m\) are placed and all lose, and the count is \(m=\min(w,m)\).

The process \(D\) is a martingale: \(D_{k+1}-D_k=2^k\varepsilon_{k+1}\), and for \(A\in\mathcal F_k=\sigma(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_k)\), independence gives \(\E[\indic_A\,2^k\varepsilon_{k+1}]=2^k\Prob(A)\E[\varepsilon_{k+1}]=0\). For \(\tau\): \(\{\tau\le k\}\) is the event that a win occurs among the first \(k\) bets when \(k<m\), and it is the whole space when \(k\ge m\). Either way it depends only on \(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_k\), so it lies in \(\mathcal F_k\). Finally \(\tau\le m\) by the first formula.

Proposition 10.7 (doubling with finite capital)
  1. \(K\ge 1\) is an integer number of cents, and \(m\) is the whole number with \(2^m\le K+1<2^{m+1}\).
  2. The bets, the net gain \(D\) and the stopping time \(\tau\) are as in Lemma 10.6, with capital \(K\).

The probability of ruin, meaning no win by bet \(m\), is \(2^{-m}\). The wealth at the stopping time is \(K+1\) after a win and \(K+1-2^m\) after ruin. The expected final wealth is \(\E[K+D_\tau]=K\).

Proof

No win by bet \(m\) means \(\varepsilon_1=\cdots=\varepsilon_m=-1\), an event of probability \(2^{-m}\). If bet \(w\le m\) wins, the losses before it sum to \(2^{w-1}-1\) and the win pays \(2^{w-1}\), so \(D_\tau=-(2^{w-1}-1)+2^{w-1}=1\), and the wealth is \(K+1\). After ruin, \(D_\tau=-(2^m-1)\), and the wealth is \(K+1-2^m\). The expectation is \[ (1-2^{-m})(K+1)+2^{-m}(K+1-2^m)=K+1-2^{-m}\cdot 2^m=K. \] This agrees with Theorem 10.3: \(D\) is a martingale with \(D_0=0\), and \(\tau\le m\) by Lemma 10.6, so \(\E[D_\tau]=0\) and \(\E[K+D_\tau]=K\).

3The doubling strategy without a capital limit

Example 10.8 (no capital, the first win)

Let the bets continue with no capital limit, so \(\tau=w\), the index of the first win. Then \(\{\tau>n\}\) is the event that the first \(n\) bets all lose, so \(\Prob(\tau>n)=2^{-n}\). The stopped net gain is \(D_{\tau\wedge n}\). On \(\{\tau\le n\}\) it equals \(+1\). On \(\{\tau>n\}\) it equals \(-(2^n-1)\).

Proposition 10.9 (the unbounded doubling strategy)
  1. The doubling strategy of Example 10.8 with no capital limit, so the bets \(\varepsilon_k\), the net gain \(D\) and the filtration \(\mathcal F_k=\sigma(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_k)\) are as in Lemma 10.6.
  2. \(\tau=w\) is the index of the first win, with \(w=\infty\) if no bet wins.

For the doubling strategy of Example 10.8: (a) \(\Prob(\tau=\infty)=0\), so \(\tau<\infty\) with probability one, and \(D_\tau=+1\) on \(\{\tau<\infty\}\), so \(D_\tau=1\) almost surely; (b) \(\E[D_\tau]=1\) while \(D_0=0\); (c) assuming Assumption CE, \(\E[D_{\tau\wedge n}]=0\) for every whole number \(n\ge 0\).

Proof

(a) The event \(\{\tau=\infty\}\) lies inside \(\{\tau>n\}\) for every \(n\), so \(\Prob(\tau=\infty)\le 2^{-n}\) for every \(n\). Letting \(n\to\infty\) gives \(\Prob(\tau=\infty)=0\). On \(\{\tau<\infty\}\) the first win is at \(w=\tau\), and the computation in Proposition 10.7 gives \(D_\tau=1\). So \(D_\tau=1\) except on an event of probability zero.

(b) By (a), \(\E[D_\tau]=\E[1]=1\), since \(D_\tau\) equals \(1\) almost surely, and expectation does not see a null set. Also \(D_0=0\).

(c) For each \(n\), \(D_{\tau\wedge n}\) is a martingale stopped at a bounded time, and Lemma 10.2 gives \(\E[D_{\tau\wedge n}]=\E[D_0]=0\).

Proposition 10.9(c), assuming Assumption CE, shows that the identity of Theorem 10.3 holds for each bounded time \(\tau\wedge n\), and Proposition 10.9(b) shows that it fails for \(\tau\). The hypothesis that fails is boundedness: \(\Prob(\tau>N)=2^{-N}>0\) for every \(N\), so no fixed \(N\) bounds \(\tau\) almost surely.

Lab 1 · The doubling strategy with finite capital and without it

Part A runs the finite-capital strategy on every coin sequence of the length it needs, for capitals \(K=1,6,10,100,1000\) cents, each sequence weighted by its exact probability. It predicts \(m=\lfloor\log_2(K+1)\rfloor\) bets, a ruin probability of \(2^{-m}\), a final wealth of \(K+1\) after a win and \(K+1-2^m\) after ruin, and an expected final wealth equal to \(K\). Part B removes the capital limit and enumerates all \(2^n\) coin sequences for \(n=1,2,3,5,10\). It predicts that the stopped net gain has expectation 0 for every \(n\), and the lab prints this expectation for each \(n\in\{1,2,3,5,10\}\). It also predicts that \(\Prob(\tau>n)=2^{-n}\), and that the wins contribute \(1-2^{-n}\) to that expectation.

"""M6 L10 lab 1: optional stopping and the doubling strategy.

A player holds K cents (an integer, K >= 1) and bets on a fair coin. Bet number k stakes 2**(k-1) cents. A win
pays the stake and a loss takes it. The strategy stops at the first win. It also stops, with no win, when the
next stake is larger than the wealth on hand: this is ruin. The wealth left is K+1 minus 2 to the power of the
number of bets, which is 0 when K+1 is a power of two (the K=1 row prints 0).

Part A, finite capital K: the stopping time tau is bounded. The lab runs the strategy on every coin sequence of
length L = bit_length(K+1) + 1, which is enough for every run to stop. Each sequence has weight 2**(-L). It prints
the number of bets the capital allows, the win and ruin probabilities, the final wealth on each branch, and the
expected final wealth. All wealths are integers in cents, and the probabilities and expectations are Fractions.

Part B, no capital limit: the same bets with no limit, stopped at the first win or after n bets, whichever comes
first. For each n in the list the lab enumerates all 2**n coin sequences, takes the net gain in cents at the
stopping time min(tau, n), and prints the expectation of that stopped gain, the expectation of its win part, and
the probability that no win occurs by n. The stopped expectation is 0 for each n in HORIZONS.

Everything is an exact enumeration on a finite space. No random draws are used, so every run prints the same lines.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

CAPITALS = (1, 6, 10, 100, 1000)
HORIZONS = (1, 2, 3, 5, 10)


def run_capital(capital: int, flips: tuple[int, ...]) -> tuple[int, str]:
    """Run the finite-capital strategy on one coin sequence (1 = win, 0 = loss): final wealth in cents, outcome."""
    wealth = capital
    for k, flip in enumerate(flips, start=1):
        stake: int = 2 ** (k - 1)
        if stake > wealth:
            return wealth, "ruin"
        if flip == 1:
            return wealth + stake, "win"
        wealth -= stake
    raise RuntimeError("the coin sequence was too short for the strategy to stop")


def run_unlimited(horizon: int, flips: tuple[int, ...]) -> int:
    """The net gain in cents of the doubling bets stopped at the first win or after horizon bets."""
    net = 0
    for k, flip in enumerate(flips[:horizon], start=1):
        stake: int = 2 ** (k - 1)
        if flip == 1:
            return net + stake
        net -= stake
    return net


def part_a() -> None:
    """Exact outcome distribution of the finite-capital strategy for each capital in CAPITALS."""
    print("Part A: doubling with finite capital K (cents)")
    for capital in CAPITALS:
        bets = (capital + 1).bit_length() - 1
        length = (capital + 1).bit_length() + 1
        total = 2**length
        wins: list[int] = []
        ruins: list[int] = []
        expected = Fraction(0)
        for flips in _sequences(length):
            wealth, outcome = run_capital(capital, flips)
            expected += Fraction(wealth, total)
            (wins if outcome == "win" else ruins).append(wealth)
        win_probability = Fraction(len(wins), total)
        ruin_probability = Fraction(len(ruins), total)
        print(
            f"capital {capital} bets {bets} win_probability {float(win_probability):.10f} "
            f"ruin_probability {float(ruin_probability):.10f} "
            f"ruin_is_one_over_2_to_bets {ruin_probability == Fraction(1, 2**bets)} "
            f"wealth_on_win {sorted(set(wins))} wealth_on_ruin {sorted(set(ruins))} "
            f"expected_final_wealth {expected} equals_start {expected == capital} "
            f"probabilities_sum_to_one {win_probability + ruin_probability == 1}"
        )


def part_b() -> None:
    """Exact stopped values for the strategy with no capital limit, stopped at min(tau, n)."""
    print("Part B: doubling with no capital limit, stopped at min(tau, n)")
    for horizon in HORIZONS:
        total = 2**horizon
        expected_stopped = Fraction(0)
        expected_win_part = Fraction(0)
        lose_all = 0
        for flips in _sequences(horizon):
            value = run_unlimited(horizon, flips)
            expected_stopped += Fraction(value, total)
            if value > 0:
                expected_win_part += Fraction(value, total)
            if value < 0:
                lose_all += 1
        probability_beyond = Fraction(lose_all, total)
        all_lose = run_unlimited(horizon, (0,) * horizon)
        print(
            f"n {horizon} P_tau_greater_than_n {float(probability_beyond):.10f} "
            f"expected_stopped_value {expected_stopped} "
            f"expected_win_part {expected_win_part} "
            f"wealth_when_all_lose {all_lose} "
            f"win_part_is_one_minus_2_to_minus_n {expected_win_part == 1 - Fraction(1, 2**horizon)}"
        )


def _sequences(length: int) -> list[tuple[int, ...]]:
    """Every coin sequence of the given length, as tuples of 0 (loss) and 1 (win)."""
    return [tuple((index >> shift) & 1 for shift in range(length)) for index in range(2**length)]


def main() -> None:
    """Print Part A and Part B."""
    part_a()
    part_b()


main()

Every Part A line matches Proposition 10.7. For \(K=1000\), the lab prints 9 bets, a win probability of 0.9980468750 and a ruin probability of 0.0019531250, which is \(1/512=2^{-9}\). The wealth after a win is 1001 and after ruin it is 489, which is \(1001-512\). The expected final wealth is exactly 1000 = \(K\), and the same holds for every capital in the list. A high win probability does not mean a gain: the ruin branch removes just enough to bring the mean back to the start. Part B prints an expected stopped value of 0 for each \(n\), as Proposition 10.9(c) predicts. Its win part is \(1-2^{-n}\), so the loss part is \(-(1-2^{-n})\) and the two cancel. As \(n\) grows, the stopped value is 1 on the win branch and \(-(2^n-1)\) on the all-loss branch, whose probability is \(2^{-n}\). The lab checks finitely many \(n\) by enumeration, so it does not prove the limit statement; Proposition 10.9(a) does.

4Where the canon breaks

  • Unbounded time. Theorem 10.3 needs \(\tau\le N\). Without it, Proposition 10.9 gives \(\E[D_\tau]=1\neq 0=\E[D_0]\). The hypothesis is not a technicality: the example in §3 is the reason it is stated.
  • Finite almost surely is not bounded. Proposition 10.9(a) shows \(\tau<\infty\) almost surely, and the conclusion still fails. Almost sure finiteness is therefore not the hypothesis that matters here.
  • Ruin is not zero wealth. In Proposition 10.7 the ruin branch keeps \(K+1-2^m\) cents, which is 0 exactly when \(K+1\) is a power of two (\(K=1, 3, 7, 15,\ldots\)). A low ruin probability with leftover wealth far below \(K\) can still have mean \(K\). The lab's ruin wealths are 0, 3, 3, 37 and 489 for the five capitals.
  • Integer cents. Stakes are integers and the wealths are integers, so the expectation is exact. No approximation enters the proof or the lab.

5Misconceptions

  1. “Doubling wins one cent almost surely, so the strategy makes money.”

    With capital \(K\), the strategy wins with probability \(1-2^{-m}\), which is 0.9980468750 at \(K=1000\). But the ruin branch leaves 489 cents, not 1000, and the expected final wealth is exactly \(K\) (Lab 1, Part A, and Proposition 10.7). A high win rate with a rare large loss has mean equal to the start.

  2. “Optional stopping holds for every stopping time of a martingale.”

    Theorem 10.3 needs \(\tau\le N\) for a fixed \(N\). Example 10.8 gives a martingale \(D\) and a stopping time \(\tau\) for which \(\E[D_\tau]=1\) while \(\E[D_0]=0\) (Proposition 10.9). The stopping rule is a legitimate stopping time, and the identity fails because the time is unbounded.

  3. “A stopping time that is finite with probability one always satisfies \(\E[M_\tau]=\E[M_0]\).”

    Proposition 10.9(a) gives \(\Prob(\tau<\infty)=1\), and the value \(D_\tau=1\) almost surely. Yet \(\E[D_\tau]=1\), not \(0\). Finiteness with probability one is not enough. On the rare branch \(\{\tau>n\}\) the loss is \(2^n-1\) cents, and that loss times its probability \(2^{-n}\) is \(1-2^{-n}\), which does not vanish as \(n\) grows.

Mechanics

M1 · Doubling with capital 6

Take \(K=6\) cents. Find \(m\), the ruin probability, the wealth after a win, the wealth after ruin, and the expected final wealth.

Solution

Since \(2^2=4\le 7<8=2^3\), \(m=2\). Ruin has probability \(2^{-2}=1/4\), so the win probability is \(3/4\). After a win the wealth is \(K+1=7\) cents. After ruin it is \(K+1-2^2=3\) cents. The expectation is \((3/4)\cdot 7+(1/4)\cdot 3=24/4=6=K\). Lab 1, Part A, prints for capital 6: win probability 0.75, ruin probability 0.25, wealth 7 after a win, 3 after ruin, and expected final wealth 6.

M2 · Doubling with capital 10

Take \(K=10\) cents. Find \(m\), the bet number of the largest stake, and the wealth after ruin.

Solution

Since \(2^3=8\le 11<16=2^4\), \(m=3\). Bets 1, 2 and 3 are placed while they lose, with stakes 1, 2 and 4 cents. The largest stake is 4 cents, on bet 3, and bet 4 would stake 8 cents, which exceeds the wealth on hand, \(10-7=3\) cents. After ruin the wealth is \(K+1-2^3=3\) cents. Lab 1 prints 3 for capital 10 on the ruin branch.

M3 · The affordability threshold

Show that bet \(k\) is affordable after \(k-1\) losses exactly when \(2^k\le K+1\), and apply this to \(K=100\) cents to find the last bet placed.

Solution

After \(k-1\) losses the wealth is \(K+1-2^{k-1}\), as in the proof of Lemma 10.6. Bet \(k\) stakes \(2^{k-1}\), so it is affordable when \(2^{k-1}\le K+1-2^{k-1}\), which is \(2^k\le K+1\). For \(K=100\), \(2^6=64\le 101<128\), so bets 1 to 6 are affordable and bet 7 is not. The last bet placed is bet 6, which stakes 32 cents.

Proof

P1 · The stopped increment

Prove the identity \(M_{\tau\wedge(k+1)}-M_{\tau\wedge k}=\indic_{\{\tau>k\}}(M_{k+1}-M_k)\) for every stopping time \(\tau\), including \(\tau=\infty\).

Solution

Consider the three cases. If \(\tau\le k\), then \(\tau\wedge k=\tau\) and \(\tau\wedge(k+1)=\tau\), so the left side is 0, and the indicator is 0. If \(\tau\ge k+1\), then \(\tau\wedge k=k\) and \(\tau\wedge(k+1)=k+1\), so the left side is \(M_{k+1}-M_k\), and the indicator is 1. The case \(\tau=\infty\) falls in the second case, since \(\infty\ge k+1\). These cases cover every outcome.

P2 · Optional stopping with the bound written out

In Theorem 10.3, write out the telescoping sum for \(N=2\), and verify that each summand has expectation zero using the martingale property at each step.

Solution

For \(N=2\), \(M_{\tau}=M_{\tau\wedge 2}=M_0+(M_{\tau\wedge 1}-M_{\tau\wedge 0})+(M_{\tau\wedge 2}-M_{\tau\wedge 1})\). The first summand is \(\indic_{\{\tau>0\}}(M_1-M_0)\), and \(\{\tau>0\}\in\mathcal F_0\). Its expectation is \(\E[M_1\indic_{\{\tau>0\}}]-\E[M_0\indic_{\{\tau>0\}}]=0\) by the martingale property at \(k=0\). The second is \(\indic_{\{\tau>1\}}(M_2-M_1)\), with \(\{\tau>1\}\in\mathcal F_1\), and its expectation is zero by the property at \(k=1\). Taking expectations gives \(\E[M_\tau]=\E[M_0]\).

P3 · The doubling outcome on a win

Show that if bet \(w\le m\) is the first win, the net gain \(D_w\) equals 1, and that the wealth equals \(K+1\).

Solution

Bets \(1,\ldots,w-1\) lose, with total stake \(\sum_{j<w}2^{j-1}=2^{w-1}-1\), so the net from them is \(-(2^{w-1}-1)\). Bet \(w\) wins and pays its stake \(2^{w-1}\). The net gain is \(-(2^{w-1}-1)+2^{w-1}=1\). The wealth is \(K+1\), since \(K\) plus the net gain is \(K+1\).

Research

R1 · The martingale hypothesis cannot be dropped

Let \(X_k=k\) for \(k\ge 0\), a deterministic process that is not a martingale, and let \(\tau\equiv 1\). Show that \(\E[X_\tau]\neq\E[X_0]\), so Theorem 10.3 fails without the martingale hypothesis.

Solution

Here \(X_\tau=X_1=1\), so \(\E[X_\tau]=1\). Also \(X_0=0\), so \(\E[X_0]=0\). The time \(\tau\equiv 1\) is bounded and a stopping time, so the only hypothesis that fails is the martingale property: \(\E[X_{k+1}\mid\mathcal F_k]=k+1\neq k=X_k\).

R2 · The truncated expectation by direct computation

Without using Theorem 10.3, show that \(\E[D_{\tau\wedge n}]=0\) for each \(n\) in Example 10.8, using \(\Prob(\tau\le n)=1-2^{-n}\).

Solution

On \(\{\tau\le n\}\), \(D_{\tau\wedge n}=1\), and \(\Prob(\tau\le n)=1-2^{-n}\). On \(\{\tau>n\}\), \(D_{\tau\wedge n}=D_n=-(2^n-1)\), with probability \(2^{-n}\). So \(\E[D_{\tau\wedge n}]=(1-2^{-n})\cdot 1+2^{-n}\cdot(-(2^n-1))=1-2^{-n}-1+2^{-n}=0\).

R3 · Where the limit of the expectations fails

Using Proposition 10.9, explain why \(\lim_n\E[D_{\tau\wedge n}]\) and \(\E[\lim_n D_{\tau\wedge n}]\) differ, and identify the part of the sum that carries the difference.

Solution

The first limit is \(\lim_n 0=0\). For almost every outcome, \(D_{\tau\wedge n}\) equals \(1\) once \(n\ge \tau(\omega)\), so \(\lim_n D_{\tau\wedge n}=1\) almost surely and its expectation is 1. The difference is carried by the all-loss branch \(\{\tau>n\}\): its probability \(2^{-n}\) times the loss \(2^n-1\) equals \(1-2^{-n}\). That product tends to 1, so the losses on that branch do not vanish in expectation, and for each \(n\) they cancel the win part exactly. The limit of the expectations is therefore 0, and the expectation of the limit is 1.
