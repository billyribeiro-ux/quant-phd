Pseudo-Random Numbers and Monte Carlo Integration

Derivatives pricing frequently boils down to computing high-dimensional expectations under risk-neutral probability measures. While low-dimensional integrals yield to deterministic quadrature, multi-factor models and path-dependent derivatives quickly suffer the curse of dimensionality. Monte Carlo integration sidesteps this barrier: by evaluating sample averages along simulated pseudo-random paths, its convergence rate remains strictly \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\) regardless of dimension. However, computers cannot generate true mathematical randomness. They rely on deterministic pseudo-random number generators (PRNGs). Understanding PRNG state transitions, period lengths, seed determinism, and Monte Carlo sampling error is essential for designing reproducible, auditable, and statistically rigorous valuation engines.

1Pseudo-random number generation and determinism

Digital computers operate deterministically; they cannot produce truly random physical phenomena without dedicated hardware noise sources. Scientific simulation relies instead on algorithmic sequences that mimic the statistical properties of independent uniform draws.

Definition 1.1 (pseudo-random number generator)

A pseudo-random number generator (PRNG) is a deterministic state machine defined by a 4-tuple \((\mathcal{S}, s_0, f, g)\):

  1. A finite state space \(\mathcal{S}\).
  2. An initial state \(s_0 \in \mathcal{S}\), called the seed.
  3. A state transition function \(f: \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{S}\), generating states \(s_{k+1} = f(s_k)\).
  4. An output mapping \(g: \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{U}\), producing pseudo-random variates \(u_k = g(s_k)\), typically in \([0, 1)\).
Proposition 1.2 (periodicity of finite-state PRNGs)

Let \((\mathcal{S}, s_0, f, g)\) be a PRNG with finite state space \(|\mathcal{S}| < \infty\). The sequence of states \(\{s_k\}_{k \ge 0}\) is eventually periodic: there exist integers \(\mu \ge 0\) (pre-period) and \(p \ge 1\) (period) such that \(s_{k+p} = s_k\) for all \(k \ge \mu\). Moreover, the period satisfies \(p \le |\mathcal{S}|\).

Proof

Consider the first \(|\mathcal{S}| + 1\) states: \(s_0, s_1, \dots, s_{|\mathcal{S}|}\). By the Pigeonhole Principle, since \(|\mathcal{S}|\) is finite, at least two states in this sequence must be identical: \(s_i = s_j\) for some \(0 \le i < j \le |\mathcal{S}|\). Let \(\mu = i\) and \(p = j - i \ge 1\). Because \(f\) is a deterministic function, \(s_{k+1} = f(s_k)\) implies that \(s_{i+1} = s_{j+1}\), and by induction, \(s_{k+p} = s_k\) for all \(k \ge \mu\). Furthermore, \(p = j - i \le |\mathcal{S}| - 0 = |\mathcal{S}|\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 1.3 (linear congruential generator)

A classic linear congruential generator (LCG) updates an integer state modulo \(m\):

\[ s_{k+1} = (a s_k + c) \pmod m, \quad u_k = \frac{s_k}{m} \]

By the Hull–Dobell Theorem, an LCG achieves maximal period \(p = m\) if and only if: (i) \(\gcd(c, m) = 1\); (ii) every prime factor of \(m\) divides \(a - 1\); and (iii) if 4 divides \(m\), then 4 divides \(a - 1\). Modern production PRNGs, such as the Mersenne Twister (MT19937) used in Python's random module, operate on 624 32-bit words, achieving an enormous period of \(2^{19937} - 1 \approx 4.3 \times 10^{6001}\) (Glasserman 2003, §2.1).

2Monte Carlo integration and the sample mean

Monte Carlo methods recast numerical integration as the evaluation of an expectation under a known probability distribution.

Definition 2.1 (Monte Carlo integration)

Monte Carlo integration approximates a definite integral or mathematical expectation \(\theta = \E[g(X)] = \int_{\mathcal{X}} g(x) f_X(x) dx\) by the sample mean of \(n\) independent, identically distributed evaluations:

\[ \hat{\theta}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i), \quad X_i \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} f_X \]

Theorem 2.2 (unbiasedness and variance of the Monte Carlo estimator)

Assume \(g(X)\) has finite expectation \(\E[g(X)] = \theta\) and finite variance \(\Var(g(X)) = \sigma^2 < \infty\). Let \(X_1, \dots, X_n\) be independent draws from \(f_X\). Then:

  1. \(\hat{\theta}_n\) is strictly unbiased: \(\E[\hat{\theta}_n] = \theta\).
  2. The variance of the estimator scales inversely with sample size: \[ \Var(\hat{\theta}_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} \]
  3. By the Strong Law of Large Numbers (M4), \(\hat{\theta}_n\) converges almost surely to \(\theta\): \[ \mathbb{P}\left(\lim_{n \to \infty} \hat{\theta}_n = \theta\right) = 1 \]
Proof

By linearity of expectation: \(\E[\hat{\theta}_n] = \E\left[\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i)\right] = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \E[g(X_i)] = \frac{1}{n} (n \theta) = \theta\). Because the draws \(X_i\) are mutually independent, the variance of the sum equals the sum of the variances: \(\Var(\hat{\theta}_n) = \Var\left(\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n g(X_i)\right) = \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^n \Var(g(X_i)) = \frac{1}{n^2} (n \sigma^2) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\). The almost sure convergence is the Kolmogorov Strong Law of Large Numbers applied to the i.i.d. sequence \(Y_i = g(X_i)\) with \(\E[|Y_i|] < \infty\). \(\blacksquare\)

3Standard error and Central Limit Theorem bounds

Because the exact variance \(\sigma^2\) is generally unknown, practitioners estimate it directly from the simulation sample.

Definition 3.1 (sample variance and standard error)

The unbiased sample variance \(s_n^2\) of \(n\) evaluations \(Y_i = g(X_i)\) is:

\[ s_n^2 = \frac{1}{n - 1} \sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \hat{\theta}_n)^2 \]

The estimated standard error \(\se(\hat{\theta}_n)\) of the estimator is:

\[ \se(\hat{\theta}_n) = \frac{s_n}{\sqrt{n}} \]

Theorem 3.2 (asymptotic normality and confidence intervals)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 2.2 with \(0 < \sigma^2 < \infty\), the Lindeberg–Lévy Central Limit Theorem (M4) implies:

\[ \frac{\hat{\theta}_n - \theta}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \]

Moreover, by Slutsky's theorem and the consistency of \(s_n \to \sigma\), the Studentized ratio converges to the standard normal: \(\frac{\hat{\theta}_n - \theta}{s_n / \sqrt{n}} \overset{d}{\longrightarrow} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\). An asymptotic \(100(1 - \alpha)\% \) confidence interval for \(\theta\) is:

\[ \left[ \hat{\theta}_n - z_{1 - \alpha/2} \frac{s_n}{\sqrt{n}}, \; \hat{\theta}_n + z_{1 - \alpha/2} \frac{s_n}{\sqrt{n}} \right] \]

where \(z_{1 - \alpha/2} = \Phi^{-1}(1 - \alpha/2)\). For a 95% confidence interval, \(z_{0.975} \approx 1.95996\).

4Dimensional independence versus quadrature

The decisive computational advantage of Monte Carlo over deterministic quadrature lies in how error bounds scale with problem dimension \(d\).

Proposition 4.1 (dimensional rate comparison)

Let \(I = \int_{[0, 1]^d} f(x) dx\) be a \(d\)-dimensional integral. Comparing composite quadrature with Monte Carlo using a total computational budget of \(N\) function evaluations:

  1. A composite Newton-Cotes rule of order \(r\) requires \(m\) points per dimension, giving total budget \(N = m^d\). The deterministic error scales as: \[ \text{Error}_{\text{quad}} = O(m^{-r}) = O(N^{-r/d}) \]
  2. The Monte Carlo estimator with \(N\) draws satisfies: \[ \text{Error}_{\text{MC}} = O(N^{-1/2}) \]

For any fixed rule order \(r\), whenever the dimension satisfies \(d > 2r\), Monte Carlo converges asymptotically faster than product quadrature.

Example 4.2 (curse of dimensionality in option baskets)

Consider pricing an option on a basket of \(d = 10\) assets using Simpson's rule (\(r = 4\)). The quadrature error scales as \(O(N^{-4/10}) = O(N^{-0.4})\), which is slower than Monte Carlo's \(O(N^{-0.5})\). For a path-dependent option with \(d = 100\) time steps, Simpson's rule error scales as \(O(N^{-4/100}) = O(N^{-0.04})\), requiring \(N = 10^{25}\) evaluations just to reduce error by a factor of 10. Monte Carlo maintains \(O(N^{-0.5})\) regardless of whether \(d = 1\) or \(d = 1000\).

5Inline laboratory: PRNG determinism and Monte Carlo rate

Lab 1 · PRNG determinism and Monte Carlo integration error scaling (SIMULATED)

Under Python's standard random.Random(seed) generator, two independent PRNG instances initialized with seed 42 will output bit-for-bit identical floating-point sequences. For the test integrand \(g(U) = e^U\) with \(U \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)\), the theoretical variance is \(\Var(e^U) = \frac{e^2 - 1}{2} - (e - 1)^2 \approx 0.2420356075\) (standard deviation \(\sigma \approx 0.4919711449\)). As sample size increases from \(n = 10^3\) to \(n = 10^6\), the standard error \(\se = s_n / \sqrt{n}\) decreases strictly by a factor of 10 for each 100-fold increase in \(n\), and the scaled absolute error \(|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta| \sqrt{n}\) remains bounded \(O(1)\).

"""Lab M8-L06-1: PRNG determinism and Monte Carlo integration error scaling.

Standard library only. Demonstrates pseudo-random determinism from seeds,
sample variance, standard error, and the O(1/sqrt(n)) Monte Carlo rate (SIMULATED).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    print("=== Part A: PRNG Determinism from Seed ===")
    rng1 = random.Random(42)
    rng2 = random.Random(42)
    draws1 = [rng1.random() for _ in range(5)]
    draws2 = [rng2.random() for _ in range(5)]
    print("PRNG 1 first 5 draws:", [f"{x:.8f}" for x in draws1])
    print("PRNG 2 first 5 draws:", [f"{x:.8f}" for x in draws2])
    print("Sequences match bit-for-bit:", draws1 == draws2)

    print("\n=== Part B: Monte Carlo Integration of int_0^1 exp(x) dx (SIMULATED) ===")
    # Exact integral theta = e - 1
    theta = math.e - 1.0
    # Exact variance: Var(e^U) = E[e^(2U)] - (E[e^U])^2 = (e^2 - 1)/2 - (e - 1)^2
    true_var = (math.exp(2.0) - 1.0) / 2.0 - theta**2
    true_sd = math.sqrt(true_var)

    print(f"Exact integral theta = e - 1: {theta:.16f}")
    print(f"Exact integrand variance:     {true_var:.10f} (SD: {true_sd:.10f})")
    print("    n       Estimate theta_n    Std Error (se)    Abs Error      Error * sqrt(n)")

    rng = random.Random(2026)
    for exponent in [3, 4, 5, 6]:
        n = 10**exponent
        # Draw n uniform variates in [0, 1]
        sample = [math.exp(rng.random()) for _ in range(n)]
        theta_hat = sum(sample) / n
        s_sq = sum((x - theta_hat) ** 2 for x in sample) / (n - 1)
        s = math.sqrt(s_sq)
        se = s / math.sqrt(n)
        abs_err = abs(theta_hat - theta)
        scaled_err = abs_err * math.sqrt(n)

        print(f"{n:8d}   {theta_hat:.10f}      {se:.4e}        {abs_err:.4e}     {scaled_err:.4f}")


main()

6Where the canon breaks

  • Period exhaustion and state-space collapse. Because PRNG state spaces are finite, any pseudo-random sequence must eventually repeat. For an old 32-bit LCG with \(m = 2^{31} - 1 \approx 2.1 \times 10^9\), modern GPUs can exhaust the entire period in less than one second. Once period exhaustion occurs, subsequent samples are completely dependent, destroying variance estimation and violating the CLT. Furthermore, Marsaglia (1968) proved that \(k\)-tuples of consecutive LCG draws fall on a relatively small number of hyperplanes in \([0, 1]^k\), introducing severe systematic bias in high-dimensional financial simulations.
  • Summation roundoff accumulation. Evaluating the Monte Carlo sample mean \(\hat{\theta}_n = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\) requires accumulating \(n\) floating-point additions. Under IEEE 754 binary64 arithmetic, naive left-to-right summation of \(n = 10^8\) positive terms incurs a worst-case accumulated roundoff error bounded by \(n u = 10^8 \times 2^{-53} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{-8}\) times the sum. If the individual payoffs are small relative to the running accumulator, subsequent terms suffer absorption, biasing the sample mean downward unless compensated summation (Kahan) or pairwise summation is used.

7Misconceptions

  1. “To gain three additional decimal digits of precision in a Monte Carlo option price, we only need to increase the simulation sample size by 1000.”

    The Monte Carlo standard error shrinks as \(O(1/\sqrt{n})\). To reduce error by a factor of \(10^k\) (gaining \(k\) decimal digits of accuracy), the required sample size increases by a factor of \((10^k)^2 = 10^{2k}\). Gaining three decimal digits requires a \(10^6\) (one-million-fold) increase in computational evaluations. Monte Carlo is inherently slow to achieve high precision; variance reduction techniques (Lesson 7 and Lesson 8) are mandatory in production.

  2. “Because pseudo-random numbers pass statistical randomness tests, successive runs of a simulation will naturally produce different results.”

    A PRNG is a purely deterministic mathematical algorithm. Given identical initial seeds, any two runs on identical hardware will generate bit-for-bit identical floating-point numbers. Reproducibility is a crucial regulatory requirement for quantitative risk models, model validation, and automated grading harnesses.

  3. “High-order Gaussian quadrature is always superior to Monte Carlo because quadrature converges exponentially or with high polynomial order.”

    Quadrature is superior only in low dimensions (\(d \le 3\)). For a \(d\)-dimensional integral, a product quadrature grid requires \(N = m^d\) points, degrading the convergence rate to \(O(N^{-r/d})\). For a multi-asset basket option (\(d = 20\)), even Simpson's rule (\(r = 4\)) converges at \(O(N^{-0.2})\), far inferior to Monte Carlo's dimension-independent \(O(N^{-0.5})\).

8Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · LCG state update

Consider an LCG with modulus \(m = 16\), multiplier \(a = 5\), and increment \(c = 1\). If the seed is \(s_0 = 3\), compute the next three states \(s_1, s_2, s_3\) and their corresponding uniform variates \(u_1, u_2, u_3 \in [0, 1)\).

Solution

State updates follow \(s_{k+1} = (5 s_k + 1) \pmod{16}\). First: \(s_1 = (5 \times 3 + 1) \pmod{16} = 16 \pmod{16} = 0\), so \(u_1 = 0/16 = 0.0\). Second: \(s_2 = (5 \times 0 + 1) \pmod{16} = 1 \pmod{16} = 1\), so \(u_2 = 1/16 = 0.0625\). Third: \(s_3 = (5 \times 1 + 1) \pmod{16} = 6 \pmod{16} = 6\), so \(u_3 = 6/16 = 0.375\). The states are \(0, 1, 6\) and variates are \(0.0, 0.0625, 0.375\).

M2 · Standard error scaling

A Monte Carlo simulation with \(n = 10,000\) paths yields a sample standard deviation \(s = 20.0\) for an option payoff. What is the estimated standard error \(\se\)? How many paths \(n_{\text{target}}\) are needed to achieve a target standard error \(\se_{\text{target}} = 0.04\)?

Solution

The initial standard error is \(\se = s / \sqrt{n} = 20.0 / \sqrt{10,000} = 20.0 / 100 = 0.20\). To achieve \(\se_{\text{target}} = 0.04\), solve \(s / \sqrt{n_{\text{target}}} = 0.04\), which yields \(\sqrt{n_{\text{target}}} = 20.0 / 0.04 = 500\), so \(n_{\text{target}} = 500^2 = 250,000\) paths. Reducing the standard error by a factor of 5 requires \(5^2 = 25\) times as many paths.

M3 · Confidence interval width

A Monte Carlo estimate of a portfolio value produces \(\hat{\theta} = 105.40\) with standard error \(\se = 0.15\). Compute the asymptotic 95% confidence interval for the true expectation using the standard normal critical value \(z_{0.975} = 1.96\).

Solution

The half-width of the 95% confidence interval is \(1.96 \times \se = 1.96 \times 0.15 = 0.294\). The confidence interval is \([105.40 - 0.294, 105.40 + 0.294] = [105.106, 105.694]\).

Proof

P1 · Periodicity and unit group order

Prove that for a multiplicative congruential generator \(s_{k+1} = a s_k \pmod m\) with \(s_0\) coprime to \(m\), the period \(p\) divides Euler's totient function \(\phi(m)\).

Solution

Since \(s_0\) is coprime to \(m\), \(s_0 \in (\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^\times\). By induction, \(s_k \equiv a^k s_0 \pmod m\). For states to remain non-zero, \(a\) must also be coprime to \(m\). The transformation corresponds to multiplying by \(a\) in the finite group of units \((\mathbb{Z}/m\mathbb{Z})^\times\). By Lagrange's theorem, the order of element \(a\) divides the group order, which is Euler's totient function \(\phi(m)\). The period is the smallest integer \(p \ge 1\) with \(a^p \equiv 1 \pmod m\), which is the multiplicative order \(\text{ord}_m(a)\). Thus \(p\) divides \(\phi(m)\) and satisfies \(p \le \phi(m) < m\).

P2 · Box-Muller transformation derivation

Derive the Box–Muller algorithm: let \(U_1, U_2 \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} \text{Uniform}(0, 1)\). Prove that \(Z_1 = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1} \cos(2\pi U_2)\) is standard normal.

Solution

The joint PDF of two independent standard normals is \(f(z_1, z_2) = \frac{1}{2\pi} e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}\). In polar coordinates \(z_1 = R \cos \Theta\), \(z_2 = R \sin \Theta\) with Jacobian \(J = R\), the joint density is \(f(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi} r e^{-r^2 / 2}\). Integrating shows \(\Theta \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 2\pi)\) and \(R^2 \sim \text{Exponential}(1/2)\). Setting \(\Theta = 2\pi U_2\) gives the uniform angle. For exponential variates, if \(U_1 \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)\), then \(-\ln U_1 \sim \text{Exponential}(1)\), so \(R^2 = -2 \ln U_1 \sim \text{Exponential}(1/2)\). Thus \(R = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1}\), and \(Z_1 = R \cos \Theta = \sqrt{-2 \ln U_1} \cos(2\pi U_2) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\).

P3 · Unbiasedness of sample variance

Prove that \(s_n^2 = \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2\) is an unbiased estimator of \(\sigma^2 = \Var(Y)\) for i.i.d. draws.

Solution

Expand the sum of squared differences: \(\sum (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2 = \sum (Y_i - \mu + \mu - \bar{Y})^2 = \sum (Y_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar{Y} - \mu)^2\). Taking expectations: \(\E\left[\sum (Y_i - \bar{Y})^2\right] = \sum \E[(Y_i - \mu)^2] - n \E[(\bar{Y} - \mu)^2] = n \sigma^2 - n \Var(\bar{Y})\). Since \(Y_i\) are independent, \(\Var(\bar{Y}) = \sigma^2 / n\). Substituting yields \(n \sigma^2 - n(\sigma^2 / n) = (n - 1) \sigma^2\). Dividing by \(n - 1\) gives \(\E[s_n^2] = \frac{(n-1)\sigma^2}{n-1} = \sigma^2\), establishing unbiasedness.

Research

R1 · Quasi-Monte Carlo vs pseudo-random Monte Carlo

Why do quantitative hedge funds often prefer pseudo-random Monte Carlo over quasi-Monte Carlo for high-dimensional stochastic volatility models?

Solution

While low-discrepancy quasi-Monte Carlo sequences achieve an asymptotic rate of \(O((\ln N)^d / N)\), the \((\ln N)^d\) term explodes when dimension \(d > 30\), requiring astronomical sample sizes before outperforming standard Monte Carlo. Furthermore, pseudo-random Monte Carlo provides straightforward, unbiased statistical error estimation via the sample variance \(\se = s / \sqrt{n}\), whereas deterministic QMC error bounds (Koksma–Hlawka) require bounding total variation in the sense of Hardy and Krause, which is computationally intractable in high dimensions.

R2 · Statistical validation of PRNGs (TestU01)

Why is passing 1D Kolmogorov–Smirnov uniformity tests insufficient to validate a PRNG for pricing multi-asset basket options?

Solution

A sequence of PRNG draws can possess perfectly uniform 1D marginal distributions while suffering severe high-dimensional dependencies. For example, consecutive pairs or \(k\)-tuples may fall on hyperplanes (the Marsaglia effect) or exhibit non-zero serial autocorrelation. In multi-asset option pricing, cross-sectional correlations and joint tail probabilities are distorted by high-dimensional structure, requiring comprehensive empirical test batteries such as TestU01's BigCrush suite.

R3 · Parallel PRNG streams on GPU clusters

Compare strided sequence advance (leap-frogging) with counter-based PRNGs (e.g. Philox) for massively parallel pricing engines on GPU architectures.

Solution

Leap-frogging assigns interleaved samples to threads, requiring fast state advance algorithms to skip \(P\) states in \(O(\log P)\) time; however, cache contention and non-contiguous memory access reduce throughput. Counter-based PRNGs (such as Philox or Threefry) evaluate a cryptographic bijection \(g_k(\text{counter})\) that computes the \(i\)-th random variate directly from an integer counter with zero state mutation. This permits completely independent, lock-free evaluation per thread without state synchronization overhead, making counter-based PRNGs the industry standard for GPU Monte Carlo simulation.

    By the Pigeonhole Principle, any deterministic state machine with a finite state space must eventually revisit a state and repeat periodically.

  3. A simulation of \(n = 2500\) independent payoffs gives a sample variance \(s^2 = 1.0\). What is the estimated standard error \(\se(\hat{\theta}_n)\)?

    \(\se = s / \sqrt{n} = \sqrt{1.0} / \sqrt{2500} = 1.0 / 50 = 0.02\).

  4. In Monte Carlo integration, how does the asymptotic convergence rate depend on the problem dimension \(d\)?

    Options

    The Monte Carlo rate \(O(N^{-1/2})\) is governed by the Central Limit Theorem and does not depend on the spatial dimension \(d\).

  5. According to Proposition 4.1, if a quadrature rule has order \(r = 8\), above what dimension \(d\) does Monte Carlo integration have a strictly faster asymptotic convergence rate than product quadrature?

    Monte Carlo is faster when \(r/d < 1/2\), which requires \(d > 2r = 2 \times 8 = 16\). Thus for dimensions strictly greater than 16, Monte Carlo dominates.

  6. What is the critical value \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) from the standard normal distribution used to construct an asymptotic 95% confidence interval?

    Options

    For \(\alpha = 0.05\), \(1 - \alpha/2 = 0.975\), and \(\Phi^{-1}(0.975) \approx 1.95996 \approx 1.960\).

  7. What is the theoretical expectation of the Monte Carlo estimation error \(\E[\hat{\theta}_n - \theta]\)?

    Because the Monte Carlo sample mean is an unbiased estimator, \(\E[\hat{\theta}_n] = \theta\), so the expected error is \(\E[\hat{\theta}_n - \theta] = 0\).

  8. Why does naive one-pass calculation of sample variance \(\frac{1}{n-1}(\sum Y_i^2 - n \bar{Y}^2)\) fail in floating-point arithmetic when \(\sigma \ll \mu\)?

    Options

    When \(\mu \gg \sigma\), \(\sum Y_i^2 \approx n \mu^2\) and \(n \bar{Y}^2 \approx n \mu^2\). Subtracting them cancels all leading bits, leaving only roundoff noise (catastrophic cancellation).

  9. Consider an LCG with modulus \(m = 16\). By Proposition 1.2, what is the maximum possible period of this generator?

    Since the state space has size \(|\mathcal{S}| = m = 16\), the period cannot exceed the size of the state space, which is 16.

  10. Which algorithm is recommended for calculating running sample variance stably in a single pass without catastrophic cancellation?

    Options

    Welford's algorithm updates the variance incrementally using differences from the running mean, maintaining numerical stability without cancellation.
