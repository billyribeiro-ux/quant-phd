Direct matrix factorizations form the computational engine of quantitative finance: simulating correlated asset returns, solving parabolic partial differential equations for American option pricing, and estimating regression betas. LU decomposition with partial pivoting solves general linear systems stably, while the Cholesky factorization exploits symmetry and positive definiteness to halve computation and storage requirements. Furthermore, numerically estimating sensitivities (option Greeks) via finite difference stencils introduces a fundamental tension between mathematical truncation error and floating-point roundoff error. Balancing this trade-off is critical for accurate risk management.

1Gaussian elimination and LU decomposition

Solving a general linear system \(A x = b\) where \(A \in \R^{n \times n}\) is non-singular can be achieved by factoring \(A\) into triangular components.

Definition 1.1 (LU decomposition with partial pivoting)

For any non-singular matrix \(A \in \R^{n \times n}\), there exists a permutation matrix \(P\), a unit lower-triangular matrix \(L\) (with \(L_{ii} = 1\) and \(|L_{ij}| \le 1\) for \(i > j\)), and an upper-triangular matrix \(U\) such that:

\[ P A = L U \]

Solving \(A x = b\) decomposes into two triangular systems: forward substitution \(L y = P b\), followed by back substitution \(U x = y\), each requiring \(O(n^2)\) flops once the \(O(\frac{2}{3} n^3)\) factorization is computed.

Partial pivoting chooses the row with the largest absolute entry in the active column as the pivot element before each elimination step, guaranteeing that all multipliers satisfy \(|L_{ij}| \le 1\) and preventing roundoff explosion.

2The Cholesky decomposition

When the coefficient matrix \(A\) is symmetric positive-definite (SPD), Gaussian elimination can be performed symmetrically without pivoting.

Definition 2.1 (Cholesky factorization)

Let \(A \in \R^{n \times n}\) be a symmetric positive-definite matrix (\(A = A^\top\) and \(x^\top A x > 0\) for all \(x \ne 0\)). The Cholesky decomposition is the unique factorization:

\[ A = L L^\top \]

where \(L\) is a lower-triangular matrix with strictly positive diagonal entries \(L_{ii} > 0\).

Theorem 2.2 (existence and uniqueness of Cholesky factorization)

A real symmetric matrix \(A \in \R^{n \times n}\) has a unique Cholesky factorization \(A = L L^\top\) with \(L_{ii} > 0\) if and only if \(A\) is positive-definite.

Proof

We proceed by induction on the matrix dimension \(n\). For \(n = 1\), \(A = [a_{11}] > 0\). Choosing \(L = [\sqrt{a_{11}}]\) gives \(L L^\top = [a_{11}] = A\), with \(L_{11} > 0\) uniquely determined. Assume the theorem holds for matrices of dimension \(n - 1\). Partition \(A \in \R^{n \times n}\) as:

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & v^\top \\ v & B \end{pmatrix}, \quad a_{11} \in \R, \ v \in \R^{n-1}, \ B \in \R^{(n-1) \times (n-1)} \]

Since \(A\) is positive-definite, \(a_{11} = e_1^\top A e_1 > 0\). We seek \(L\) partitioned as:

\[ L = \begin{pmatrix} l_{11} & 0 \\ w & L_1 \end{pmatrix} \implies L L^\top = \begin{pmatrix} l_{11}^2 & l_{11} w^\top \\ l_{11} w & w w^\top + L_1 L_1^\top \end{pmatrix} \]

Equating blocks requires: (1) \(l_{11} = \sqrt{a_{11}} > 0\) (uniquely defined); (2) \(w = v / l_{11}\) (uniquely defined); and (3) \(L_1 L_1^\top = B - w w^\top = B - \frac{1}{a_{11}} v v^\top\). The matrix \(S = B - \frac{1}{a_{11}} v v^\top\) is the Schur complement of \(a_{11}\) in \(A\). For any non-zero \(z \in \R^{n-1}\), let \(y = \begin{pmatrix} -v^\top z / a_{11} \\ z \end{pmatrix} \ne 0\). Then \(y^\top A y = z^\top S z > 0\), so \(S\) is symmetric positive-definite of dimension \(n - 1\). By the induction hypothesis, \(S\) has a unique Cholesky factorization \(S = L_1 L_1^\top\) with positive diagonal. Hence \(L\) exists and is unique. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.3 (computational cost and stability)

Cholesky requires \(\frac{1}{3} n^3\) flops and \(n(n+1)/2\) storage locations, exactly half the operations of general LU. Because \(A_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^i L_{ij}^2\), every entry of \(L\) is strictly bounded: \(|L_{ij}| \le \sqrt{A_{ii}}\). This intrinsic bound prevents any element growth, rendering Cholesky unconditionally backward stable without pivoting.

3Finite-difference stencils and the step-size trade-off

Finite differences approximate derivatives by replacing infinitesimal differentials with finite increments \(h > 0\).

Definition 3.1 (finite difference stencils)

For a sufficiently smooth function \(f\), the standard finite difference stencils for the first derivative \(f'(x)\) are:

  • Forward difference: \(D_+ f(x) = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}\) (first-order, \(O(h)\))
  • Central difference: \(D_0 f(x) = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h}\) (second-order, \(O(h^2)\))
Proposition 3.2 (truncation and roundoff trade-off in forward difference)

Assume \(f \in C^2[x, x + h]\) with \(|f''(t)| \le M_2\) and function evaluations satisfy \(\text{fl}(f(t)) = f(t)(1 + \delta)\) with \(|\delta| \le u\). The total computational error in the forward difference is bounded by:

\[ \left|\text{fl}(D_+ f(x)) - f'(x)\right| \le \frac{M_2}{2} h + \frac{2 u |f(x)|}{h} \]

The error bound is minimized at the optimal step size:

\[ h^* = 2 \sqrt{\frac{u |f(x)|}{M_2}} \propto \sqrt{u} \]

Proof

By Taylor's theorem, \(f(x + h) = f(x) + h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(\xi)\). The mathematical truncation error is \(\left|\frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} - f'(x)\right| = \frac{h}{2} |f''(\xi)| \le \frac{M_2}{2} h\). When evaluated in floating-point arithmetic, \(\tilde{f}(x) = f(x) + \varepsilon_1\) and \(\tilde{f}(x + h) = f(x + h) + \varepsilon_2\) with \(|\varepsilon_1|, |\varepsilon_2| \le u |f(x)|\). The roundoff error in the numerator is \(|\varepsilon_2 - \varepsilon_1| \le 2 u |f(x)|\). Dividing by \(h\), the roundoff error contribution is \(\frac{2 u |f(x)|}{h}\). The total error bound is \(E(h) = \frac{M_2}{2} h + \frac{2 u |f(x)|}{h}\). Setting \(\frac{d E}{dh} = \frac{M_2}{2} - \frac{2 u |f(x)|}{h^2} = 0\) yields \(h^{*2} = \frac{4 u |f(x)|}{M_2}\), so \(h^* = 2 \sqrt{\frac{u |f(x)|}{M_2}}\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.3 (central difference optimal step size)

For the central difference, Taylor expansion shows that first-order truncation terms cancel, leaving truncation error \(\frac{M_3}{6} h^2\). Minimizing \(\frac{M_3}{6} h^2 + \frac{u |f|}{h}\) yields \(h^* \propto u^{1/3} \approx (10^{-16})^{1/3} \approx 10^{-5}\), achieving a minimum error of \(O(u^{2/3}) \approx 10^{-11}\), substantially more accurate than forward differences (\(O(u^{1/2}) \approx 10^{-8}\)).

4Inline laboratory: Cholesky and finite difference U-curve

Lab 1 · Cholesky decomposition and finite difference step-size trade-off

Cholesky factorization of a 3x3 SPD matrix produces exact rational factors in Fraction that reconstruct \(A\) with zero error. For the forward difference approximation of \(\frac{d}{dx} e^x\) at \(x = 1\), error follows a U-shaped curve: truncation dominates for \(h > 10^{-8}\), roundoff dominates for \(h < 10^{-8}\), and the optimal error \(\approx 6.6 \times 10^{-9}\) occurs at \(h \approx \sqrt{2u} \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-8}\).

"""Lab M8-L04-1: Cholesky decomposition and finite difference step-size trade-off.

Standard library only. Demonstrates exact Cholesky factorization in Fraction
and the truncation-roundoff error balance in forward finite differences.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from fractions import Fraction


def cholesky_3x3_fraction(a: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[list[Fraction]]:
    """Compute Cholesky factor L for 3x3 SPD matrix in exact arithmetic."""
    l_mat: list[list[Fraction]] = [[Fraction(0) for _ in range(3)] for _ in range(3)]

    # Row 0
    # L00 = sqrt(A00)
    l00_sq = a[0][0]
    # For our chosen test matrix, l00_sq is a perfect square of a rational
    l00 = Fraction(math.isqrt(l00_sq.numerator), math.isqrt(l00_sq.denominator))
    l_mat[0][0] = l00
    l_mat[1][0] = a[1][0] / l00
    l_mat[2][0] = a[2][0] / l00

    # Row 1
    l11_sq = a[1][1] - l_mat[1][0] ** 2
    l11 = Fraction(math.isqrt(l11_sq.numerator), math.isqrt(l11_sq.denominator))
    l_mat[1][1] = l11
    l_mat[2][1] = (a[2][1] - l_mat[2][0] * l_mat[1][0]) / l11

    # Row 2
    l22_sq = a[2][2] - l_mat[2][0] ** 2 - l_mat[2][1] ** 2
    l22 = Fraction(math.isqrt(l22_sq.numerator), math.isqrt(l22_sq.denominator))
    l_mat[2][2] = l22

    return l_mat


def matmul_3x3_fraction(a: list[list[Fraction]], b: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[list[Fraction]]:
    """Matrix multiplication in Fraction."""
    res: list[list[Fraction]] = [[Fraction(0) for _ in range(3)] for _ in range(3)]
    for i in range(3):
        for j in range(3):
            res[i][j] = sum((a[i][k] * b[k][j] for k in range(3)), Fraction(0))
    return res


def transpose_3x3_fraction(a: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[list[Fraction]]:
    return [[a[j][i] for j in range(3)] for i in range(3)]


def main() -> None:
    print("=== Part A: Cholesky Decomposition of 3x3 SPD Matrix ===")
    # A = [[4, 2, -2], [2, 10, 2], [-2, 2, 6]]
    a_frac = [
        [Fraction(4), Fraction(2), Fraction(-2)],
        [Fraction(2), Fraction(10), Fraction(2)],
        [Fraction(-2), Fraction(2), Fraction(6)],
    ]
    l_frac = cholesky_3x3_fraction(a_frac)
    lt_frac = transpose_3x3_fraction(l_frac)
    reconstructed = matmul_3x3_fraction(l_frac, lt_frac)

    print("Matrix A:")
    for row in a_frac:
        print("  ", [str(x) for x in row])
    print("Cholesky factor L (exact Fraction):")
    for row in l_frac:
        print("  ", [str(x) for x in row])
    print("L * L^T matches A exactly:", reconstructed == a_frac)

    print("\n=== Part B: Forward Difference Step-Size Trade-off on d/dx exp(x) at x=1 ===")
    x0 = 1.0
    true_deriv = math.exp(x0)
    u = 2.0 ** (-53)  # unit roundoff for binary64
    print(f"True derivative exp(1): {true_deriv:.16f}")
    print(f"Machine unit roundoff u: {u:.2e}")
    print(f"Theoretical optimal step h* ~ sqrt(2*u): {math.sqrt(2.0 * u):.2e}")
    print(" k    Step h       FD Approximation       Absolute Error   Dominant Reg.")

    for k in range(1, 17):
        h = 10.0 ** (-k)
        fd_approx = (math.exp(x0 + h) - math.exp(x0)) / h
        err = abs(fd_approx - true_deriv)
        regime = "Truncation" if k < 8 else ("Optimal" if k == 8 else "Roundoff")
        print(f"{k:2d}   {h:.1e}   {fd_approx:.16f}   {err:.2e}   {regime}")


main()

5Where the canon breaks

  • Empirical covariance matrices can fail positive definiteness. Sample covariance matrices estimated with missing data or asynchronous quotes can have negative eigenvalues due to nonsynchronous closing prices. Attempting Cholesky factorization on such matrices fails with a square-root-of-negative-number exception. In production, eigenvalue clipping or higham nearest correlation matrix projection must precede Cholesky.
  • Step size cannot be arbitrarily small in Greeks calculation. Bumping an underlying price by \(h = 10^{-8}\) in complex option pricing models can trigger catastrophic cancellation in payoffs. For payout functions with jump discontinuities (e.g. Digitals or barriers), finite differences fail to converge; likelihood ratio or pathwise automatic differentiation must be used.

6Misconceptions

  1. “Choosing a smaller step size \(h\) in numerical differentiation always increases accuracy.”

    In exact continuous calculus, \(\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} = f'(x)\). In floating-point arithmetic, as \(h \to 0\), \(\text{fl}(f(x+h)) - \text{fl}(f(x))\) suffers from catastrophic cancellation, leaving roundoff error to be divided by \(h\), driving the computational error to infinity.

  2. “Cholesky factorization requires pivoting to maintain numerical stability.”

    Unlike general LU decomposition, Cholesky factorization requires no row or column pivoting because \(L_{ij}^2 \le A_{ii}\) guarantees that multipliers are bounded by matrix diagonal entries. Cholesky is unconditionally backward stable.

  3. “LU decomposition without pivoting is acceptable as long as the diagonal entries \(A_{ii}\) are non-zero.”

    Even if \(A_{ii} \ne 0\), if a diagonal entry is tiny relative to off-diagonal elements, dividing by the pivot creates massive multipliers (\(|L_{ij}| \gg 1\)), which leads to catastrophic roundoff growth in subsequent elimination stages.

7Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Manual Cholesky computation

Compute by hand the Cholesky factor \(L\) for \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 9 & 6 \\ 6 & 13 \end{pmatrix}\).

Solution

For a 2x2 SPD matrix: \(L_{11} = \sqrt{A_{11}} = \sqrt{9} = 3\). Next, \(L_{21} = A_{21} / L_{11} = 6 / 3 = 2\). Finally, \(L_{22} = \sqrt{A_{22} - L_{21}^2} = \sqrt{13 - 2^2} = \sqrt{9} = 3\). The Cholesky factor is \(L = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Verify: \(L L^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 9 & 6 \\ 6 & 13 \end{pmatrix} = A\).

M2 · Optimal central difference step size

For \(f(x) = \sin(x)\) at \(x = 1\) in binary64 (\(u \approx 1.11 \times 10^{-16}\)), calculate the theoretical optimal step size \(h^*\) for central difference approximation.

Solution

For central differences, the third derivative is \(f'''(x) = -\cos(x)\), so \(|f'''(1)| = \cos(1) \approx 0.5403\). Also \(|f(1)| = \sin(1) \approx 0.8415\). The total error bound is \(E(h) = \frac{M_3}{6} h^2 + \frac{u |f|}{h}\). Setting \(E'(h) = \frac{M_3}{3} h - \frac{u |f|}{h^2} = 0\) yields \(h^* = \left(\frac{3 u |f|}{M_3}\right)^{1/3} = \left(\frac{3 \times 1.11 \times 10^{-16} \times 0.8415}{0.5403}\right)^{1/3} \approx (5.186 \times 10^{-16})^{1/3} \approx 8.03 \times 10^{-6}\).

M3 · Correlated normal generation via Cholesky

Explain how the Cholesky factor \(L\) of a correlation matrix \(\Sigma\) converts a vector of independent standard normals \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)\) into correlated normals \(X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)\).

Solution

Define \(X = L Z\). Since expectation is linear, \(\E[X] = L \E[Z] = 0\). The covariance matrix is \(\Cov(X) = \E[X X^\top] = \E[(L Z)(L Z)^\top] = \E[L Z Z^\top L^\top] = L \E[Z Z^\top] L^\top\). Since \(Z\) has independent standard components, \(\E[Z Z^\top] = I\). Thus \(\Cov(X) = L I L^\top = L L^\top = \Sigma\). This provides the standard method for multi-asset Monte Carlo simulation.

P1 · Central difference truncation order

Using Taylor expansions, prove that the central difference stencil \(D_0 f(x) = \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h}\) has truncation error \(O(h^2)\) for \(f \in C^3\).

Solution

Expand \(f(x + h)\) and \(f(x - h)\) to third order: \(f(x + h) = f(x) + h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) + \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(\xi_1)\), and \(f(x - h) = f(x) - h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) - \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(\xi_2)\). Subtracting the two expansions: \(f(x + h) - f(x - h) = 2h f'(x) + \frac{h^3}{6} (f'''(\xi_1) + f'''(\xi_2))\). Dividing by \(2h\): \(\frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h} = f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{12} (f'''(\xi_1) + f'''(\xi_2))\). The even powers of \(h\) cancel out completely, proving the error is \(O(h^2)\).

P2 · Second derivative central stencil

Derive the standard central difference stencil for the second derivative \(f''(x)\) and prove its truncation error is \(O(h^2)\).

Solution

Expand \(f(x + h) = f(x) + h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) + \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \frac{h^4}{24} f^{(4)}(\xi_1)\), and \(f(x - h) = f(x) - h f'(x) + \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) - \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + \frac{h^4}{24} f^{(4)}(\xi_2)\). Adding the two expansions: \(f(x + h) + f(x - h) = 2 f(x) + h^2 f''(x) + \frac{h^4}{24} (f^{(4)}(\xi_1) + f^{(4)}(\xi_2))\). Rearranging: \(\frac{f(x + h) - 2f(x) + f(x - h)}{h^2} = f''(x) + O(h^2)\). This is the standard 3-point stencil for the second derivative (e.g. option gamma \(\frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\)).

P3 · Positive definiteness of Schur complement

Prove that if \(A = \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & v^\top \\ v & B \end{pmatrix}\) is symmetric positive-definite, then the Schur complement \(S = B - \frac{1}{a_{11}} v v^\top\) is also symmetric positive-definite.

Solution

Symmetry of \(S\) follows directly because \(B = B^\top\) and \((v v^\top)^\top = v v^\top\). To show positive-definiteness, let \(z \in \R^{n-1}\) be any non-zero vector. Construct the vector \(y \in \R^n\) as \(y = \begin{pmatrix} -\frac{v^\top z}{a_{11}} \\ z \end{pmatrix}\). Since \(z \ne 0\), \(y \ne 0\). Compute \(y^\top A y = \begin{pmatrix} -\frac{v^\top z}{a_{11}} & z^\top \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} a_{11} & v^\top \\ v & B \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} -\frac{v^\top z}{a_{11}} \\ z \end{pmatrix} = a_{11} \left(\frac{v^\top z}{a_{11}}\right)^2 - 2 \frac{v^\top z}{a_{11}} (v^\top z) + z^\top B z = -\frac{(v^\top z)^2}{a_{11}} + z^\top B z = z^\top \left(B - \frac{1}{a_{11}} v v^\top\right) z = z^\top S z\). Since \(A\) is positive-definite and \(y \ne 0\), \(y^\top A y > 0\), which proves \(z^\top S z > 0\) for all \(z \ne 0\).

E3 · Flop count comparison

For an equity portfolio of \(N = 1000\) assets, compare the number of floating-point operations required to solve \(A x = b\) using standard LU versus Cholesky factorization.

Solution

General LU decomposition requires \(\frac{2}{3} N^3 = \frac{2}{3} \times 10^9 \approx 6.67 \times 10^8\) flops. Cholesky factorization requires \(\frac{1}{3} N^3 = \frac{1}{3} \times 10^9 \approx 3.33 \times 10^8\) flops. Cholesky saves exactly \(3.33 \times 10^8\) operations (a 50% reduction) and avoids storing the upper triangle.

Research

R1 · Higham's nearest correlation matrix

Describe Nicholas Higham's alternating projections algorithm for finding the nearest valid correlation matrix to an empirical matrix with negative eigenvalues.

Solution

Higham's algorithm solves \(\min_{X} \|A - X\|_F\) subject to \(X \in \mathcal{S}_n^+\) (positive semidefinite) and \(\text{diag}(X) = 1\). The algorithm alternates between two orthogonal projections in Frobenius norm: (1) project onto \(\mathcal{S}_n^+\) by computing spectral decomposition \(X = Q \Lambda Q^\top\) and replacing \(\Lambda\) with \(\max(\Lambda, 0)\); and (2) project onto unit diagonal matrices by setting \(X_{ii} = 1\). Using Dykstra's correction for convex sets, alternating projections converges to the unique global nearest correlation matrix, which can then be factored via Cholesky.

R2 · Complex-step derivative approximation

Explain how the complex-step derivative formula \(f'(x) \approx \frac{\text{Im}(f(x + i h))}{h}\) eliminates catastrophic cancellation, allowing \(h\) to be chosen as small as \(10^{-100}\).

Solution

Expanding \(f(x + i h)\) in complex Taylor series: \(f(x + ih) = f(x) + ih f'(x) - \frac{h^2}{2} f''(x) - i \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + O(h^4)\). Taking the imaginary part: \(\text{Im}(f(x + ih)) = h f'(x) - \frac{h^3}{6} f'''(x) + O(h^5)\). Dividing by \(h\): \(\frac{\text{Im}(f(x + ih))}{h} = f'(x) - \frac{h^2}{6} f'''(x) + O(h^4)\). Crucially, this formula involves no subtraction of function values; the numerator is evaluated directly from the imaginary part of complex arithmetic. Because there is no difference \(\tilde{f}(x+h) - \tilde{f}(x)\), no catastrophic cancellation can occur, and \(h = 10^{-20}\) produces machine-exact derivatives without roundoff error.

R3 · Adjoint algorithmic differentiation (AAD)

Why has Adjoint Algorithmic Differentiation (AAD) replaced finite differences for portfolio risk sensitivity calculations on major quantitative desks?

Solution

To compute \(N\) Greeks using finite differences requires \(N + 1\) full portfolio pricing runs (or \(2N\) for central differences), scaling as \(O(N)\). For a bank holding thousands of contracts dependent on \(N \approx 10{,}000\) risk factors (yield curves, volatility smiles, FX spots), finite differences require hours of compute. AAD implements the reverse mode of automatic differentiation (applying the chain rule backwards from the scalar portfolio value to all inputs). The Baur-Skeel / Griewank theorem proves that computing all \(N\) sensitivities via AAD takes at most 3 to 4 times the computational cost of a single pricing run, independent of \(N\), providing an acceleration of several orders of magnitude.

  1. What structural properties must a real matrix \(A\) possess to admit a Cholesky factorization \(A = L L^\top\)?

    Options

    By Theorem 2.2, a real matrix has a unique Cholesky factorization if and only if it is symmetric positive-definite.

  2. What fraction of the floating-point operations of general LU factorization is required by Cholesky factorization?

    Cholesky requires \(\frac{1}{3} n^3\) flops compared to \(\frac{2}{3} n^3\) for general LU, exactly a fraction of 0.5 (half the operations).

  3. Why does Cholesky factorization not require row or column pivoting?

    Options

    Because \(A_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^i L_{ij}^2\), entries of \(L\) are bounded by \(\sqrt{A_{ii}}\), preventing element growth without pivoting.

  4. What is the truncation error order of the forward difference stencil \(D_+ f(x) = \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h}\)?

    Options

    By Taylor expansion, \(f(x+h) = f(x) + h f'(x) + O(h^2)\), so \((f(x+h) - f(x))/h = f'(x) + O(h)\).

  5. What is the theoretical optimal step size \(h^*\) for forward differences in binary64 arithmetic?

    Options

    Balancing \(O(h)\) truncation with \(O(u/h)\) roundoff error yields \(h^* \propto \sqrt{u} \approx 10^{-8}\).

  6. What is the truncation error order \(p\) in \(O(h^p)\) for the central difference stencil \(\frac{f(x+h) - f(x-h)}{2h}\)?

    Central difference has order \(p = 2\) because odd powers of \(h\) cancel out in the symmetric Taylor subtraction.

  7. In Lab 1, what happens to the forward difference error when \(h\) is decreased from \(10^{-8}\) to \(10^{-16}\)?

    Options

    For \(h < 10^{-8}\), roundoff error \(O(u/h)\) dominates and grows inversely with \(h\), reaching \(2.72\) at \(h = 10^{-16}\).

  8. How does complex-step differentiation achieve exact derivatives without roundoff error?

    Options

    Complex-step differentiation extracts the derivative from the imaginary part without subtracting function values, avoiding cancellation entirely.
