Importance Sampling and Robust Inversion

  • Uses: normal distribution (M4), implied volatility (FC2), option arbitrage bounds (FC2), condition number (L02), Newton's method (L03)
  • Introduces: importance sampling, robust implied-volatility inversion
  • Ttheorem
  • Areplicated
  • Bpublished, not settled
  • Csingle study or convention
  • Dlore
  • Xrefuted

Rare financial events—such as deep out-of-the-money barrier breaches, catastrophic credit defaults, or multi-sigma market crashes—virtually never occur under crude Monte Carlo paths. Standard sampling wastes computation observing common outcomes while relative error on tail probabilities explodes to infinity. Importance sampling changes the simulation measure, exponentially tilting the proposal density to force frequent occurrences of rare events and recovering unbiased expectations via likelihood ratio reweighting. Inverting implied volatility faces an analogous breakdown: fixed brackets reject valid high-volatility quotes, while vanishing vega destabilizes unconstrained Newton updates. Dynamic bracket expansion paired with safeguarded Newton steps produces an industrial solver that guarantees convergence across extreme market conditions.

1Rare events and relative error breakdown

Consider estimating a tail probability \(p = \mathbb{P}(X > c) = \E[\mathbb{I}_{\{X > c\}}]\) for an extreme threshold \(c \gg 0\) where \(p \ll 1\). Under crude Monte Carlo with indicator variates \(Y_i = \mathbb{I}_{\{X_i > c\}}\), the variance is \(\Var(Y) = p(1 - p) \approx p\).

The standard error is \(\se = \sqrt{p(1 - p)/n} \approx \sqrt{p/n}\). The relative standard error is:

\[ \frac{\se}{\hat{p}} \approx \frac{\sqrt{p/n}}{p} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n p}} \]

To achieve a modest 10% relative accuracy (\(\frac{\se}{p} = 0.10\)), the required sample size satisfies \(n p = 100\), or \(n = 100 / p\). For a rare credit event with probability \(p = 10^{-6}\), crude Monte Carlo demands \(n = 10^8\) paths, with over 99.9999% of generated paths producing useless zeros.

2Importance sampling and exponential tilting

Importance sampling replaces the nominal probability density with a proposal density that assigns high probability to the rare event of interest.

Definition 2.1 (importance sampling)

Importance sampling estimates an expectation \(\theta = \E_f[g(X)] = \int_{\mathcal{X}} g(x) f(x) dx\) under nominal density \(f\) by sampling from an alternative proposal density \(q\) satisfying \(q(x) > 0\) whenever \(g(x) f(x) \ne 0\):

\[ \theta = \int_{\mathcal{X}} g(x) \frac{f(x)}{q(x)} q(x) dx = \E_q[g(Y) w(Y)] \]

where \(w(y) = \frac{f(y)}{q(y)}\) is the likelihood ratio (Radon–Nikodym derivative), and \(Y \sim q\). The importance sampling estimator from \(n\) i.i.d. draws \(Y_1, \dots, Y_n \sim q\) is:

\[ \hat{\theta}_{\text{is}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n g(Y_i) w(Y_i) \]

Theorem 2.2 (unbiasedness and variance of importance sampling)

Assume the proposal support condition holds: \(q(x) > 0\) whenever \(g(x)f(x) \ne 0\). Then:

  1. \(\hat{\theta}_{\text{is}}\) is strictly unbiased: \(\E_q[\hat{\theta}_{\text{is}}] = \theta\).
  2. The variance of a single draw is: \[ \Var_q(g(Y)w(Y)) = \int_{\mathcal{X}} \frac{(g(x) f(x))^2}{q(x)} dx - \theta^2 \]
  3. If \(g(x) \ge 0\), the zero-variance optimal proposal density is: \[ q^*(x) = \frac{g(x) f(x)}{\int g(y) f(y) dy} = \frac{g(x) f(x)}{\theta} \] Under \(q^*\), \(g(Y) w(Y) = g(Y) \frac{f(Y)}{g(Y) f(Y) / \theta} = \theta\) is constant, yielding \(\Var_{q^*}(g(Y) w(Y)) = 0\).
Proof

Unbiasedness: \(\E_q[g(Y) w(Y)] = \int_{\mathcal{X}} g(y) \frac{f(y)}{q(y)} q(y) dy = \int_{\mathcal{X}} g(y) f(y) dy = \theta\). For variance: \(\E_q[(g(Y) w(Y))^2] = \int (g(y) \frac{f(y)}{q(y)})^2 q(y) dy = \int \frac{(g(y) f(y))^2}{q(y)} dy\). Subtracting \(\theta^2\) gives the variance formula. Under \(q^*(x) = g(x) f(x) / \theta\), the ratio \(g(x) w(x) = g(x) \frac{f(x)}{g(x) f(x) / \theta} = \theta\) for every \(x\). The random variable is deterministic, so its variance is identically zero. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.3 (exponential tilting for Gaussian tail probability)

To estimate \(p = \mathbb{P}(Z > c)\) where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\), choose the mean-shifted Gaussian proposal \(Y \sim \mathcal{N}(c, 1)\). The likelihood ratio is:

\[ w(y) = \frac{\phi(y)}{\phi(y - c)} = \frac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-y^2 / 2}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-(y - c)^2 / 2}} = \exp\left( -c y + \frac{c^2}{2} \right) \]

For any \(y \ge c\), \(w(y) \le e^{-c^2 / 2}\), guaranteeing that the second moment of the estimator is strictly bounded by \(e^{-c^2 / 2} p\), preventing weight explosion (Glasserman 2003, §4.6).

3Robust implied-volatility inversion

Market quotes on option exchanges require solving \(C_{\text{BS}}(S, K, \tau, r, \sigma) = C_{\text{market}}\) for the implied volatility \(\sigma\). Standard implementations suffer from two catastrophic failure modes:

  1. Fixed-bracket failure: Setting an ad hoc fixed search bracket such as \([10^{-6}, 10]\) fails whenever extreme quotes or high volatility push \(\sigma > 10\), even though the price satisfies strict no-arbitrage bounds \(C_{\text{market}} < S\).
  2. Vega collapse and Newton divergence: As \(\sigma \to 0\) or \(\sigma \to \infty\), or when \(\tau \to 0\) (near expiry) or \(|S - K| \gg 0\) (deep out-of-the-money), option vega \(\nu = \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} = S \sqrt{\tau} \phi(d_1)\) underflows to zero. An unconstrained Newton step \(\Delta \sigma = -f(\sigma)/\nu\) explodes, shooting the iterate to negative values or infinity.
Definition 3.1 (robust implied-volatility inversion)

Robust implied-volatility inversion is an algorithm that guarantees convergence for every valid market quote inside the strict no-arbitrage bounds by combining:

  1. Input validation: Rejecting quotes on or outside strict arbitrage bounds: for a call, \(C \in (\max(S - K e^{-r\tau}, 0), S)\); for a put, \(P \in (\max(K e^{-r\tau} - S, 0), K e^{-r\tau})\).
  2. Dynamic bracket expansion: Initializing at \([a, b] = [1.0, 2.0]\) and geometrically expanding \(b \leftarrow 2b\) until \(C(b) \ge C_{\text{market}}\), guaranteeing a valid bracket containing the true root.
  3. Safeguarded Newton acceleration: Attempting a Newton step \(\sigma_{k+1} = \sigma_k - f(\sigma_k)/\nu\); if the step falls outside the current bracket \((a, b)\) or if \(\nu < \nu_{\text{tol}}\), falling back to bisection \(\sigma_{k+1} = \frac{1}{2}(a + b)\).
  4. Conditioning classification: Tagging inputs where \(\nu(\hat{\sigma}) \ge 0.01\) as well-conditioned ("solved"), and inputs with \(\nu(\hat{\sigma}) < 0.01\) as ill-conditioned ("ill_conditioned").
Theorem 3.2 (global convergence and residual accuracy)

Let \(C_{\text{market}}\) satisfy the strict no-arbitrage bounds. Dynamic bracket expansion terminates in a finite number of steps \(k \le \lceil \log_2(\sigma^* / 2) \rceil + 1\). Safeguarded Newton bisection converges unconditionally to a solution \(\hat{\sigma}\) satisfying:

\[ |C(\hat{\sigma}) - C_{\text{market}}| \le 10^{-12} \cdot S \]

in at most \(B \le 35\) pricing evaluations.

4Inline laboratories: importance sampling and bracket expansion

Lab 1 · Importance sampling for Gaussian tail probability (SIMULATED)

For estimating the Gaussian tail probability \(\mathbb{P}(Z > 4) = \frac{1}{2} \text{erfc}(4/\sqrt{2}) \approx 3.167124 \times 10^{-5}\) with \(N = 100,000\) draws: (i) crude Monte Carlo will observe only 2 to 5 hits, exhibiting high relative standard error (\(> 50\%\)); (ii) importance sampling with mean shift \(\mu = 4\) will evaluate every path with positive weight, reducing standard error by more than 80-fold and variance by more than 6,000-fold.

"""Lab M8-L08-1: Importance sampling for Gaussian tail probability estimation.

Standard library only. Compares crude Monte Carlo to exponentially tilted importance
sampling for estimating P(Z > 4) (SIMULATED).
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


def main() -> None:
    c = 4.0
    # Exact probability: P(Z > 4) = 1 - Phi(4) = 1/2 erfc(4 / sqrt(2))
    exact_prob = 0.5 * math.erfc(c / math.sqrt(2.0))

    print("=== Importance Sampling for Gaussian Tail P(Z > 4) (SIMULATED) ===")
    print(f"Exact theoretical tail probability: {exact_prob:.12e}")

    n = 100_000
    rng = random.Random(42)

    # 1. Crude Monte Carlo: Z ~ N(0, 1)
    z_crude = [rng.gauss(0.0, 1.0) for _ in range(n)]
    hits_crude = [1.0 if z > c else 0.0 for z in z_crude]
    p_crude = sum(hits_crude) / n
    var_crude = p_crude * (1.0 - p_crude)
    se_crude = math.sqrt(var_crude / n)
    rel_se_crude = (se_crude / p_crude) if p_crude > 0 else float("inf")

    # 2. Importance Sampling with shifted proposal Y ~ N(mu, 1) with mu = c = 4
    # Likelihood ratio w(y) = phi(y) / phi(y - c) = exp(-c*y + c^2 / 2)
    # Integrand: 1_{Y > c} * exp(-c*Y + c^2 / 2)
    rng_is = random.Random(42)
    y_is = [rng_is.gauss(c, 1.0) for _ in range(n)]
    c_sq_half = 0.5 * c * c
    weights = [math.exp(-c * y + c_sq_half) if y > c else 0.0 for y in y_is]

    p_is = sum(weights) / n
    var_is = sum((w - p_is) ** 2 for w in weights) / (n - 1)
    se_is = math.sqrt(var_is / n)
    rel_se_is = se_is / p_is

    print(f"\nSample size: N = {n}")
    print(f"Crude MC:     Estimate = {p_crude:.6e}, Hits = {int(sum(hits_crude))}")
    print(f"              Std Error = {se_crude:.4e}, Rel Std Error = {rel_se_crude:.2%}")
    print(f"Importance S: Estimate = {p_is:.6e}")
    print(f"              Std Error = {se_is:.4e}, Rel Std Error = {rel_se_is:.2%}")
    print(f"Variance reduction factor Var(Crude) / Var(IS): {var_crude / var_is:.1f}x")
    print(f"Standard error reduction factor SE(Crude) / SE(IS): {se_crude / se_is:.1f}x")


main()

Lab 2 · Dynamic bracket expansion for implied-volatility inversion

For an extreme high-volatility call quote \(S = 100, K = 100, \tau = 1.0, r = 0.05, C = 99.99995\): (i) a fixed bracket \([10^{-6}, 10]\) fails because \(C(10.0) = 99.99994409 < C_{\text{market}}\); (ii) dynamic bracket expansion doubles the upper bound to \([8.0, 16.0]\) in 3 steps, successfully bracketing the root; (iii) safeguarded Newton inversion converges in 5 iterations to \(\hat{\sigma} \approx 10.04302364\) with price residual \(|C(\hat{\sigma}) - C| < 10^{-12} S\).

"""Lab M8-L08-2: Dynamic bracket expansion for implied-volatility inversion.

Standard library only. Compares a naive fixed bracket [1e-6, 10] to dynamic bracket expansion
on an extreme high-volatility call quote.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math


def bs_call_price(s: float, k: float, tau: float, r: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    sqrt_t = math.sqrt(tau)
    v_sqrt_t = sigma * sqrt_t
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * tau) / v_sqrt_t
    d2 = d1 - v_sqrt_t
    disc = math.exp(-r * tau)
    c1 = 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(d1 / math.sqrt(2.0)))
    c2 = 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(d2 / math.sqrt(2.0)))
    return s * c1 - k * disc * c2


def vega(s: float, k: float, tau: float, r: float, sigma: float) -> float:
    sqrt_t = math.sqrt(tau)
    d1 = (math.log(s / k) + (r + 0.5 * sigma * sigma) * tau) / (sigma * sqrt_t)
    return s * sqrt_t * math.exp(-0.5 * d1 * d1) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)


def main() -> None:
    s = 100.0
    k = 100.0
    tau = 1.0
    r = 0.05
    target_price = 99.99995

    print("=== Fixed vs Dynamic Bracket Inversion for High-Volatility Call ===")
    print(f"Option quote: S={s}, K={k}, tau={tau}, r={r}, target price={target_price}")
    print(f"Strict no-arbitrage upper bound (S): {s}")
    print(f"Quote is strictly valid (price < S): {target_price < s}")

    # 1. Fixed bracket [1e-6, 10]
    lo_fixed, hi_fixed = 1e-6, 10.0
    c_lo_fixed = bs_call_price(s, k, tau, r, lo_fixed)
    c_hi_fixed = bs_call_price(s, k, tau, r, hi_fixed)

    print("\nFixed bracket [1e-6, 10]:")
    print(f"  C(1e-6) = {c_lo_fixed:.8f} (f_lo = {c_lo_fixed - target_price:.6f})")
    print(f"  C(10.0) = {c_hi_fixed:.8f} (f_hi = {c_hi_fixed - target_price:.6f})")
    fixed_brackets_root = (c_lo_fixed - target_price) * (c_hi_fixed - target_price) <= 0.0
    print(f"  Fixed bracket contains root: {fixed_brackets_root} (fails!)")

    # 2. Dynamic bracket expansion
    a = 1.0
    b = 2.0
    fa = bs_call_price(s, k, tau, r, a) - target_price
    fb = bs_call_price(s, k, tau, r, b) - target_price
    expansions = 0

    while fb < 0.0:
        expansions += 1
        a = b
        fa = fb
        b *= 2.0
        fb = bs_call_price(s, k, tau, r, b) - target_price

    print("\nDynamic bracket expansion:")
    print(f"  Expansions performed: {expansions}")
    print(f"  Expanded bracket: [{a:.1f}, {b:.1f}]")
    print(f"  C({a:.1f}) - price = {fa:.6e}")
    print(f"  C({b:.1f}) - price = {fb:.6e}")
    print(f"  Dynamic bracket contains root: {fa * fb <= 0.0}")

    # 3. Safeguarded Newton solve
    sigma = 0.5 * (a + b)
    steps = 0
    for _ in range(50):
        steps += 1
        f = bs_call_price(s, k, tau, r, sigma) - target_price
        if abs(f) <= 1e-12 * s:
            break
        if f < 0.0:
            a = sigma
        else:
            b = sigma
        v = vega(s, k, tau, r, sigma)
        sigma = sigma - f / v if v > 1e-14 and a < sigma - f / v < b else 0.5 * (a + b)

    recomputed = bs_call_price(s, k, tau, r, sigma)
    res = abs(recomputed - target_price)
    print(f"\nSolved implied volatility: {sigma:.8f}")
    print(f"Inversion iterations: {steps}")
    print(f"Recomputed price: {recomputed:.10f}")
    print(f"Residual |C(sigma) - price|: {res:.2e}")
    print(f"Residual <= 1e-12 * S: {res <= 1e-12 * s}")


main()

5Where the canon breaks

  • Weight explosion under poorly chosen proposal distributions. If the proposal density \(q(x)\) has lighter tails than the nominal density \(f(x)\), the likelihood ratio \(w(x) = f(x)/q(x)\) grows exponentially in the tails. In that case, the second moment \(\int \frac{(gf)^2}{q} dx = \infty\) diverges, causing the variance of the importance sampling estimator to become infinite. In empirical simulations, infinite-variance estimators display deceptive pseudo-convergence: sample paths appear stable until a single catastrophic outlier draw causes massive jumps in the running mean, violating the Central Limit Theorem.
  • Ill-conditioning and loss of forward precision near zero vega. When an option is deep out-of-the-money or within minutes of expiry, option vega \(\nu = \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma}\) approaches machine zero. By the condition number formula from Lesson 2, the condition number of implied volatility inversion with respect to option price is \(\kappa = \left| \frac{C}{\sigma \nu} \right| \to \infty\). Even if the solver achieves an exact floating-point residual \(|C(\hat{\sigma}) - C_{\text{market}}| = 0\), the forward error \(|\hat{\sigma} - \sigma^*|\) can be substantial because an entire interval of volatility values produces identical IEEE 754 rounded option prices. Solvers must report conditioning regions explicitly.

6Misconceptions

  1. “Any proposal distribution that increases the number of rare event hits will reduce estimator variance.”

    Shifting the proposal to generate more hits reduces variance only if the likelihood ratio \(w(y)\) remains well-behaved. If the proposal density does not decay appropriately in the tail, large values of \(w(y)\) create enormous variance, resulting in an estimator that is far worse than crude Monte Carlo.

  2. “A fixed search bracket \([0.0001, 10.0]\) is sufficient for all market implied volatilities because volatility never exceeds 1000%.”

    During extreme market panics, or for distressed equities, meme stocks, and near-the-money options undergoing corporate restructurings, implied volatilities can exceed 1500% (\(\sigma > 15\)). More critically, synthetic quotes constructed during model calibration or sensitivity grid scans can legitimately demand \(\sigma > 10\). A production solver must expand brackets dynamically.

  3. “When vega is zero, an implied volatility solver should report an algorithm crash or error.”

    Vanishing vega is a property of the financial derivative's mathematical conditioning, not a bug in the numerical solver. When vega underflows, the solver should satisfy the residual contract via bisection and return the solution tagged as "ill_conditioned", rather than crashing with an unhandled exception.

7Problem set

Mechanics

M1 · Gaussian tail likelihood ratio

For standard Gaussian \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\) and proposal \(Y \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, 1)\) with shift \(\mu = 3\), compute the likelihood ratio \(w(y) = \frac{\phi(y)}{\phi(y - \mu)}\) at the threshold point \(y = 3\).

Solution

By Proposition 2.3, the likelihood ratio is \(w(y) = \exp(-\mu y + \mu^2 / 2)\). Substituting \(\mu = 3\) and \(y = 3\): \(w(3) = \exp(-3 \times 3 + 3^2 / 2) = \exp(-9 + 4.5) = \exp(-4.5) \approx 0.011109\).

M2 · Dynamic bracket doubling count

Starting from an initial upper bracket \(b_0 = 2.0\), how many geometric doublings \(b_{k+1} = 2 b_k\) are required to bracket a true implied volatility \(\sigma^* = 14.5\)?

Solution

The sequence of upper bounds is: \(b_0 = 2.0\); after 1 doubling: \(b_1 = 4.0\); after 2 doublings: \(b_2 = 8.0\); after 3 doublings: \(b_3 = 16.0\). Because \(b_3 = 16.0 \ge 14.5\), exactly 3 doublings are performed to encompass \(\sigma^*\).

M3 · Relative error of crude Monte Carlo

If an event has true probability \(p = 10^{-4}\), what sample size \(n\) is required for crude Monte Carlo to achieve a relative standard error \(\se / p \le 0.05\) (5% relative precision)?

Solution

The relative standard error is \(\frac{\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}}{p} \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{n p}}\). Enforcing \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n p}} \le 0.05 = \frac{1}{20}\) gives \(\sqrt{n p} \ge 20\), so \(n p \ge 400\). For \(p = 10^{-4}\), \(n \ge 400 / 10^{-4} = 4{,}000{,}000\) paths.

Proof

P1 · Likelihood ratio boundedness under mean shift

Prove that for the mean-shifted Gaussian proposal \(Y \sim \mathcal{N}(c, 1)\) applied to the tail event \(\mathbb{I}_{\{Y > c\}}\), the likelihood ratio satisfies \(w(y) \le e^{-c^2 / 2}\) for all \(y \ge c\).

Solution

The likelihood ratio is \(w(y) = \exp(-c y + c^2 / 2)\). Taking the derivative with respect to \(y\): \(\frac{d}{dy} w(y) = -c \exp(-c y + c^2 / 2) < 0\) for \(c > 0\). Since \(w(y)\) is strictly decreasing in \(y\), its maximum on the restricted domain \([c, \infty)\) occurs at the left boundary \(y = c\). Evaluating at \(y = c\): \(w(c) = \exp(-c(c) + c^2 / 2) = \exp(-c^2 + c^2 / 2) = \exp(-c^2 / 2)\). Thus \(w(y) \le \exp(-c^2 / 2)\) for all \(y \ge c\).

P2 · Zero-variance optimal importance sampling

Prove that if \(g(x) > 0\), the importance sampling proposal \(q^*(x) = \frac{g(x) f(x)}{\theta}\) yields an estimator with zero variance.

Solution

Since \(g(x) > 0\) and \(f(x) \ge 0\), \(g(x) f(x) \ge 0\), and \(\int g(x) f(x) dx = \theta > 0\). Thus \(q^*(x) \ge 0\) and integrates to 1, forming a valid probability density. The importance sampling random variable is \(W = g(Y) \frac{f(Y)}{q^*(Y)}\) where \(Y \sim q^*\). Substituting \(q^*\): \(W = g(Y) \frac{f(Y)}{\frac{g(Y) f(Y)}{\theta}} = \theta\). Since \(W\) takes the constant value \(\theta\) on the entire support, its variance is identically zero: \(\Var(W) = \E[(W - \theta)^2] = \E[(\theta - \theta)^2] = 0\).

P3 · Vega positivity and monotonicity of call price

Prove that for all \(S, K, \tau > 0\), Black–Scholes vega \(\nu = \frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} > 0\), and deduce that the call price \(C(\sigma)\) is strictly monotonically increasing in \(\sigma\).

Solution

Differentiating \(C = S \Phi(d_1) - K e^{-r\tau} \Phi(d_2)\) with respect to \(\sigma\): \(\frac{\partial C}{\partial \sigma} = S \phi(d_1) \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} - K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2) \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial \sigma}\). By the Black–Scholes identity \(S \phi(d_1) = K e^{-r\tau} \phi(d_2)\). Because \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\), \(\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial \sigma} - \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial \sigma} = \sqrt{\tau}\). Factoring yields \(\nu = S \sqrt{\tau} \phi(d_1)\). Since \(S > 0\), \(\tau > 0\), and \(\phi(d_1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-d_1^2 / 2} > 0\), vega is strictly positive: \(\nu > 0\). Because the derivative is strictly positive on \((0, \infty)\), \(C(\sigma)\) is strictly monotonically increasing in \(\sigma\), guaranteeing that any root \(C(\sigma) = C_{\text{market}}\) is unique.

E2 · Fixed bracket failure in Lab 2

R1 · Asymptotic optimality of importance sampling

Define what it means for an importance sampling estimator to be asymptotically efficient (or logarithmically efficient) as threshold \(c \to \infty\).

Solution

Let \(p(c) = \mathbb{P}(X > c)\). As \(c \to \infty\), \(p(c) \to 0\) exponentially. An importance sampling estimator \(\hat{p}_n(c)\) based on a family of proposals \(q_c\) is logarithmically efficient if \(\lim_{c \to \infty} \frac{\ln \E_{q_c}[\hat{p}_1(c)^2]}{\ln p(c)} = 2\). Since Jensen's inequality guarantees \(\E[\hat{p}_1^2] \ge (\E[\hat{p}_1])^2 = p(c)^2\), logarithmic efficiency means that the rate of decay of the second moment matches the square of the first moment, ensuring that the number of paths required to achieve a fixed relative standard error grows sub-exponentially in \(c\) (Glasserman 2003, §4.6).

R2 · Conditioning surface of Black–Scholes inversion

Analyze how condition number \(\kappa = \left|\frac{C}{\sigma \nu}\right|\) behaves in the plane of log-moneyness \(m = \ln(S/K)\) and time-to-maturity \(\tau\). Where on this surface is numerical inversion most ill-conditioned?

Solution

Vega is \(\nu = S \sqrt{\tau} \phi(d_1)\). As \(\tau \to 0\), \(\nu\) vanishes as \(\sqrt{\tau}\) for at-the-money options, and vanishes exponentially as \(\exp(-m^2 / (2 \sigma^2 \tau))\) for out-of-the-money options. As \(|m| \to \infty\) (deep ITM or OTM), \(d_1 \to \pm \infty\), so \(\phi(d_1) \to 0\) exponentially. Thus \(\kappa \to \infty\) in two major regimes: (1) short-dated options (\(\tau \to 0\)), and (2) extreme wing strikes (\(|m| \gg 0\)). In these regions, a double-precision floating-point price change of \(10^{-16}\) corresponds to a massive shift in implied volatility, making forward error bounds impossible to enforce without vega filtering.

R3 · Analytical starting guesses (Li & Lee, Jaeckel)

Why do modern quantitative analytics libraries (such as Peter Jaeckel's "By Implication" algorithm) employ rational Chebyshev approximations as starting guesses rather than pure bisection?

Solution

Pure bisection requires 40 to 50 iterations to reach double-precision accuracy (\(2^{-50} \approx 10^{-15}\)). Rational Chebyshev approximations transform price into transformed variables (such as normalized Black price or square root of total variance) where the inverse function is nearly linear or algebraic. Jaeckel constructs rational polynomials that provide initial starting guesses with forward accuracy already better than \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-4}\) across all moneyness and expiry regimes. From this initial guess, two Householder (third-order) or Newton iterations achieve full machine precision (\(10^{-16}\)) in just 2 to 3 function evaluations, achieving high-throughput production pricing.
