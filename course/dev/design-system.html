Put–call parity

This page is the component library's reference implementation. Builders copy its markup, and the browser smoke test checks it on every change. The content is a short, complete lesson written to the program's evidence standard.

Definition and theorem

Definition · European options

A European call with strike \(K\) and maturity \(T\) pays \(\max(S_T - K, 0)\) at \(T\); a European put pays \(\max(K - S_T, 0)\). Here \(S_T\) is the price of the underlying, which pays no dividends, at \(T\), and \(r\) is the continuously compounded risk-free rate.

Theorem · Put–call parity

In a frictionless market without arbitrage, the prices at time \(0\) of a European call \(C\) and put \(P\) with the same strike and maturity satisfy

\[ C - P = S_0 - K e^{-rT}. \]
Proof

At \(T\), a long call and a short put pay \(\max(S_T-K,0) - \max(K-S_T,0) = S_T - K\) in every state. A long share and a short zero-coupon bond with face value \(K\) pay the same \(S_T - K\) in every state. Two portfolios with identical payoffs in every state must have the same price today, or buying the cheaper one and selling the dearer one is an arbitrage. The first portfolio costs \(C - P\); the second costs \(S_0 - K e^{-rT}\). ∎

The theorem needs no model of how \(S_t\) moves, which is why parity holds whatever volatility a model assumes. Any pricing model must satisfy it. For example, the Black–Scholes–Merton prices, derived under geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility and continuous frictionless trading, satisfy parity identically, which the lab below checks numerically.

Lab 1 · Parity in the Black–Scholes model

Hypothesis: the Black–Scholes call and put prices for the same strike and maturity differ by exactly \(S_0 - K e^{-rT}\), up to floating-point rounding. 

from math import erf, exp, log, sqrt


def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
    """Standard normal CDF via the error function."""
    return 0.5 * (1.0 + erf(x / sqrt(2.0)))


def black_scholes(spot: float, strike: float, rate: float, vol: float, years: float) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """European call and put prices (no dividends), continuously compounded rate, annualized vol."""
    d1 = (log(spot / strike) + (rate + 0.5 * vol**2) * years) / (vol * sqrt(years))
    d2 = d1 - vol * sqrt(years)
    call = spot * norm_cdf(d1) - strike * exp(-rate * years) * norm_cdf(d2)
    put = strike * exp(-rate * years) * norm_cdf(-d2) - spot * norm_cdf(-d1)
    return call, put


S, K, r, sigma, T = 100.0, 105.0, 0.03, 0.20, 0.5
call, put = black_scholes(S, K, r, sigma, T)
lhs = call - put
rhs = S - K * exp(-r * T)
print(f"call  {call:.6f}")
print(f"put   {put:.6f}")
print(f"C - P {lhs:.6f}")
print(f"S - K e^(-rT) {rhs:.6f}")
print(f"parity gap {abs(lhs - rhs):.1e}")

call  4.178300
put   7.615053
C - P -3.436754
S - K e^(-rT) -3.436754
parity gap 7.1e-15

Interpretation: the gap is at the level of double-precision rounding. Parity holds because both prices come from one no-arbitrage model. A gap of that size in market quotes is not tradeable once bid–ask spreads and financing costs are counted.

Payoff at maturity

Where the canon breaks

Parity is exact only under its assumptions. American options can be exercised early, so parity becomes a pair of inequalities. Dividends must be subtracted from \(S_0\). Short-sale constraints and funding spreads widen the band in which apparent violations cannot be arbitraged away.

An empirical claim, graded

Time-series momentum, in which an instrument's own past excess return predicts its future return, is documented across equity index, bond, currency and commodity futures, and over more than a century of data. The evidence is contested: one study argues that the pooled regressions mostly reflect differences in average returns across instruments, not genuine time-series predictability. The grade is B, not A, because the main result is published and widely reproduced but its interpretation remains disputed.

Check yourself

A call and a put on a non-dividend stock, both with \(K = 50\) and \(T = 1\), trade at \(C = 6.00\) and \(P = 3.00\). The stock is at \(S_0 = 51.00\) and \(r = 0.04\). Is there an arbitrage, ignoring costs?

Worked solution

Parity requires \(C - P = S_0 - K e^{-rT} = 51 - 50 e^{-0.04} \approx 51 - 48.04 = 2.96\). The market has \(C - P = 3.00\): the call side is \(0.04\) too expensive. Sell the call, buy the put, buy the share, and borrow \(50 e^{-0.04}\). That locks in about \(0.04\) per share today, with a zero net payoff at maturity in every state. Whether it survives bid–ask spreads and borrowing costs is a separate question, and usually the answer is no.
