[definition] Definition 1.1 (heavy-tailed law)< grade=- :: /span> A random variable \(X\) is heavy-tailed when, for every \(c > 0\), \[ \lim_{x \to \infty} e^{cx}\,P(|X| > x) = \infty. \] A law that is not heavy-tailed is called light-tailed in this lesson. Not heavy-tailed means that for some \(c > 0\) the quantity \(e^{cx}P(|X| > x)\) does not tend to infinity. The definition is a statement about the tail probability alone. It does not say whether a moment is finite. Proposition 1.5 checks the two laws used here against it.
[remark] Assumption 1 (density of the Student-t law with three degrees of freedom)< grade=- :: /span> The Student-t law \(V\) with \(\nu = 3\) has the density \(f_V(t) = \dfrac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}(1 + t^2/3)^{-2}\). Lesson 6 defines \(V\) as \(Z/\sqrt{W/3}\) and states its variance (Lesson 6, Proposition 2.6), but it does not state this density. This page takes the density as Assumption 1, and each result that uses it says so.
[remark] Assumption 2 (inverse tangent, used without proof)< grade=- :: /span> The function \(\arctan\) is differentiable on the real line with \(\arctan'(x) = 1/(1 + x^2)\), \(\arctan 0 = 0\), and \(\arctan x \to \pm\pi/2\) as \(x \to \pm\infty\). The page takes these facts as known and proves none of them. The Taylor expansions in Proposition 1.7 follow from the derivative by Taylor's theorem with remainder (M2). Each result that uses this assumption says so in its statement.
[definition] Definition 1.2 (standard Cauchy law and Student-t with three degrees of freedom)< grade=- :: /span> The standard Cauchy law \(C\) has density \(f_C(x) = \dfrac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}\). The Student-t law \(V\) with \(\nu = 3\) degrees of freedom has the density \(f_V\) of Assumption 1, \[f_V(t) = \dfrac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}\,(1 + t^2/3)^{-2}\]. Lesson 6 defines this law as \(V = Z/\sqrt{W/3}\), with \(Z\) standard normal and \(W\) the sum of three squared independent standard normals. The results of this lesson use the density, except Remark 2.2, which cites the variance from Lesson 6, Proposition 2.6 (under Lesson 6's Assumption 1), Proposition 1.5(c), which uses the moment-generating function of Lesson 7, Proposition 1.3, and no density. Proposition 1.3 and its consequences use the densities \(f_C\) and \(f_V\) directly, so they are not exceptions.
[proposition] Proposition 1.3 (distribution functions in closed form)< grade=T :: /span> Assuming Assumption 2, for real \(x\), the distribution function of \(C\) is \(F_C(x) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi \arctan x\), and, assuming Assumptions 1 and 2, the distribution function of \(V\) is \[ F_V(x) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi \Bigl(\arctan\frac{x}{\sqrt3} + \frac{x/\sqrt3}{1 + x^2/3}\Bigr). \]
[proof]  grade=- :: /span> The tangent function has derivative \(1 + \tan^2\theta\) on \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\), so \(\arctan'(x) = 1/(1 + x^2)\) by Assumption 2. Hence \(\frac{d}{dx}F_C(x) = f_C(x)\). Also \(\arctan x \to \pm\pi/2\) as \(x \to \pm\infty\), so \(F_C(-\infty) = 0\) and \(F_C(\infty) = 1\). A distribution function is the integral of its density, so \(F_C\) is the distribution function of \(C\). For \(V\), set \(y = x/\sqrt3\). The derivative of \(\arctan(x/\sqrt3)\) is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + y^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{3}{3 + x^2}\). The derivative of \(\frac{x/\sqrt3}{1 + x^2/3}\) is \[\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{(1 + x^2/3) - x\cdot 2x/3}{(1 + x^2/3)^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{1 - x^2/3}{(1 + x^2/3)^2}\]. Adding these, with \(\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{3}{3 + x^2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + x^2/3}\), the sum is \[\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{(1 + x^2/3) + (1 - x^2/3)}{(1 + x^2/3)^2} = \frac{2}{\sqrt3\,(1 + x^2/3)^2}\]. Multiplying by \(1/\pi\) gives \[\frac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}(1 + x^2/3)^{-2} = f_V(x)\]. As \(x \to \pm\infty\), \(\arctan(x/\sqrt3) \to \pm\pi/2\) and \(\frac{x/\sqrt3}{1 + x^2/3} \to 0\), so \(F_V(\pm\infty) = 1\) and \(0\). \(\blacksquare\)
[proposition] Proposition 1.4 (tails of the two laws)< grade=T :: /span> Assuming Assumption 2, for \(x > 0\), \(P(|C| > x) = \dfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan\dfrac{1}{x}\), and, assuming Assumptions 1 and 2, \(P(|V| > x) =\dfrac{2}{\pi}\Bigl(\arctan\dfrac{\sqrt3}{x} - \dfrac{x\sqrt3}{3 + x^2}\Bigr)\).
[proof]  grade=- :: /span> By symmetry of the density, \[P(|C| > x) = 2P(C > x) = 2(1 - F_C(x)) = 1 - \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan x\]. For \(x > 0\), \(\arctan x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(1/x)\), which gives \[P(|C| > x) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan(1/x)\]. For \(V\), the same symmetry and Proposition 1.3 give \[P(|V| > x) = 2(1 - F_V(x))\]. Write \(y = x/\sqrt3 > 0\). Then \(\arctan y = \tfrac{\pi}{2} - \arctan(1/y)\), and \[1 - F_V(x) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac1\pi\bigl(\arctan y + \tfrac{y}{1 + y^2}\bigr) = \tfrac1\pi\bigl(\arctan(\sqrt3/x) - \tfrac{y}{1 + y^2}\bigr)\]. Since \(\tfrac{y}{1 + y^2} = \tfrac{x/\sqrt3}{1 + x^2/3} = \tfrac{x\sqrt3}{3 + x^2}\), the second formula follows with the factor 2. \(\blacksquare\)
[proposition] Proposition 1.5 (the Cauchy and Student-t(3) laws are heavy-tailed; the normal law is light-tailed)< grade=T :: /span> (a) Assuming Assumption 2, \(C\) is heavy-tailed. (b) Assuming Assumption 1, \(V\) is heavy-tailed. (c) Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1 and Lesson 7's Assumption 1 (through Lesson 7, Proposition 1.3), a standard normal \(Z\) is light-tailed: \[P(|Z| > x) \le 2e^{-x^2/2}\] for \(x > 0\), so \(e^{cx}P(|Z| > x) \to 0\) for every \(c > 0\).
[proof]  grade=- :: /span> Two elementary inequalities. For \(u \ge 0\), \(h(u) = \arctan u - u + u^3/3\) has \(h(0) = 0\) and \[h'(u) = \frac{1}{1 + u^2} - 1 + u^2 = \frac{u^4}{1 + u^2} \ge 0\], so \(\arctan u \ge u - u^3/3\), where the derivative of \(\arctan\) is the one in Assumption 2. Also \[e^{cx} \ge (cx)^2/2\] for \(x \ge 0\), by the first terms of the exponential series, so \(e^{cx}/x \ge c^2 x/2 \to \infty\). (a) For \(x \ge 1\), with \(u = 1/x\), Proposition 1.4 and the first inequality give \(P(|C| > x) \ge \tfrac{2}{\pi}\bigl(\tfrac1x - \tfrac{1}{3x^3}\bigr) \ge \tfrac{1}{\pi x}\) (since \(\tfrac1{3x^3} \le \tfrac{1}{2x}\) for \(x \ge 1\)). Hence \(e^{cx}P(|C| > x) \ge e^{cx}/(\pi x) \to \infty\). (b) For \(t^2 \ge 3\), \(1 + t^2/3 \le 2t^2/3\), so \(f_V(t) \ge \frac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{9}{4t^4}\). Integrating over \([x, \infty)\) for \(x \ge \sqrt3\) gives \[P(V > x) \ge \frac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{9}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{3x^3} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2\pi x^3}\], and by symmetry \(P(|V| > x) \ge \frac{\sqrt3}{\pi x^3}\). Then \(e^{cx}P(|V| > x) \ge e^{cx}\,\frac{\sqrt3}{\pi x^3} \to \infty\), because \[e^{cx}/x^3 \ge (cx)^4/(24 x^3) = c^4 x/24 \to \infty\]. (c) For \(s > 0\), \(e^{sx}\mathbf{1}\{Z > x\} \le e^{sZ}\) pointwise, because when \(Z \le x\) the left side is 0 and \(e^{sZ} \ge 0\), and when \(Z > x\) we have \(sZ > sx\) (since \(s > 0\)), so \(e^{sZ} > e^{sx}\) because the exponential function is increasing. Taking expectations, \[P(Z > x) \le e^{-sx}E[e^{sZ}]\]. Lesson 7, Proposition 1.3 gives \(E[e^{sZ}] = e^{s^2/2}\). Choose \(s = x\) to get \[P(Z > x) \le e^{-x^2/2}\]. By symmetry, \[P(|Z| > x) \le 2e^{-x^2/2}\], and \(e^{cx}\cdot 2e^{-x^2/2} \to 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
[remark] Remark 1.6 (integrals in the proofs)< grade=- :: /span> For a law with a density, the expectation is the integral of \(x\) times the density, a definite integral (M2). The integrals in Propositions 1.3, 1.4, 1.5(b), 2.1 and 2.3 are of this kind. Proposition 1.5(c) uses no density: it takes \(E[e^{sZ}] = e^{s^2/2}\) from Lesson 7, Proposition 1.3. Lesson 9's finite sums do not cover them, and this lesson does not extend Lesson 9's theorems. Proposition 1.5 says that the Cauchy law is heavy-tailed in the sense of Definition 1.1, that the Student-t(3) law is heavy-tailed too (assuming Assumption 1), and that the normal law is not. The next proposition gives the limits of the scaled tails, which the lab prints.
[proposition] Proposition 1.7 (the scaled tails)< grade=T :: /span> Assuming Assumption 2, as \(x \to \infty\), \(x\,P(|C| > x) \to \tfrac{2}{\pi}\), and, assuming Assumptions 1 and 2, \(x^3\,P(|V| > x) \to \tfrac{4\sqrt3}{\pi}\).
[proof]  grade=- :: /span> For the Cauchy law, \(xP(|C| > x) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\arctan u}{u}\) with \(u = 1/x \to 0\), and \(\arctan u / u \to 1\) because \(\arctan'(0) = 1\), which is Assumption 2. For \(V\), let \[y = \sqrt3/x \to 0\]. By Proposition 1.4, \[P(|V| > x) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\bigl(\arctan y - \tfrac{y}{1 + y^2}\bigr)\], since \(\tfrac{x\sqrt3}{3 + x^2} = \tfrac{y}{1 + y^2}\) for \(y = \sqrt3/x\). Taylor's theorem with remainder (M2) gives \[\arctan y = y - y^3/3 + O(y^5)\] and \[\tfrac{y}{1 + y^2} = y - y^3 + O(y^5)\]. The difference is \(\tfrac{2}{3}y^3 + O(y^5)\), so \(P(|V| > x) = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac23 y^3 + O(y^5)\). With \(y^3 = 3\sqrt3/x^3\), this gives \[P(|V| > x) = \tfrac{4}{3\pi}\cdot 3\sqrt3\,x^{-3} + O(x^{-5}) = \tfrac{4\sqrt3}{\pi}x^{-3} + O(x^{-5})\]. Multiply by \(x^3\) and let \(x \to \infty\). \(\blacksquare\) 2Moments, and infinite variance
[proposition] Proposition 2.1 (the standard Cauchy law has no mean and no variance)< grade=T :: /span> For the standard Cauchy law \(C\), \(E|C| = \infty\) and \(E[C^2] = \infty\). Hence \(E[C]\) is not defined, and \(\operatorname{Var}(C)\) is not finite.
[proof]  grade=- :: /span> Since \(f_C\) is even, \[E|C| = 2\int_0^\infty x\,f_C(x)\,dx \ge 2\int_1^\infty \frac{x}{\pi(1 + x^2)}\,dx\], because the integrand \(x f_C(x)\) is nonnegative on \([0, \infty)\), so the integral over \([1, \infty)\) cannot exceed the integral over \([0, \infty)\). Substitute \(v = 1 + x^2\), so \(dv = 2x\,dx\): the integral is \[\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_2^\infty \frac{dv}{v} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\lim_{M \to \infty}(\ln M - \ln 2) = \infty\], so \(E|C| = \infty\). For the second moment, \(x^2 \ge |x|\) when \(|x| \ge 1\), so \[E[C^2] \ge \int_{|x| \ge 1} |x|\,f_C(x)\,dx = E|C| - \int_{|x| < 1}|x|\,f_C(x)\,dx\]. The subtracted integral is at most \(2/\pi\), because \(|x| f_C(x) \le 1/\pi\) on \([-1, 1]\) and that interval has length 2. Since \(E|C| = \infty\), the right side is \(\infty\). \(\blacksquare\)
[remark] Remark 2.2 (the Student-t(3) variance)< grade=- :: /span> Lesson 6 (Proposition 2.6, under that lesson's Assumption 1) gives \(\operatorname{Var}(V) = \nu/(\nu - 2)\) for \(\nu > 2\). With \(\nu = 3\) this is \(3\). So the Student-t(3) law has a finite variance, while, assuming Assumption 1, Proposition 1.5(b) shows it is heavy-tailed. Heavy-tailed and infinite-variance are different properties, and this lesson does not claim that the two coincide. The variance formula is cited from Lesson 6 and not derived here.
[proposition] Proposition 2.3 (the fourth moment of the Student-t(3) law is infinite)< grade=T :: /span> Assuming Assumption 1, for the Student-t law \(V\) with \(\nu = 3\), \(E[V^4] = \infty\). Hence the kurtosis of Lesson 3 is not defined for \(V\).
[proof]  grade=- :: /span> For \(t^2 \ge 3\), the density bound from the proof of Proposition 1.5(b) gives \(f_V(t) \ge \frac{2}{\pi\sqrt3}\cdot\frac{9}{4t^4} = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi t^4}\). Since \(f_V\) is even, \[E[V^4] = 2\int_0^\infty t^4 f_V(t)\,dt \ge 2\int_{\sqrt3}^\infty t^4 \cdot \frac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi t^4}\,dt = \frac{3\sqrt3}{\pi}\int_{\sqrt3}^\infty dt = \infty\]. On \([\sqrt3, \infty)\) the integrand \(t^4 f_V(t)\) is at least the constant \(3\sqrt3/(2\pi)\), so the integral diverges. \(\blacksquare\)
[definition] Definition 2.4 (failure of the CLT under infinite variance)< grade=- :: /span> The failure of the CLT under infinite variance is the situation in which the variance of \(X\) is not finite, so that the finite-variance hypothesis of Lesson 9's central limit theorem fails. The standardization \((\bar X_n - \mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)\) of Lesson 9 then has no finite \(\sigma\) to use, and the normal limit of Lesson 9, Definition 2.4, is not established for that standardization. Proposition 2.1 gives a law where this holds. This page proves only that the finite-variance hypothesis fails for \(C\); it does not prove that the normal limit fails. Assumption 3, stated below, gives a claim about the sample mean of \(C\), without proof.
[remark] Assumption 3 (stable limit of sample means under infinite variance, used without proof)< grade=- :: /span> For the standard Cauchy law, the sample mean of \(n\) independent copies is again standard Cauchy for every \(n\). A stable limit law of this kind would be the candidate limit for the sample mean under infinite variance. This page does not prove the statement, cites no source for it, and gives it no grade. Lab 1, Part C, prints sample quantities for the Cauchy sample mean, and the lab does not establish it. No source for this statement is identified on this page. Theorem 1.3 and Proposition 2.3 of Lesson 9 are proved for finitely valued laws, and neither \(C\) nor \(V\) is finitely valued. So this lesson does not apply those two results to either law. Proposition 1.2 of Lesson 9 needs no finitely-valued hypothesis, and Problem E3 applies it to \(V\). The statements about \(V\) above use Assumption 1, the tails of Proposition 1.5, and, for the closed forms, Assumption 2; the variance of \(V\) is cited from Lesson 6 (Remark 2.2). The Cauchy law does not satisfy the hypotheses of the weak law of Lesson 9, because it has no mean (Proposition 2.1), so Theorem 1.3 of Lesson 9 does not apply to it.
