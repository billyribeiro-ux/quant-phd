[problem M1] M1 · The Chebyshev bound for the die at \(n = 100\) For the fair die, with \(\sigma^2 = 35/12\), find the Chebyshev bound on \(P(|\bar X_{100} - 3.5| \ge 0.5)\). Solution By Theorem 1.3 the bound is \[\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2) = (35/12)/(100 \cdot 0.25) = (35/12)/25 = 35/300 = 7/60\], which is about 0.1167. This is the value that Lab 1 prints at \(n = 100\), to four decimals, as 0.1167.
[problem M2] M2 · The variance of the sample mean of four dice For four independent fair dice, find the variance of their sample mean. Solution By Proposition 1.2 with \(n = 4\) and \(\sigma^2 = 35/12\), \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_4) = (35/12)/4 = 35/48\). This is about 0.729.
[problem M3] M3 · A two-value law at \(n = 2\) Let \(X\) take the values 0 and 2 with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, and let \(X_1, X_2\) be i.i.d. copies. Compute \(P(|\bar X_2 - 1| \ge \tfrac12)\) exactly, and compare it with the bound of Theorem 1.3. Solution Here \(\mu = 1\) and \(\sigma^2 = 1\). The sample mean \(\bar X_2\) takes the value 0 with probability \(\tfrac14\), 1 with probability \(\tfrac12\), and 2 with probability \(\tfrac14\). The event \(|\bar X_2 - 1| \ge \tfrac12\) is \(\bar X_2 \in \{0, 2\}\), with probability \(\tfrac12\). The bound is \(\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2) = 1/(2 \cdot \tfrac14) = 2\), which is greater than 1 and so says nothing here, while the exact probability is \(\tfrac12\). Proof
[problem P1] P1 · Chebyshev's inequality on a finite law Prove Proposition 1.1 without citing any other result of this lesson. Solution Let \(W = (Y - m)^2 \ge 0\). For each outcome, \(a^2\mathbf{1}\{|Y - m| \ge a\} \le W\): if \(|Y - m| \ge a\), then \(W = (Y-m)^2 \ge a^2\), and otherwise the left side is 0 and \(W \ge 0\). Taking expectations, which preserves inequalities between finite sums, gives \[a^2 P(|Y - m| \ge a) \le E[W] = v\]. Dividing by \(a^2 > 0\) gives the bound.
[problem P2] P2 · The product rule for two independent variables Prove \(E[g(X)h(Y)] = E[g(X)]E[h(Y)]\) for independent \(X\) and \(Y\) with finitely many values, and complex-valued \(g\) and \(h\). Solution Independence means \[P(X = x, Y = y) = P(X = x)P(Y = y)\]. Then \[E[g(X)h(Y)] = \sum_{x,y} g(x)h(y)P(X=x)P(Y=y) = \bigl(\sum_x g(x)P(X=x)\bigr)\bigl(\sum_y h(y)P(Y=y)\bigr)\], by the distributive law, which is the product of the two expectations.
[problem P3] P3 · The telescoping bound For complex numbers \(a\) and \(b\) with \(|a|, |b| \le 1\), show that \(|a^n - b^n| \le n|a - b|\). Solution The identity \[a^n - b^n = (a - b)\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} a^k b^{n-1-k}\] holds by expanding the sum, which telescopes. Each term has modulus at most 1, since \(|a^k| \le 1\) and \(|b^{n-1-k}| \le 1\). Taking moduli, \[|a^n - b^n| \le |a - b|\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} 1 = n|a - b|\]. Empirical

[problem E2] E2 · The standard error of a simulated estimate (Lab 1) Lab 1, Part C, prints an estimate 0.8552 for \(n = 5\) with standard error 0.0056. Verify the standard error, and state what it does and does not say about the gap to \(\Phi(1)\). Solution The lab's standard error is \(\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/4000}\), computed here from the printed 4-decimal estimate \(\hat p = 0.8552\). That is \[\sqrt{0.8552 \cdot 0.1448/4000} = \sqrt{3.096 \times 10^{-5}} = 0.00556\], printed as 0.0056. The gap of \[0.8552 - 0.841345 = 0.0139\] is \(0.0139 / 0.0056 = 2.48\) printed standard errors, which is 2.5 to one decimal (a ratio of printed values). The unrounded estimate of this run is \(\hat p = 3421/4000 = 0.85525\), printed as 0.8552, so the gap is \(0.85525 - 0.841345 = 0.013905\) and the standard error is \(0.005563\), and the ratio is \(2.50\). This is a statement about this run only. The standard error does not cover the choice of seed, and the three sizes share one seed, so the three gaps are not independent evidence.

[problem R1] R1 · Second moments are not enough Proposition 2.3(a) gives \(E[Z_n^2] = 1\) for every \(n\). Explain why this does not establish the central limit theorem, and identify which step of Proposition 2.3(b) uses more than the second moment. Solution A statement about \(E[Z_n^2]\) fixes the scale of \(Z_n\) for each \(n\), but it does not determine the law of \(Z_n\), so it cannot give \(P(Z_n \le z) \to \Phi(z)\) for every \(z\). Two laws with the same variance can have different probabilities of exceeding a given level. The step that uses more than the second moment is the expansion of Proposition 2.2, which uses the absolute third moment \(E|Y|^3\), and the limit that follows from it. In the expansion, the second moment enters through the coefficient \(-s^2/2\) and the normalization \(E[Y^2] = 1\) of Proposition 2.2.
[problem R2] R2 · Why the distribution-function limit is not established here Explain why Proposition 2.3(b) does not by itself give the distribution-function limit of Definition 2.4, and which tools this lesson does not state. Solution Every law of \(Z_n\) is finitely valued, so the distribution function of \(Z_n\) is a step function, while the target \(\Phi\) is continuous (Lesson 6, by its density). The lesson proves a limit of characteristic functions only. It states no result that converts such a limit into a limit of distribution functions, and it does not identify \(e^{-t^2/2}\) with a characteristic function of a normal law.
[problem R3] R3 · The limit in Proposition 2.3(b) Assume Assumption 1. Deduce \((1 - x/n)^n \to e^{-x}\) for fixed real \(x \ne 0\) (the case \(x = 0\) is trivial), and say which step uses the assumption. Solution Assumption 1 gives \(\ln(1 + u)/u \to 1\) as \(u \to 0\), and it is not derived here. For fixed \(x\), \(u = -x/n \to 0\), and \(-x/u = n\), so \[n\ln(1 - x/n) = -x \cdot \ln(1 + u)/u \to -x\], a product of two limits. The continuity of \(\exp\), the other part of Assumption 1, then gives \[(1 - x/n)^n = \exp(n\ln(1 - x/n)) \to e^{-x}\]. Both steps use the assumption.
