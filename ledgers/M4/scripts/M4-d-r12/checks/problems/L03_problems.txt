[problem mech-1] M1 · Bernoulli with \(p = \tfrac14\) For the Bernoulli law with \(p = \tfrac14\), find \(\sigma\), \(\gamma_3\) and \(\gamma_4\) in exact form. Solution Here \(q = \tfrac34\), so \(pq = \tfrac{3}{16}\) and \(\sigma = \sqrt{3}/4\). By Example 2.6, \(\gamma_3 = (1 - 2p)/\sqrt{pq} = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{4}{\sqrt 3} = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt 3}\), which is about 1.155. The kurtosis is \(\gamma_4 = (1 - 3pq)/(pq) = (1 - \tfrac{9}{16}) \cdot \tfrac{16}{3} = \tfrac{7}{16} \cdot \tfrac{16}{3} = \tfrac73\).
[problem mech-2] M2 · A symmetric two-point law with mean 1 Let \(X\) take the values 0 and 2 with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. Find \(\mu\), \(\gamma_3\) and \(\gamma_4\). Solution The mean is \(\mu = 1\). Then \(X - \mu\) is \(\pm 1\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, so \(m_2 = 1\), \(m_3 = \tfrac12(1) + \tfrac12(-1) = 0\), and \(m_4 = 1\). So \(\sigma = 1\), \(\gamma_3 = 0\), and \(\gamma_4 = m_4/m_2^2 = 1\). This is the equality case of Proposition 2.5.
[problem mech-3] M3 · The third moment of the skewed law For the law with values 0, 1, 2, 10 and probabilities \(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18\), verify that \(m_3 = \tfrac{2157}{32}\) and state the sign of \(\gamma_3\). Solution The mean is \(\mu = \tfrac74\). The four terms of \(m_3 = \sum (x - \mu)^3 p(x)\) are \(-\tfrac{343}{64} \cdot \tfrac12 = -\tfrac{1372}{512}\), \(-\tfrac{27}{64} \cdot \tfrac14 = -\tfrac{54}{512}\), \(\tfrac{1}{64} \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac{1}{512}\), and \(\tfrac{35937}{64} \cdot \tfrac18 = \tfrac{35937}{512}\). Their sum is \(\tfrac{34512}{512} = \tfrac{2157}{32}\). Since \(m_3 > 0\) and \(\sigma > 0\), \(\gamma_3 = m_3/\sigma^3 > 0\) by Definition 2.2 and Proposition 2.3. Proof
[problem proof-1] P1 · Affine invariance of kurtosis Prove Proposition 2.4 for \(X\) taking at least two values on a finite probability space with positive weights, and \(Y = aX + b\) with \(a \ne 0\), using only Definition 2.1 and the properties of expectation. Solution \(E[Y] = a\mu + b\), so \(Y - E[Y] = a(X - \mu)\). The variance of \(Y\) is \(E[a^2 (X - \mu)^2] = a^2 \sigma^2\), so \(\sigma_Y = |a|\sigma\). Then \(Z_Y = a(X - \mu)/(|a|\sigma) = \operatorname{sign}(a) Z_X\). Fourth powers remove the sign, so \(E[Z_Y^4] = E[Z_X^4]\), which gives \(\gamma_{4,Y} = \gamma_{4,X}\). The third power keeps the sign, so \(\gamma_{3,Y} = \operatorname{sign}(a) \gamma_{3,X}\).
[problem proof-2] P2 · The second standardized moment is one Prove that \(E[Z^2] = 1\) for the standardized variable of Definition 2.1, without using Proposition 2.3 as a black box. Solution \(Z^2 = (X - \mu)^2 / \sigma^2\). Since \(\sigma^2 = E[(X - \mu)^2]\) by Lesson 2, Definition 2.1, the expectation of \(Z^2\) is \(E[(X - \mu)^2]/\sigma^2 = \sigma^2/\sigma^2 = 1\). The step pulls the constant \(\sigma^{-2}\) out of the expectation, which is linearity (Lesson 2, Proposition 1.3).
[problem proof-3] P3 · The fourth moment of the Bernoulli law Verify the identity \(q^3 + p^3 = 1 - 3pq\) for \(q = 1 - p\), and use it to confirm \(m_4 = pq(1 - 3pq)\) in Example 2.6. Solution Since \(q + p = 1\), \(q^3 + p^3 = (q + p)(q^2 - qp + p^2) = q^2 - qp + p^2\). Also \(q^2 + 2qp + p^2 = (q + p)^2 = 1\), so \(q^2 + p^2 = 1 - 2qp\), and \(q^2 - qp + p^2 = 1 - 2qp - qp = 1 - 3pq\). With \(q^3 + p^3 = 1 - 3pq\), the expression \(m_4 = p\,q^4 + q\,p^4 = pq(q^3 + p^3) = pq(1 - 3pq)\) follows. Empirical

[problem emp-2] E2 · Checking the Bernoulli closed forms (Lab 1) Lab 1 prints two Bernoulli lines, one for the squared skewness and one for the kurtosis, and each ends in True. Verify both printed values from Example 2.6. Solution For \(p = \tfrac1{10}\), \(pq = \tfrac{9}{100}\). The squared skewness is \((1 - 2p)^2/(pq) = (\tfrac{4}{5})^2 / \tfrac{9}{100} = \tfrac{16}{25} \cdot \tfrac{100}{9} = \tfrac{64}{9}\). The kurtosis is \((1 - 3pq)/(pq) = \tfrac{73}{100} \cdot \tfrac{100}{9} = \tfrac{73}{9}\). Both match the printed values, and the True lines show that the moment definitions give the same fractions.
[problem emp-3] E3 · Why the sampler uses integer weights (Lab 1) Lab 1 draws with weights 4, 2, 1 and 1. Explain why these weights give the probabilities \(\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18\), and say whether the sample proportions must equal these probabilities exactly. Solution The weights total 8, and \(4/8 = \tfrac12\), \(2/8 = \tfrac14\), \(1/8 = \tfrac18\). So the weights are the probabilities up to the common factor 8, and the sampler selects each value with its probability. The sample proportions are random and need not equal the probabilities exactly. The printed sample moments are computed from the draws, so they inherit that randomness. Research
[problem res-1] R1 · When is kurtosis exactly one Using Proposition 2.5, find every finite law with \(\gamma_4 = 1\), and show that a law with the same mean and variance but a different two-point structure has \(\gamma_4 > 1\) unless it is the symmetric two-point law. Identify the law in the equality case. Solution Proposition 2.5 says \(\gamma_4 = 1\) exactly when \(X\) takes two values with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. Those values are \(\mu - \sigma\) and \(\mu + \sigma\), since \(Z = \pm 1\) gives \(X = \mu \pm \sigma\). Any other finite law, with three or more values or with two values of unequal probability, has \(\operatorname{Var}(Z^2) > 0\) by Lesson 2, Proposition 2.4 applied to \(W = Z^2\). The reason is that \(W\) is constant only when \(Z\) takes two values \(\pm c\), and then \(E[Z] = 0\) forces probability \(\tfrac12\) on each, which is the symmetric two-point law. So its kurtosis is strictly greater than 1. The equality case is therefore the symmetric two-point law at \(\mu \pm \sigma\), and it is the only finite law with at least two values and kurtosis 1.
[problem res-2] R2 · Why a single kurtosis is not a tail statement A report states that the kurtosis of returns is 8, so the returns are fat-tailed. Using Example 2.6, explain why the number 8 alone does not identify the tail, and state what additional quantity the claim would need. Solution The Bernoulli law with \(p = \tfrac1{10}\) has kurtosis \(\tfrac{73}{9}\), which is about 8.11, and it has only two values, so it has no tail beyond them. A kurtosis of about 8 is therefore compatible with a law that has no tail beyond its values: the Bernoulli law above has kurtosis about 8.11 and no tail. The word fat-tailed is a statement about how the probability of large values decays, and it needs the tail probabilities themselves, for example \(P(|X - \mu| > t)\) for a range of \(t\). This lesson does not compute those, so it cannot support the word.
[problem res-3] R3 · Symmetric about the mean implies zero skewness Let \(X\) take the values \(\mu - d\) and \(\mu + d\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, with \(d > 0\). Show \(\gamma_3 = 0\), and explain why this does not prove the converse. Solution \(X - \mu\) is \(\pm d\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each, so \(m_3 = \tfrac12 d^3 + \tfrac12 (-d)^3 = 0\), and \(\gamma_3 = 0\). For the converse, the law of §4 has \(\gamma_3 = 0\) but is not symmetric, so a zero skewness alone does not force symmetry. The implication runs one way: symmetry about the mean gives zero third central moment, and zero third central moment does not give symmetry.
