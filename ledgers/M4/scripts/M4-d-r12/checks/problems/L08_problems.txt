[problem M1] M1 · The covariance of a second factor Compute \(AA^{\top}\) for \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Solution The first row of \(A\) is \((1, 1)\), and its inner products with itself and with the second row \((0, 2)\) are \(1 + 1 = 2\) and \(0 + 2 = 2\). The second row has inner product \(0 + 4 = 4\) with itself. So \(AA^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\), which is symmetric, as Proposition 1.6 requires.
[problem M2] M2 · A diagonal factor Let \(X = \mu + AZ\) with \(\mu = (0, 5)\) and \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). State \(E[X]\) and the covariance matrix of \(X\). Solution By Proposition 1.3, \(E[X] = \mu = (0, 5)\). The covariance is \(AA^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 9 \end{pmatrix}\), since \(A\) is diagonal, so \(AA^{\top} = A^{\top}A = A^2\), and the diagonal entries are \(1^2 = 1\) and \(3^2 = 9\). The coordinates are uncorrelated, with variances 1 and 9.
[problem M3] M3 · Checking the rotation Verify that \(Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) satisfies \(QQ^{\top} = I\). Solution \(Q^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\). The product \(QQ^{\top}\) has entries \((0)(0) + (-1)(-1) = 1\) in position \((1,1)\), \((0)(1) + (-1)(0) = 0\) in position \((1,2)\), \((1)(0) + (0)(-1) = 0\) in position \((2,1)\), and \((1)(1) + (0)(0) = 1\) in position \((2,2)\). So \(QQ^{\top} = I\). Proof
[problem P1] P1 · The mean of a linear image Prove that \(E[\mu + AZ] = \mu\) for a standard normal vector \(Z\), stating each property of expectation that you use. Solution Each coordinate of \(AZ\) is \(\sum_i A_{ri} Z_i\), a finite sum of constant multiples of the coordinates. Linearity of expectation for finite sums (Lesson 2) gives \(E[\sum_i A_{ri} Z_i] = \sum_i A_{ri} E[Z_i]\), and each \(E[Z_i] = 0\) by Lesson 6. So \(E[AZ] = 0\), and adding the constant vector \(\mu\) gives \(E[\mu + AZ] = \mu + E[AZ] = \mu\), since the expectation of a constant is that constant.
[problem P2] P2 · Symmetry of AA transposed Prove that \(AA^{\top}\) is symmetric for every real matrix \(A\), using only the rule for the transpose of a product. Solution The transpose of a product reverses the order of the factors, \((XY)^{\top} = Y^{\top}X^{\top}\). Applying this with \(X = A\) and \(Y = A^{\top}\) gives \[(AA^{\top})^{\top} = (A^{\top})^{\top}A^{\top} = AA^{\top}\], because the transpose of a transpose is the original matrix. So \(AA^{\top}\) equals its own transpose.
[problem P3] P3 · Nonnegativity of the quadratic form Show that \(w^{\top}AA^{\top}w \ge 0\) for every \(w\), and identify the case of equality for the factor of Example 1.5. Solution Write \(u = A^{\top}w\). Then \[w^{\top}AA^{\top}w = (A^{\top}w)^{\top}(A^{\top}w) = u^{\top}u = \sum_j u_j^2 \ge 0\]. For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), \(A^{\top}w = (2w_1 + w_2, w_2)\), so the form is \((2w_1 + w_2)^2 + w_2^2\), which is zero only when \(w_2 = 0\) and \(2w_1 = 0\). That is \(w = 0\), so the form is positive definite, as stated in Section 1. Empirical
[problem E1] E1 · The entry gaps of the X sample (Lab 1) Lab 1 prints the sample covariance of \(X\) as \(((4.0073, 2.0187), (2.0187, 1.9936))\). Compute the four entry gaps from \(\Sigma\), and check that the largest is the 0.0187 that the lab prints. Solution The gaps are \(|4.0073 - 4| = 0.0073\) for entry \((1,1)\), \(|2.0187 - 2| = 0.0187\) for entries \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\), and \(|1.9936 - 2| = 0.0064\) for entry \((2,2)\). The gaps are differences of the printed 4-decimal entries, so each is accurate to about 0.0001. The largest is 0.0187, which matches the lab's printed largest entry gap. The largest gap sits in the off-diagonal entries, and the lab prints only the largest value, not the entry where it occurs.
[problem E2] E2 · The gap from the wrong matrix (Lab 1) Lab 1 prints a largest entry gap of 1.0187 between the sample covariance of \(X\) and \(A^{\top}A\). Compute the exact gap between \(A^{\top}A\) and \(\Sigma\), and explain how the sample gap of 1.0187 arises. Solution \(A^{\top}A - \Sigma\) has entries \(5 - 4 = 1\), \[1 - 2 = -1\], \[1 - 2 = -1\] and \[1 - 2 = -1\], so the exact largest entry gap between \(A^{\top}A\) and \(\Sigma\) is 1. In entry \((1,2)\), the target \(\Sigma_{12} = 2\) lies 1 above \(A^{\top}A_{12} = 1\), and the sample value 2.0187 lies a further 0.0187 above \(\Sigma_{12}\). The sample gap from \(A^{\top}A\) in that entry is therefore \(2.0187 - 1 = 1.0187\), which is the printed value. It is the exact gap of 1 plus the sample error of 0.0187.
[problem E3] E3 · Two samples from the same draws (Lab 1) Lab 1 forms \(X = \mu + AZ\) and \(Y = \mu + BZ\) from the same draws, and reports largest entry gaps of 0.0187 for \(X\) and 0.0930 for \(Y\). State what these two numbers do and do not show. Solution Both numbers are gaps from the same exact matrix \(\Sigma\), since \(BB^{\top} = \Sigma\) by Part A. They show that, in this one run, every entry of the sample covariance matrix of \(X\) lies within 0.0187 of the matching entry of \(\Sigma\), and every entry of that of \(Y\) lies within 0.0930 of it. They do not show which sample is closer in general, because the draws \(Z\) are the same but \(X\) and \(Y\) are different linear images of them, and one run cannot separate the factor's effect from the sampling error. The gap of 0.0930 is the largest entry gap printed for \(Y\), in entry \((1,1)\), where the sample value 3.9070 lies 0.0930 below 4. Research
[problem R1] R1 · A lower-triangular factor is unique for this \(\Sigma\) Show that the only lower-triangular matrix \(L = \begin{pmatrix} a & 0 \\ b & c \end{pmatrix}\) with \(a > 0\), \(c > 0\) and \(LL^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) is \(A\) of Example 1.5. Solution \(LL^{\top} = \begin{pmatrix} a^2 & ab \\ ab & b^2 + c^2 \end{pmatrix}\). Matching entries gives \(a^2 = 4\), so \(a = 2\) because \(a > 0\). Then \(ab = 2\) gives \(b = 1\), and \(b^2 + c^2 = 2\) gives \(c^2 = 1\), so \(c = 1\) because \(c > 0\). Hence \(L = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = A\). The second factor \(B\) of Example 1.5 is not lower-triangular, since its \((1,2)\) entry is \(-2\), so this result does not contradict Remark 1.4. It shows that uniqueness holds within the class of lower-triangular factors with positive diagonal, and not for all factors.
[problem R2] R2 · Two factors, one law of the mean Let \(X = \mu + AZ\) and \(Y = \mu + BZ\) with \(B = AQ\) and \(QQ^{\top} = I\). Show that \(E[X] = E[Y]\) and that both covariance matrices equal \(\Sigma\). Explain what this does not show about the laws of \(X\) and \(Y\). Solution Assuming Lesson 6's Assumption 1, Proposition 1.3 applied to each gives \(E[X] = E[Y] = \mu\), and covariances \(AA^{\top}\) and \(BB^{\top} = AQQ^{\top}A^{\top} = AA^{\top}\), both equal to \(\Sigma\). This shows that the first two moments agree. It does not show that \(X\) and \(Y\) have the same joint law, because the proof uses only the mean and the covariance, and Definition 1.2 asks for equality of laws. The page does not prove that the law depends on \(A\) only through \(AA^{\top}\).
[problem R3] R3 · The probability in the normal-margins example In Section 4, show that \(X\) and \(-X\) have the same law and deduce \(P(Y \le y) = P(X \le y)\) for \(Y = \varepsilon X\). Solution The standard normal density of Lesson 6 is an even function of \(x\), so \(X\) and \(-X\) have the same law. Therefore \(P(-X \le y) = P(X \ge -y)\), and by the same evenness \(P(X \ge -y) = P(X \le y)\). Next, \(Y = \varepsilon X\) equals \(X\) when \(\varepsilon = 1\) and \(-X\) when \(\varepsilon = -1\). Since \(\varepsilon\) is independent of \(X\), each sign has probability \(\tfrac12\) and is independent of \(X\), so \[P(Y \le y) = \tfrac12 P(X \le y) + \tfrac12 P(-X \le y) = P(X \le y)\]. The independence used here is the one of Lesson 4.
