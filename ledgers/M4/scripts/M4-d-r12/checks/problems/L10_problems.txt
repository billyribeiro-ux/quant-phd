[problem M1] M1 · The Cauchy tail at one Find \(P(|C| > 1)\) exactly. Solution By Proposition 1.4 with \(x = 1\), under Assumption 2, \(P(|C| > 1) =\tfrac{2}{\pi}\arctan 1 = \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac12\). This is the exact value, and it agrees with the symmetry of \(C\) about 0 and with \(F_C(1) = \tfrac34\).
[problem M2] M2 · The Student-t(3) distribution function at \(\sqrt3\) Assuming Assumptions 1 and 2, evaluate \(F_V(\sqrt3)\) exactly. Solution Here \(x/\sqrt3 = 1\) and \(x^2/3 = 1\). So \[F_V(\sqrt3) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi(\arctan 1 + \tfrac{1}{2}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac1\pi(\tfrac\pi4 + \tfrac12) = \tfrac34 + \tfrac{1}{2\pi}\], which is about 0.9092.
[problem M3] M3 · The two-sided Student-t(3) tail at zero Assuming Assumptions 1 and 2, use Proposition 1.4 to find \(\lim_{x \to 0^+} P(|V| > x)\), and check the limit against \(P(|V| > 0) = 1\). Solution As \(x \to 0^+\), \(\sqrt3/x \to \infty\), so \(\arctan(\sqrt3/x) \to \tfrac\pi2\), and \(\tfrac{x\sqrt3}{3 + x^2} \to 0\). Therefore \(P(|V| > x) \to \tfrac{2}{\pi}\cdot\tfrac\pi2 = 1\). This is the value that \(P(|V| > 0) = 1\) requires, because the t(3) law has a density and so no atom at 0. Proof
[problem P1] P1 · The inequality \(\arctan u \ge u - u^3/3\) Under Assumption 2, prove that \(\arctan u \ge u - u^3/3\) for \(u \ge 0\), using the derivative of \(h(u) = \arctan u - u + u^3/3\). Solution \[h'(u) = \frac{1}{1 + u^2} - 1 + u^2 = \frac{1 - (1 + u^2) + u^2(1 + u^2)}{1 + u^2} = \frac{u^4}{1 + u^2}\], which is nonnegative. So \(h\) is nondecreasing on \([0, \infty)\), and \(h(0) = 0\). Hence \(h(u) \ge 0\), which is the inequality.
[problem P2] P2 · The Cauchy median and quartiles Using \(F_C\) from Proposition 1.3 (under Assumption 2), find the 25th and 75th percentiles of \(C\) and their difference. Solution \(F_C(q) = \tfrac14\) gives \(\arctan q = -\tfrac\pi4\), so \(q = -1\). \(F_C(q) = \tfrac34\) gives \(\arctan q = \tfrac\pi4\), so \(q = 1\). The difference is \[1 - (-1) = 2\], which is the interquartile range used in Lab 1, Part C.
[problem P3] P3 · The integral test for the Cauchy mean Show directly that \(\int_1^\infty \frac{x}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \infty\), and deduce that \(E|C| = \infty\). Solution With \(v = 1 + x^2\), \(dv = 2x\,dx\), so \[\int_1^M \frac{x}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \tfrac12\int_2^{1 + M^2} \frac{dv}{v} = \tfrac12(\ln(1 + M^2) - \ln 2)\], which tends to \(\infty\) as \(M \to \infty\). Since \[E|C| = 2\int_0^\infty x f_C(x)\,dx \ge \tfrac{2}{\pi}\int_1^\infty \frac{x}{1 + x^2}\,dx = \infty\], \(E|C| = \infty\). Empirical

[problem E2] E2 · The Cauchy interquartile range against its exact value (Lab 1) Lab 1, Part C, prints interquartile ranges of 1.8220 at \(n = 10\) and 1.8838 at \(n = 100\) for the Cauchy sample mean, and it prints the exact range of one draw as 2. Say what the printed numbers do and do not show. Solution Both printed sample ranges are within 0.2 of 2 in this run, so in this run the sample mean's spread does not shrink with \(n\). The two numbers come from 400 replications with one seed for each size, and no standard error is printed for them. They do not show the exact range of the sample mean for any \(n\). [problem E3] E3 · The Student-t(3) standardized variance (Lab 1) Lab 1, Part C, prints a variance of \(\sqrt n\,\bar X_n/\sqrt3\) equal to 0.8807 at \(n = 10\) and 1.1987 at \(n = 100\). State the value that the finite-variance calculation of Lesson 9 gives for this variance, say whether this page proves it, and say what the printed values can and cannot show. Solution Lesson 9, Proposition 1.2 needs only identically distributed draws with a finite mean and variance and pairwise independence, so it applies to \(V\). It gives \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \operatorname{Var}(V)/n = 3/n\), with \(\operatorname{Var}(V) = 3\) from Remark 2.2, so \(\operatorname{Var}(\sqrt n\,\bar X_n/\sqrt3) = n \cdot (3/n)/3 = 1\). The printed values can differ from 1 only by sampling error, since each is the sample variance of 400 replications, and the lab prints no standard error, so this page does not measure that error. [problem R1] R1 · An exponential tail is light Let \(X\) have \[P(|X| > x) = e^{-x}\] for \(x \ge 0\). Decide, with proof, whether \(X\) is heavy-tailed in the sense of Definition 1.1. Solution Take \(c = \tfrac12\). Then \[e^{cx}P(|X| > x) = e^{-x/2} \to 0\], not \(\infty\). So Definition 1.1 fails for this \(c\), and \(X\) is light-tailed. The definition requires the limit to be infinite for every \(c > 0\), and one failing \(c\) is enough to make the law light-tailed in this lesson's sense.
[problem R2] R2 · The Student-t(3) variance and tail together Using Remark 2.2 and Proposition 2.3 (under Assumption 1), state which of \(E[V^2]\) and \(E[V^4]\) are finite, and say which of the claims of this lesson would change if \(V\) had infinite variance. Solution \(E[V^2] = 3\) is finite (Remark 2.2), and \(E[V^4] = \infty\) (Proposition 2.3). The same bound also gives \(E|V|^3 = \infty\). For \(t^2 \ge 3\), \(t^3 f_V(t) \ge \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2\pi t}\), by the density bound in the proof of Proposition 1.5(b), and \(\int_{\sqrt3}^\infty dt/t = \infty\), so \(E|V|^3 \ge 2\int_{\sqrt3}^\infty t^3 f_V(t)\,dt = \infty\) (under Assumption 1). The page does not claim the general range of finite moments. The claim that the scaled sample mean is standardized by \(\sqrt3\) would change if the variance were infinite, because the standardization needs a finite \(\sigma\). The heavy-tail claim of Proposition 1.5(b) uses only the density, so it would not change.
[problem R3] R3 · Why the Cauchy case is not a counterexample to Lesson 9 Explain which hypothesis of Theorem 1.3 of Lesson 9 fails for the Cauchy law, and why the failure is not repaired by taking a larger sample. Solution Theorem 1.3 needs the hypotheses of Proposition 1.2, a finite mean \(\mu\) and a finite variance \(\sigma^2\), which give \(E[\bar X_n] = \mu\) and \(\operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \sigma^2/n\). Proposition 2.1 shows \(E|C| = \infty\), so the Cauchy law has no mean and its variance is infinite. A larger sample does not change the law of each draw, so the hypothesis remains false at every \(n\). The theorem does not claim anything about a law that violates its hypothesis, so the case is outside it, not a counterexample.
