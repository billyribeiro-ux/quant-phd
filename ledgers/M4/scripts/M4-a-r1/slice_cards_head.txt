M4-c001 L01 T | What is a finite probability space? || A finite set Omega of outcomes with weights p(omega) > 0 that sum to 1 (Section 1).
M4-c002 L01 T | What is a random variable on a finite probability space? || A function X from Omega to the real numbers (Definition 1.1).
M4-c003 L01 T | What is the distribution of a random variable X? || The map x to P(X = x), the sum of p(omega) over the outcomes with X(omega) = x (Definition 1.2).
M4-c004 L01 T | What is the distribution function of X? || F(x) = P(X <= x) for real x (Definition 2.1).
M4-c005 L01 T | What is the shape of the distribution function of a finite law? || Hypotheses: positive weights and distinct values x_1 to x_m in increasing order. Then F(x) = 0 for x < x_1 and F(x) = 1 for x >= x_m (Proposition 2.2(a)).
M4-c006 L01 T | By how much does the distribution function of a finite law jump at a value x_k? || Hypotheses: positive weights and distinct values. The jump at x_k is p_k > 0, so F(x_k) minus the left limit at x_k equals p_k (Proposition 2.2(d)).
M4-c007 L01 T | What is the quantile function of X? || For 0 < p <= 1, Q(p) = inf{x in R : F(x) >= p} (Definition 3.1), the left-endpoint convention.
M4-c008 L01 T | What is Q(p) for a finite law with distinct values x_1 to x_m in increasing order? || Hypotheses: positive weights, distinct values, 0 < p <= 1. Q(p) = x_(k*), where k* is the least index k with F_k >= p. The index exists because F_m = 1 >= p (Proposition 3.2).
M4-c009 L01 T | For a finite law with positive weights and distinct values, what is Q(p) when F_(k-1) < p <= F_k? || Hypotheses: positive weights and distinct values. Q(p) = x_k, with F_0 = 0 and 1 <= k <= m (Remark 3.3). At p = F_k this gives Q(F_k) = x_k, not x_(k+1).
M4-c010 L01 T | What is the quantile of n equally likely distinct values? || Hypotheses: distinct values y_1 to y_n in increasing order, each with probability 1/n, and 0 < p <= 1. Then Q(p) = y_(ceil(pn)), the ceil(pn)-th smallest value, where ceil(pn) is the least integer at least pn (Proposition 3.4).
M4-c011 L01 T | Which index is the 99% quantile of 200 equally likely distinct losses? || 198. Here pn = 198 is an integer, so the index is 198 (Example 3.5).
M4-c012 L01 T | For the table of Example 3.6, what is Q(1/2)? || 0, because F(-1) = 3/10 < 1/2 and F(0) = 3/5 >= 1/2 (Example 3.6).
M4-c013 L01 T | Is F(Q(p)) = p always true? || Hypotheses: finite law with positive weights and distinct values, and 0 < p <= 1. No. Proposition 3.2 gives F(Q(p)) >= p, not equality. In Example 3.6, Q(1/2) = 0, but F(0) = 3/5, not 1/2.
M4-c015 L01 T | Is the 0.99 quantile of 150 equally likely distinct values the 148th smallest, because pn = 148.5 rounds down? || No. Hypotheses: n equally likely distinct values, 0 < p <= 1. The index is the least integer at least pn, so Proposition 3.4 gives ceil(148.5) = 149, not the floor, 148.
M4-c016 L01 T | Does the grid check of Lab 1 prove Proposition 3.2 for every p? || No. Lab 1 compares the two forms of Q at 100 grid points p = j/100 for one table. The proof of Proposition 3.2 covers every p in (0, 1].
M4-c017 L02 T | What is the expectation of a random variable with distinct values x_1 to x_m? || E[X] = sum over k of x_k p_k, where p_k = P(X = x_k) (Definition 1.1). It is the probability-weighted average of the values of X.
M4-c018 L02 T | Does summing X(omega) p(omega) over outcomes give the same expectation? || Hypothesis: finite probability space with positive weights. Yes. The sum over outcomes of X(omega) p(omega) equals the sum over values of x_k p_k (Proposition 1.2), because the events {X = x_k} partition the outcome set.
M4-c019 L02 T | Is expectation linear on a finite probability space? || Yes. For random variables U and V on the same finite probability space and constants alpha, beta, delta, E[alpha U + beta V + delta] = alpha E[U] + beta E[V] + delta (Proposition 1.3).
M4-c020 L02 T | How is the expectation of a random variable with a continuous density f defined? || E[X] = integral of x f(x) dx over the real line, when the integral of |x| f(x) dx is finite (Definition 1.6).
M4-c021 L02 T | What is the variance of X? || Var(X) = E[(X - mu)^2] with mu = E[X] (Definition 2.1). A variance is never negative, because it is the expectation of a square.
M4-c022 L02 T | What is the shortcut formula for variance? || Hypotheses: finite probability space with positive weights. Var(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 (Proposition 2.2).
M4-c023 L02 T | Does scaling X by a constant a multiply its variance by a? || No. Hypothesis: finite probability space with positive weights. Var(aX + b) = a^2 Var(X) for constants a and b, so scaling multiplies the variance by a^2, and a shift b leaves it unchanged (Proposition 2.3).
M4-c024 L02 T | When is the variance of a finite law zero? || Hypotheses: finite probability space with positive weights. Var(X) = 0 if and only if X takes exactly one value (Proposition 2.4).
M4-c025 L02 T | What is E[X] for a fair six-sided die? || E[X] = 7/2 (Example 1.4): the average of the faces 1 to 6, each with probability 1/6.

M4-c027 L02 T | Is the expectation always one of the values the variable takes? || No. For a fair die E[X] = 7/2, which is not a face of the die (Example 1.4).
M4-c029 L02 T | Is Var(X) always equal to E[X^2]? || Hypothesis: finite probability space with positive weights. No. Var(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 (Proposition 2.2), so the two agree only when E[X] = 0. For the die E[X^2] = 91/6 and Var(X) = 35/12 (Example 2.5).

M4-c032 L02 T | On a finite probability space with positive weights, is E[(X - c)^2] smallest at c = E[X]? || Yes. Hypotheses: finite probability space with positive weights. For every real c, E[(X - c)^2] = Var(X) + (E[X] - c)^2 (Problem P2), so the minimum is at c = E[X].

M4-c034 L03 T | What is the k-th moment of X? || E[X^k], for a positive integer k (Section 1).
M4-c222 L03 T | What is the k-th central moment of X? || m_k = E[(X - mu)^k], with mu = E[X] (Section 1).
M4-c035 L03 T | What is the first central moment m_1 of X? || Hypothesis: finite probability space with positive weights. m_1 = 0 (Proposition 1.1).
M4-c223 L03 T | What is the second central moment m_2 of X? || Hypothesis: finite probability space with positive weights. m_2 = Var(X) (Proposition 1.1).
M4-c036 L03 T | What is the standardized variable Z of X? || Hypotheses: finite probability space with positive weights, and X takes at least two values. Z = (X - mu)/sigma with sigma = sqrt(Var(X)) > 0 (Definition 2.1).
M4-c037 L03 T | What is the skewness of X? || gamma_3 = E[Z^3], the third standardized moment, with Z = (X - mu)/sigma (Definitions 2.1 and 2.2).
M4-c224 L03 T | What is the kurtosis of X? || gamma_4 = E[Z^4], the fourth standardized moment, with no subtraction of 3 (Definition 2.2).
M4-c038 L03 T | How is the skewness written in central moments? || Hypotheses: at least two values, positive weights. gamma_3 = m_3/sigma^3 (Proposition 2.3).
M4-c225 L03 T | How is the kurtosis written in central moments? || Hypotheses: at least two values, positive weights. gamma_4 = m_4/sigma^4 = m_4/m_2^2 (Proposition 2.3).
M4-c039 L03 T | How does kurtosis change under Y = aX + b with a not zero? || Hypotheses: finite probability space with positive weights, X takes at least two values, and a is not 0. Kurtosis is unchanged (Proposition 2.4).
M4-c040 L03 T | What is the lower bound on kurtosis, and when is it attained? || Hypotheses: at least two values, positive weights. gamma_4 >= 1, with equality if and only if X takes exactly two values, each with probability 1/2 (Proposition 2.5).

M4-c049 L04 T | What is a joint table for X and Y? || A list of values p(x,y) >= 0 over pairs of values, with total 1 (Definition 1.1).
M4-c050 L04 T | When are two events independent? || When P(A and B) = P(A) P(B) (Definition 1.2).
M4-c051 L04 T | When are discrete random variables X and Y independent? || When p(x,y) = P(X = x) P(Y = y) for every pair (x,y) of values, so every cell of the joint table is the product of its two marginals (Definition 1.2).
M4-c052 L04 T | Is the independence test applied cell by cell? || Yes. One cell that fails the product rule makes the pair dependent (Remark 1.3).
M4-c053 L04 T | Are two events with positive probability and no common outcome independent? || No. Their intersection has probability 0, while P(A) P(B) > 0, so they are dependent (Proposition 1.4).
M4-c054 L04 T | What is E[XY] for independent variables with finitely many values? || E[XY] = E[X] E[Y] (Proposition 1.5).
M4-c055 L04 T | What is the covariance of X and Y? || Cov(X,Y) = E[(X - E[X])(Y - E[Y])] (Definition 1.6).
M4-c056 L04 T | What is the shortcut formula for covariance? || Hypothesis: finitely many values. Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] - E[X] E[Y] (Proposition 1.7).
M4-c057 L04 T | Do independent variables with finitely many values have zero covariance? || Yes, Cov(X,Y) = 0 (Proposition 1.8).
M4-c058 L04 T | Does zero covariance imply independence? || No. For X uniform on {-1, 0, 1} and Y = X^2, the covariance is 0, but p(0,0) = 1/3 while P(X = 0) P(Y = 0) = 1/9 (Example 1.9).
M4-c059 L04 T | What are the conditional distribution and the conditional expectation of X given Y = y, in the elementary form? || For P(Y = y) > 0: P(X = x | Y = y) = p(x,y)/P(Y = y), and E[X | Y = y] = sum over x of x P(X = x | Y = y) (Definition 2.1). Only finite tables are covered (Remark 2.2).
M4-c060 L04 T | Does total expectation hold for a finite table? || Yes, for X and Y with finitely many values: E[X] = sum over y with P(Y = y) > 0 of E[X | Y = y] P(Y = y) (Proposition 2.3).
M4-c061 L04 T | Under independence, does E[X | Y = y] equal E[X]? || Yes, when X and Y are independent with finitely many values: E[X | Y = y] = E[X] for every y with P(Y = y) > 0 (Proposition 2.4).
