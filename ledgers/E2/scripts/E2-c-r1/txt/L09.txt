52: A seller with several identical units can charge the winners in two ways. This lesson compares them. Under the uniform-price format every winner pays the same clearing bid (Definition 1.1), and under the discriminatory format each winner pays its own bid. The comparisons here hold the bids fixed: they say what each payment rule collects from the same bids. They say nothing about how bidders would change their bids. Lab 1 checks the revenue ranking and the one-unit identity in exact cents over all 256 profiles of a small grid, and it prints one fixed profile for payments and efficiency (Definition 3.1).
54: 1Several units, the ranking and the two payment rules
56: Money is in integer cents. A seller offers \(k\) identical units to \(n\) bidders, with \(1 \le k
59: Assumption 1 (units, demand and bids)
60: There are \(k\) identical units and \(n\) bidders, with \(1 \le k
64: Definition 1.1 (ranking, winners and the clearing bid)
65: The uniform-price and discriminatory formats are two payment rules for the same allocation. Rank the bidders by bid, from the highest to the lowest, and break a tie in favour of the lower bidder index. Let \(b_{(1)} \ge b_{(2)} \ge \dots \ge b_{(n)}\) be the bids in this order. The winners are the \(k\) bidders ranked first. The clearing bid is \(b_{(k+1)}\), the bid of the first loser. Under the uniform-price format, each winner pays the clearing bid \(b_{(k+1)}\). Under the discriminatory format, each winner pays its own bid. A loser pays 0 under both formats.
68: The ranking is a complete order on the bidders, because the tie-break by index separates any two bidders with equal bids. So the set of winners has exactly \(k\) members under either format. The allocation is the same under both formats, and only the payments differ.
71: Definition 1.2 (revenue)
72: The revenue of a format on a bid profile is the total cents paid by the winners. Under the uniform-price format it is \(R_u(b) = k\,b_{(k+1)}\). Under the discriminatory format it is \(R_d(b) = b_{(1)} + \dots + b_{(k)}\), the sum of the winners' own bids, because the winners' bids are the \(k\) highest.
75: 2The discriminatory format collects at least as much on fixed bids
78: Proposition 2.1 (the revenue ranking on fixed bids)
79: Under Assumption 1, for every bid profile \(b\), \(R_d(b) \ge R_u(b)\). Equality holds exactly when \(b_{(1)} = b_{(2)} = \dots = b_{(k+1)}\), that is, when each winner's bid equals the clearing bid.
83: Proof
84: For each \(j\) with \(1 \le j \le k\), the ranking gives \(b_{(j)} \ge b_{(k+1)}\). Summing these \(k\) inequalities gives \(b_{(1)} + \dots + b_{(k)} \ge k\,b_{(k+1)}\), which is \(R_d(b) \ge R_u(b)\). Equality of two sums of \(k\) terms, each term bounded below by the same number, holds exactly when every term is equal to that number. So equality holds exactly when \(b_{(j)} = b_{(k+1)}\) for every \(j\) with \(1 \le j \le k\), which is the stated condition.
88: Remark 2.2 (what the ranking claim does not say)
89: Proposition 2.1 compares the two rules on the same bids. It does not say that the discriminatory format earns more when bidders respond to the rule. A bidder that knows the rule may bid differently under each format, for example by shading its bid below its value under one format and not the other (bid shading, which L08 defines for a signal), and the bids would then be a different profile. Such a comparison needs the equilibrium bids of both formats, which a Nash equilibrium of each game would give. This lesson does not compute them.

246: 3Efficiency: which bidders win
248: Efficiency compares the value of the winners with the largest total value that \(k\) units could go to. The term allocative efficiency is used here in that sense, as Definition 3.1 states.
251: Definition 3.1 (allocative efficiency)
252: Let \(v_{[1]} \ge v_{[2]} \ge \dots \ge v_{[n]}\) be the values in decreasing order, and let \(W(b)\) be the set of winners under a bid profile \(b\). The allocative efficiency of \(b\) is the pair \(\bigl(\sum_{i \in W(b)} v_i,\ v_{[1]} + \dots + v_{[k]}\bigr)\), both in cents. When the second entry is positive, its ratio, the first entry divided by the second, is the efficiency. If the second entry is not positive, that is, the k highest values have a sum of zero or less, the ratio is not defined, and the efficiency is not defined.
256: Proposition 3.2 (truthful bids are efficient under either format)
257: Under Assumption 1, if each bidder bids its value, \(b = v\), and the k highest values have a positive sum, then the value of the winners is \(v_{[1]} + \dots + v_{[k]}\) under both formats. The efficiency is 1.
261: Proof
262: With \(b = v\), the ranking of Definition 1.1 lists the bidders by value, from the highest to the lowest, with ties to the lower index. The winners are the first \(k\) bidders in this list, and their values are \(v_{[1]}, \dots, v_{[k]}\). Their sum is \(v_{[1]} + \dots + v_{[k]}\). With ties in value, two bidders with equal values can change places in the list, but the values in the first \(k\) places stay the same. The winners are the same under both formats (Problem P3), so the same holds for both.
266: Remark 3.3 (efficiency under other bids)
267: For bids other than the values, the winners can have a lower total value. Part A of Lab 1 is such a case. Bidder 1 has value 5000 cents and bids 9500 cents, so it wins, and bidder 3 has value 7000 cents and bids 7000 cents, so it does not. The efficiency is 7/8.
270: 4One unit: the uniform rule is the second-price rule
273: Proposition 4.1 (the one-unit case)
274: Under Assumption 1 with \(k = 1\), the uniform-price winner pays \(b_{(2)}\), the second-highest bid. The discriminatory winner pays its own bid \(b_{(1)}\). Both formats have the same winner.
278: Proof
279: With \(k = 1\), the clearing bid is \(b_{(k+1)} = b_{(2)}\), and the uniform rule charges the winner the clearing bid. The discriminatory rule charges the winner its own bid, which is \(b_{(1)}\). The winner is the bidder ranked first under both formats (Problem P3). Both statements follow from Definition 1.1 with \(k = 1\).
282: Part C of Lab 1 checks the identity on all 256 profiles with bids in {0, 1, 2, 3}. With one unit, the uniform rule is the second-price rule and the discriminatory rule is the first-price rule. This lesson uses those two names as labels for the rules, with no attribution.
285: 5Where the canon breaks
287: Fixed bids. Proposition 2.1 and Lab 1 Part B hold the bids fixed. Under either format, a bidder that anticipates the rule may bid differently, and the revenue ranking of the fixed-bid comparison is then not a ranking of outcomes. Equilibrium bids for multi-unit formats are not computed in this lesson.
288: Unit demand. Assumption 1 says each bidder wants at most one unit. A bidder that wants several units has a bid for each unit, and the rules of Definition 1.1 do not describe that case.
289: Efficiency under non-truthful bids. Proposition 3.2 says efficiency is 1 under truthful bids, when the k highest values have a positive sum. Under other bids the allocation can give a value below the best possible, as Lab 1 Part A shows with efficiency 7/8.
290: Practice. This lesson states no fact about how any government or other auction is run. No issuer page is in the registry for that purpose, so no practice claim is made.
295: 6Misconceptions
298: “The uniform-price format collects less, so a seller should always choose the discriminatory format.”
299: Proposition 2.1 is a statement about the same bids under the two rules. It does not say what bidders would bid under each rule. The lesson makes no claim about the ranking after bidders respond (Remark 2.2). The fixed-bid ranking is a fact about the payment rules, and it is not a recommendation.
302: “Under the uniform-price format, each winner pays its own bid.”
303: Under the uniform format each winner pays the clearing bid \(b_{(k+1)}\). In Lab 1 Part A, bidder 0 bids 8000 cents and pays 7000 cents. The own-bid rule is the discriminatory format.
306: “With one unit the uniform-price format is the first-price format.”
307: With \(k = 1\) the clearing bid is the second-highest bid, so the uniform format charges the winner the second-highest bid. Proposition 4.1 proves this, and Lab 1 Part C prints 256 of 256 profiles that agree. The first-price rule, in which the winner pays its own bid, is the discriminatory format with \(k = 1\).

317: Mechanics
319: M1 · The ranking and the winners
320: Four bidders bid \(b = (5000, 8000, 6000, 7000)\) cents, and \(k = 2\). Give the ranking, the winners and the clearing bid.
321: Solution
322: The bids in order from highest are 8000 (bidder 1), 7000 (bidder 3), 6000 (bidder 2), and 5000 (bidder 0). So the ranking is 1, 3, 2, 0. The winners are bidders 1 and 3. The clearing bid is \(b_{(3)} = 6000\), the bid of bidder 2, the first loser.
326: M2 · Both revenues for the profile of M1
327: For the profile of M1, compute the revenue of each format in cents, and the difference.
328: Solution
329: Uniform: \(R_u = k \times b_{(3)} = 2 \times 6000 = 12000\) cents. Discriminatory: \(R_d = b_{(1)} + b_{(2)} = 8000 + 7000 = 15000\) cents. The difference is \(15000 - 12000 = 3000\) cents, and Proposition 2.1 says it is nonnegative.
333: M3 · One unit, the same profile

335: Solution
336: With \(k = 1\) the winner is bidder 1, with bid 8000. The clearing bid is \(b_{(2)} = 7000\), so under the uniform format the winner pays 7000 cents. Under the discriminatory format it pays its own bid, 8000 cents.

351: P2 · Efficiency is 1 under truthful bids
352: Let each bidder bid its value, \(b_i = v_i\). Show that under either format the efficiency of §3 equals 1 when the k highest values have a positive sum, and that the value of the winners equals the sum of the \(k\) highest values, including when values tie.
353: Solution
354: With \(b = v\), the ranking orders the bidders by value, with ties to the lower index. The winners are the first \(k\) bidders in that order. The sum of their values is the sum of the \(k\) highest values, because the ranking lists the values in decreasing order, and the sum of the first \(k\) entries of a decreasing list is the sum of the \(k\) largest entries. With ties in value, two bidders with equal values swap places in the ranking without changing the values in the first \(k\) places. So the efficiency is \(1\) under both formats, provided the sum of the k highest values is positive.
358: P3 · The allocation does not depend on the format
359: Show that for a fixed bid profile the winners are the same under the two formats, so efficiency under fixed bids depends only on the bids and not on the format.
360: Solution
361: Definition 1.1 defines the winners by the ranking alone, and the ranking uses only the bids and the index. The payment rule enters only after the winners are chosen. So the set of winners, and therefore the value of the winners and the efficiency of §3, do not depend on the format.

401: R2 · What a multi-unit equilibrium would need
402: Say what must be specified before the revenue of the two formats can be compared as outcomes of a game, rather than on fixed bids.
403: Solution
404: A game comparison needs a bidding rule for each format: a strategy for each bidder that maps its value, or its signal, to a bid for each unit it could win.
