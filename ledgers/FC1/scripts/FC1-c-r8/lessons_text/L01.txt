  21: A financial intermediary (defined in Definition 1.1) sits between savers and borrowers, and in the model below a bank’s lending is tied to its deposits. This lesson states a stylised bank with its assumptions written out, proves the total deposits the model produces, and checks the proof in exact arithmetic. It then defines a financial intermediary and shows what a loss on its loans does to its equity and deposits. The lesson makes no claim about any real bank.
  22: 1Intermediaries and the balance sheet
  23: A balance sheet lists what a firm holds and what it owes at one date. The holdings are its assets. The amounts owed are its liabilities. The residual is its equity. This section fixes the definitions the rest of the lesson uses. All amounts are in dollars, and money printed to the cent is rounded once from its exact value, half up.
  24: Definition 1.1 (financial intermediary)
  25: A financial intermediary is a firm that issues claims on itself to savers, and holds claims on other parties, such as loans, as its assets. The claims it has issued are its liabilities. Its equity is its assets minus its liabilities.
  26: Remark 1.2 (the identity is a definition)
  27: Because equity is defined as assets minus liabilities, assets equal liabilities plus equity for every firm. The identity holds by definition, so it states no finding about any firm. What the definition does fix is the sign convention: a negative equity means the liabilities exceed the assets.
  28: 2Bank money creation in a stylised model
  29: The model has one bank, three assumptions and a sequence of stages. A stage is a date at which the bank receives deposits and makes loans. Each assumption is written out so that a proof can cite it by number.
  30: Assumption 2.0 (the stylised bank)
  31: The bank is a single firm that holds reserves and loans as its assets and deposits as its liabilities. Let \(D_t\) be the deposits it receives at stage \(t\) (new deposits, not its balance), and let \(X_t\) be its reserves and \(\ell_t\) its loans at that stage. Its total deposits after stages \(0\) to \(n\) are \(S_n = \sum_{t=0}^{n} D_t\), defined in Section 2. Assumptions A1 to A3 below hold.
  32: A1 (constant reserve ratio). \(X_t = \rho D_t\) at every stage, for a constant \(\rho\) with \(0 < \rho \le 1\).
  33: A2 (full redeposit). The whole of the loan made at stage \(t\), \(\ell_t = D_t - X_t\), is deposited at the bank at stage \(t+1\).
  34: A3 (closed system). No cash leaves the bank, no deposit is withdrawn, no loan is repaid, and the bank has no other assets or liabilities in the stages considered.
  35: Definition 2.1 (bank money creation)
  36: Bank money creation is the increase in total deposits that results when the bank makes a loan and the proceeds are deposited back at the same bank. In the model of Assumption 2.0 it is the sum of the redeposits made after stage 0. The definition describes the model's mechanism only. Section 5 says what the lesson does not claim about real banks.
  37: Take an initial deposit \(d > 0\) at stage 0, so \(D_0 = d\). Under A1 the reserves are \(X_0 = \rho d\) and the loan is \(\ell_0 = (1-\rho)d\). Under A2 that loan is deposited at stage 1, so \(D_1 = (1-\rho)d\). The same step repeats, which gives the recursion
  38: \[ D_{t+1} = (1-\rho)\,D_t, \qquad\text{so}\qquad D_t = (1-\rho)^t d. \]
  39: Total deposits after stages \(0\) to \(n\) are \(S_n = \sum_{t=0}^{n} D_t\). The deposit multiplier (Proposition 3.1) is the ratio \(S_n/d\).
  40: Proposition 2.2 (the balance sheet balances at every stage)
  41: Under Assumption 2.0, and with \(\ell_t\) the loan made at stage \(t\), \(X_t + \ell_t = D_t\) for every \(t\). Summing over stages \(0\) to \(n\), cumulative reserves plus cumulative loans equal total deposits, \(\sum_{t=0}^{n} X_t + \sum_{t=0}^{n} \ell_t = S_n\).
  42: Proof
  43: By A1 and the definition of the loan, \(X_t + \ell_t = \rho D_t + (1-\rho)D_t = D_t\). This holds for each \(t\). Summing the identity over \(t = 0, \dots, n\) gives \(\sum_t X_t + \sum_t \ell_t = \sum_t D_t = S_n\), which is the cumulative statement. It uses nothing beyond the stage identity. \(\blacksquare\)
  44: 3The deposit multiplier
  45: The next result gives total deposits in closed form. It uses the geometric series of M1, which is linked on this page.
  46: Proposition 3.1 (the deposit multiplier)
  47: Under Assumption 2.0 with \(d > 0\), for every \(n \ge 0\),
  48: \[ S_n = d\,\frac{1 - (1-\rho)^{n+1}}{\rho}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \frac{S_n}{d} = \sum_{t=0}^{n} (1-\rho)^t = \frac{1 - (1-\rho)^{n+1}}{\rho}. \]
  49: The ratio \(S_n/d\) converges to \(1/\rho\) as \(n\) grows, so total deposits converge to \(d/\rho\).
  50: Proof
  51: Let \(q = 1 - \rho\). Since \(\rho > 0\), \(q \ne 1\), and the finite geometric sum is \(\sum_{t=0}^{n} q^t = (1-q^{n+1})/(1-q)\). Here \(1 - q = \rho\), so the sum is \((1-(1-\rho)^{n+1})/\rho\). The recursion of Section 2 gives \(S_n = d\sum_{t=0}^{n} q^t\), which is the closed form.
  52: For the limit, A1 gives \(0 \le q < 1\), because \(0 < \rho \le 1\). Then \(q^{n+1} \to 0\), so \(S_n \to d/\rho\). When \(\rho = 1\), \(q = 0\), and the formula gives \(S_n = d\) for every \(n\), which agrees with the sum directly, since \(0^0 = 1\) and \(0^t = 0\) for \(t \ge 1\). \(\blacksquare\)
  53: Remark 3.2 (which hypothesis does what)
  54: The proof uses \(\rho \ne 0\) to divide, \(\rho \le 1\) to keep \(q\) nonnegative so that the limit exists, and A2 to write the recursion. The condition \(\rho > 0\) cannot be dropped. Set \(\rho = 0\). The recursion gives \(D_t = d\) for every \(t\), so \(S_n = (n+1)d\), which grows without bound and has no limit. A value \(\rho > 1\) would make the first loan \((1-\rho)d\) negative, which A1 excludes.
  55: Example 3.3 (a deposit of 1,000 dollars at a 10 percent reserve ratio)
  56: Take \(d = 1000\) dollars and \(\rho = 1/10\). Then \(D_t = 1000 \cdot 0.9^t\) dollars. Total deposits after stage 1 are \(S_1 = 1900\) dollars, and after stage 2 they are \(S_2 = 2710\) dollars. After stage 10 they are \(S_{10} = 1000 \times (1-0.9^{11})/0.1 = 1000 \times 6.8618940391 = 6861.8940391\) dollars, which is 6,861.89 dollars to the cent, half up. The multiplier at stage 10 is \(6.8618940391\), printed as 6.8619 in Lab 1, and it approaches \(1/\rho = 10\). The limit of total deposits is \(10{,}000\) dollars.
  57: Remark 3.4 (rounding happens once, from the exact value)
  58: Each total in Example 3.3 is computed in exact arithmetic from the closed form. Rounding the multiplier to four places before multiplying by 1,000 would move the stage 10 total to 6,861.90 dollars, which is a different cent. Lab 1, Part A, applies the rule that the cents are rounded once, half up, from the exact value.
  59: Proposition 3.5 (leakage lowers the limit)
  60: Replace A2 by the weaker assumption A2′: a share \(\varphi\) with \(0 \le \varphi \le 1\) of each loan is redeposited at the bank at the next stage, and the rest is held as cash outside the bank. Keep A1, with \(0 < \rho \le 1\), and keep the no-withdrawal and no-repayment parts of A3; the cash held outside the bank is the leakage that A2′ adds, so A3's no-cash-leaves clause is replaced. Then total deposits converge to \(d/(1 - \varphi(1-\rho))\). This limit is at most \(d/\rho\), with equality if and only if \(\varphi = 1\) or \(\rho = 1\).
  61: Proof
  62: Under A2′, \(D_{t+1} = \varphi(1-\rho) D_t\), so \(D_t = d\,\kappa^t\) with \(\kappa = \varphi(1-\rho)\). Here \(0 \le \kappa \le 1 - \rho < 1\), so the geometric series converges and \(\sum_{t \ge 0} D_t = d/(1-\kappa)\). That is the stated limit. For the comparison, \((1-\kappa) - \rho = (1-\rho) - \varphi(1-\rho) = (1-\rho)(1-\varphi) \ge 0\). So \(1 - \kappa \ge \rho > 0\), and \(d/(1-\kappa) \le d/\rho\). Equality holds exactly when \((1-\rho)(1-\varphi) = 0\), that is, when \(\varphi = 1\) or \(\rho = 1\). \(\blacksquare\)
  63: Example 3.6 (half the loan redeposited)
  64: With \(d = 1000\) dollars, \(\rho = 1/10\) and \(\varphi = 1/2\), the limit is \(1000/(1 - 0.45) = 1818.18\) dollars, rounded to the cent, half up, from \(1818.1818\ldots\). That is 18.18 percent of the full-redeposit limit of 10,000 dollars, a base stated here. Lab 1, Part C, prints the same limit in cents.
  65: 4An intermediary's loss buffer
  66: The last result concerns one balance sheet after a loss. It uses only the balance sheet identity of Remark 1.2 and one further assumption, which is stated in full.
  67: Assumption 4.0 (deposits keep their value)
  68: An intermediary has deposits \(K\), equity \(E\) and assets \(A = K + E\). A loss \(\lambda \ge 0\) lowers the value of the assets and of nothing else. The deposits remain claims for their full face value, so the model's deposit liabilities stay at \(K\). Costs of the loss and any change in the deposit contract are excluded.
  69: Proposition 4.1 (equity absorbs the loss)
  70: Under Assumption 4.0, after a loss \(\lambda\) the assets equal \(A - \lambda\), the deposits equal \(K\), and the equity equals \(E - \lambda\). The equity is nonnegative after the loss if and only if \(\lambda \le E\).
  71: Proof
  72: The assets after the loss are \(A - \lambda = K + E - \lambda\). By Remark 1.2, equity is assets minus deposits, so equity is \((K + E - \lambda) - K = E - \lambda\). This is nonnegative if and only if \(\lambda \le E\). \(\blacksquare\)
  73: Remark 4.2 (a test, not a legal rule)
  74: The condition \(E - \lambda \ge 0\) is the test this model applies. This lesson states no legal or regulatory rule. Proposition 4.1 concerns the arithmetic of one balance sheet under Assumption 4.0.

  76: Predicted from Propositions 2.2, 3.1, 3.5 and 4.1 before running. Part A: the partial sums of total deposits equal the closed form for stages 0 to 10 exactly, the multiplier at stage 10 is 6.8619 to four places, and the limit is 10 for a 1/10 reserve ratio. Part B: reserves plus loans equal deposits at every stage, and cumulatively. Part C: a half redeposit share gives a smaller limit than full redeposit, 181818 cents against 1000000 cents. Part D: equity after a loss of 50, 100 and 150 dollars on 900 dollars of deposits and 100 dollars of equity is 50, 0 and \(-50\) dollars, and equity is nonnegative only for the first two.   78: The bank is a stylised model with the parameters typed in below. It is not the balance sheet of any bank, and no
  79: figure printed here describes any real institution. Every balance is an exact Fraction of a dollar. A money amount
  80: printed in cents is rounded once, half up, from its exact value. The inputs are non-negative, so half up is
  81: floor(x + 1/2). Standard library only; no network; no random numbers.
  82: """
  83: from __future__ import annotations
  84: import math
  85: from fractions import Fraction
  86: INITIAL_DEPOSIT = Fraction(1000)  # dollars, deposited at stage 0
  87: RESERVE_RATIO = Fraction(1, 10)  # reserves as a share of deposits, constant across stages
  88: STAGES = 10
  89: def cents_half_up(dollars: Fraction) -> int:
  90: """Whole cents for a non-negative amount in dollars, rounded once, half up."""
  91: if dollars < 0:
  92: raise ValueError("this lab stages non-negative amounts only")
  93: return math.floor(dollars * 100 + Fraction(1, 2))
  94: def deposit_path(initial: Fraction, reserve_ratio: Fraction, stages: int, redeposit: Fraction) -> list[Fraction]:
  95: """Deposits D_0..D_stages: D_{t+1} = redeposit * (1 - reserve_ratio) * D_t, each in exact dollars."""
  96: path = [initial]
  97: for _ in range(stages):
  98: loan = (1 - reserve_ratio) * path[-1]
  99: path.append(redeposit * loan)
 100: return path
 101: def multiplier_closed_form(reserve_ratio: Fraction, n: int) -> Fraction:
 102: """The sum of (1 - reserve_ratio)^t for t = 0..n, using the geometric closed form."""
 103: q = 1 - reserve_ratio
 104: return (1 - q ** (n + 1)) / reserve_ratio
 105: def main() -> None:
 106: print("A. Total deposits after stages 0 to n of lending, against the closed form")
 107: path = deposit_path(INITIAL_DEPOSIT, RESERVE_RATIO, STAGES, Fraction(1))
 108: running = Fraction(0)
 109: all_match = True
 110: for n in range(STAGES + 1):
 111: running += path[n]
 112: closed = INITIAL_DEPOSIT * multiplier_closed_form(RESERVE_RATIO, n)
 113: all_match = all_match and running == closed
 114: print(

 116: f"total deposits, in cents = {cents_half_up(running):>9d}"
 117: )
 118: print("the partial sums equal the closed form exactly for n = 0 to 10:", all_match)
 119: limit = 1 / RESERVE_RATIO
 120: print(f"limit of the multiplier as n grows, 1 / reserve ratio: {float(limit):.4f}")
 121: print(f"limit in cents: {cents_half_up(INITIAL_DEPOSIT * limit)}")
 122: print()
 123: print("B. Balance sheet at each stage: reserves plus loans equal the deposits received at that stage")
 124: balanced = True
 125: reserves_total = Fraction(0)
 126: loans_total = Fraction(0)

 134: deposits_total = sum(path, Fraction(0))

 136: print(
 137: "cumulative reserves plus cumulative loans equal total deposits:",
 138: reserves_total + loans_total == deposits_total,
 139: )
 140: print(f"total deposits after stage {STAGES}, in cents: {cents_half_up(deposits_total)}")
 141: print()
 142: print("C. Leakage variant: only a share of each loan is redeposited, the rest is held as cash")
 143: for share in (Fraction(1), Fraction(1, 2)):
 144: leaky = deposit_path(INITIAL_DEPOSIT, RESERVE_RATIO, STAGES, share)
 145: leaky_limit = INITIAL_DEPOSIT / (1 - share * (1 - RESERVE_RATIO))
 146: print(
 147: f"redeposit share {float(share):.2f}: total deposits after stage {STAGES}, in cents = "
 148: f"{cents_half_up(sum(leaky, Fraction(0))):>9d}; limit in cents = {cents_half_up(leaky_limit)}"
 149: )
 150: full_limit = INITIAL_DEPOSIT / (1 - (1 - RESERVE_RATIO))
 151: half_limit = INITIAL_DEPOSIT / (1 - Fraction(1, 2) * (1 - RESERVE_RATIO))
 152: print("the full-redeposit limit exceeds the half-redeposit limit:", full_limit > half_limit)
 153: print()

 196: 5Where the canon breaks
 197: The stylised bank is exact inside its assumptions and a simplification outside them. Four places where the analyst must act:
 198: The model has no real bank behind it. Assumption 2.0 fixes one bank, one reserve ratio and no withdrawals. The model omits any equity, other assets, other liabilities and cash flows that a real balance sheet may have. Proposition 3.5 shows that one departure, leakage, lowers the limit. The lesson does not measure any other departure.
 199: The mechanism is a definition here. Definition 2.1 describes what happens inside the model. Whether bank lending creates deposits in a particular economy, and how much, is an empirical question. No source for real bank practice is cited on this page, so the lesson states no finding about it.
 200: The reserve ratio is an input. Assumption A1 treats \(\rho\) as constant and given. Proposition 3.1 shows that the limit depends on \(\rho\): at 1/10 the limit is ten times the initial deposit, and at 1/2 it is twice. The lesson does not say what \(\rho\) should be.
 201: The equity test is a model test. Proposition 4.1 says equity is nonnegative when the loss does not exceed equity, under Assumption 4.0. It does not say what a supervisor or a court would do with a negative value, and it does not model how losses are recognised over time.
 202: 6Misconceptions
 203: “In the stylised bank, lending cannot increase total deposits, because the bank only lends money it already holds.”
 204: The loan of stage 0 is paid into an account at the same bank, so it becomes a new deposit at stage 1 under A2. Total deposits after stages 0 and 1 are \(1000 + 900 = 1900\) dollars, which Lab 1, Part A, prints as multiplier 1.9000 (190000 cents) for the 1/10 reserve ratio. The bank held 1,000 dollars of deposits and created a second deposit from its loan.
 205: “The deposit multiplier is \(1/\rho\) at every stage.”
 206: The multiplier at stage \(n\) is \((1-(1-\rho)^{n+1})/\rho\). For \(\rho < 1\) it is smaller than \(1/\rho\) at every finite \(n\). At stage 10 with \(\rho = 1/10\) it is 6.8619, and it reaches 10 only in the limit, as Proposition 3.1 shows.
 207: “Equity is one of the assets of the intermediary.”
 208: Equity is the residual, assets minus liabilities (Definition 1.1). Under Assumption 4.0 a loss reduces equity and leaves the deposits unchanged, so Proposition 4.1 has equity absorb the loss. Listing equity as an asset as well as the residual would count it twice in the identity of Remark 1.2, because equity is already assets minus liabilities.

 211: Mechanics
 212: M1 · Reserves and the first loan
 213: An intermediary receives an initial deposit of 500 dollars and keeps a constant reserve ratio of 1/5. Find its reserves and its loan at stage 0, and its deposits at stage 1, in dollars.
 214: Solution
 215: Reserves are \(\rho d = 500/5 = 100\) dollars. The loan is \((1-\rho)d = 400\) dollars. Under A2 the loan is redeposited, so the deposits at stage 1 are \(D_1 = 400\) dollars. This equals \((1-\rho)d\), as the recursion of Section 2 requires.
 216: M2 · Total deposits after two stages
 217: An initial deposit of 800 dollars and a reserve ratio of 1/4. Find \(S_2\) exactly, in dollars, using Proposition 3.1.
 218: Solution
 219: Here \(\rho = 1/4\) and \(1 - \rho = 3/4\). Proposition 3.1 gives \(S_2 = 800\,(1 - (3/4)^3)/(1/4) = 800 \cdot (37/64) \cdot 4 = 1850\) dollars. The stage-by-stage deposits are 800, 600 and 450 dollars, and they sum to 1850 dollars, so the closed form and the recursion agree.
 220: M3 · A loss that exceeds equity
 221: An intermediary has deposits of 700 dollars and equity of 300 dollars. A loss of 320 dollars falls on its loans. Find its equity after the loss, and say whether it is nonnegative in the sense of Remark 4.2.
 222: Solution
 223: By Proposition 4.1 the equity after the loss is \(E - \lambda = 300 - 320 = -20\) dollars. The deposits remain 700 dollars under Assumption 4.0. Because \(\lambda > E\), the equity is negative, so the test of Remark 4.2 fails.
 224: Proof
 225: P1 · The total is increasing and bounded
 226: Under Assumption 2.0 with \(0 < \rho \le 1\), show that \(S_n\) is nondecreasing in \(n\) and that \(S_n \le d/\rho\) for every \(n\).
 227: Solution
 228: The difference is \(S_{n+1} - S_n = D_{n+1} = (1-\rho)^{n+1} d\). Since \(0 \le 1 - \rho < 1\), this is nonnegative, so \(S_n\) does not decrease. For the bound, Proposition 3.1 gives \(S_n = d(1-(1-\rho)^{n+1})/\rho\). The term \((1-\rho)^{n+1}\) is nonnegative, so \(S_n \le d/\rho\).
 229: P2 · No limit at a zero reserve ratio
 230: Relax A1 to \(\rho = 0\), keeping A2 and A3. Show that \(S_n\) has no finite limit as \(n\) grows.
 231: Solution
 232: With \(\rho = 0\) the redeposit factor is \(1 - \rho = 1\), so \(D_t = d\) for every \(t\). Then \(S_n = (n+1)d\). For any bound \(B\), choose \(n\) with \((n+1)d > B\); then \(S_n > B\). A sequence that exceeds every bound has no finite limit. This is why Remark 3.2 requires \(\rho > 0\).
 233: P3 · A loss beyond equity
 234: Under Assumption 4.0, suppose \(\lambda > E\). Show that the deposits are unchanged and that the equity is negative.
 235: Solution
 236: Assumption 4.0 keeps the deposit claims at \(K\), so the deposits are unchanged. The equity after the loss is \(E - \lambda\) by Proposition 4.1. If \(\lambda > E\) then \(E - \lambda < 0\). The assets after the loss equal \(K + (E - \lambda)\), which is less than \(K\), so the assets no longer cover the deposits.
 237: Empirical
 238: E1 · Total deposits at stage 10
 239: Using only the printed output of Lab 1, Part A, state total deposits after stage 10 in cents, and the multiplier at stage 10 as printed.
 240: Solution
 241: Lab 1, Part A, prints a total of 686189 cents and a multiplier of 6.8619 for \(n = 10\). The printed cents equal 6,861.89 dollars, which matches Example 3.3. The multiplier is the printed value at four places, and the exact value is 6.8618940391.
 242: E2 · The leakage limit as a share of the full limit
 243: Using only the printed limits of Lab 1, Part C, express the half-redeposit limit as a percent of the full-redeposit limit. State the base.
 244: Solution
 245: The base is the full-redeposit limit, 1000000 cents, which is 10,000 dollars. The half-redeposit limit is 181818 cents. The ratio is \(181818/1000000 = 0.181818\ldots\), which is 18.18 percent to two places, rounded half up. The exact ratio is \(2/11 = 1/5.5\), because the half-redeposit limit is \(1000/0.55 = 20000/11\) dollars (Example 3.6) and the full limit is 10,000 dollars.
 246: E3 · The largest loss with nonnegative equity
 247: Using only the printed output of Lab 1, Part D, find the largest loss the intermediary absorbs with nonnegative equity in the model, and the equity at that loss.
 248: Solution
 249: Lab 1, Part D, prints nonnegative equity for losses of 50 and 100 dollars, with equity of 50 and 0 dollars, and negative equity for 150 dollars. The largest loss in the printed set with nonnegative equity is 100 dollars, which leaves equity of 0. By Proposition 4.1 the general bound is \(\lambda \le E = 100\) dollars, so the printed set reaches the bound exactly.
 250: Research
 251: R1 · A stage-dependent reserve ratio
 252: Let the reserve ratio at stage \(t\) be \(\rho_t\), with \(c \le \rho_t \le 1\) for every \(t\), for a constant \(c > 0\). Write \(D_t\) and \(S_n\) in terms of the \(\rho_s\), and show that \(S_n\) stays bounded.
 253: Solution
 254: The recursion becomes \(D_{t+1} = (1-\rho_t)D_t\), so \(D_t = d\prod_{s<t}(1-\rho_s)\) and \(S_n = \sum_{t=0}^{n} D_t\). Each factor satisfies \(0 \le 1-\rho_s \le 1-c\), so \(D_t \le d(1-c)^t\). Then \(S_n \le d\sum_t (1-c)^t = d/c\). The bound \(d/c\) is the limit of Proposition 3.1 with \(\rho\) replaced by the floor \(c\), and it holds for any path of ratios that stays at or above that floor.
 255: R2 · Equity needed for a given loss
 256: An intermediary wants nonnegative equity after a loss of \(\lambda\) dollars. Using Proposition 4.1, state the least equity it needs before the loss, and say what the answer leaves out.
 257: Solution
 258: Proposition 4.1 requires \(\lambda \le E\), so the least equity before the loss is \(E = \lambda\) dollars. The answer leaves out the costs of the loss, any change in the deposit contract, and losses that arrive in several steps, none of which the model treats.
 259: R3 · Can leakage reach twice the full limit?
 260: Under Proposition 3.5, can any choice of \(\varphi \in [0,1]\) make the limit of total deposits equal \(2d/\rho\)? Answer with a proof.
 261: Solution
 262: No. Proposition 3.5 gives a limit of \(d/(1-\varphi(1-\rho))\), which is at most \(d/\rho\) for every \(\varphi\). Since \(d/\rho > 0\), the value \(2d/\rho\) is strictly larger than \(d/\rho\). Hence no \(\varphi\) in \([0,1]\) gives \(2d/\rho\), and the answer is no.
