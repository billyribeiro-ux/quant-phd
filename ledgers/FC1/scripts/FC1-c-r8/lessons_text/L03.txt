  21: A dealer that stands ready to trade earns a spread on each share it trades, and it holds the shares it has not yet passed on. The two sources are separate terms of Proposition 2.3. This lesson defines the dealer and the bid–ask spread, then proves an exact identity: the dealer's profit valued at the mid (mark-to-mid, Definition 2.1) equals half the quoted spread times the shares traded, plus a term that comes from mid-price moves. A two-dealer game with one-cent prices shows that the equilibrium ask can sit 0, 1 or 2 cents above cost. Lab 1 checks each identity in whole cents.
  22: 1Quotes, dealers and the spread
  23: Definition 1.1 (dealer)
  24: A dealer is an agent that posts a bid, at which it will buy, and an ask, at which it will sell, and that trades with customers from its own inventory. In this lesson a dealer is a market maker in this sense only.
  25: Definition 1.2 (bid–ask spread)
  26: Let a dealer post bid \(b\) and ask \(a\), in whole cents, with \(a \ge b\). The quoted spread is \(s = a - b\), the mid is \(m = (a + b)/2\), and the half-spread is \(h = s/2\). In this lesson every quote is written as \(a = m + h\) and \(b = m - h\) with \(m\) and \(h\) whole numbers of cents. The quoted spread is then \(s = 2h\), an even number of cents, and every amount that follows is a whole number of cents.
  27: Remark 1.3 (units and the relative spread)
  28: The quoted spread is a price difference per share, in cents. Its relative size is \(s/m\), the quoted spread divided by the mid. Relative spread is a fraction of the mid and carries no currency unit. When a percentage is quoted, the basis is the quoted spread over the mid, as in Example 1.4.
  29: Example 1.4 (quotes at a mid of 5000 cents)
  30: Take \(m = 5000\) and \(h = 15\). The bid is \(b = 4985\) and the ask is \(a = 5015\), so \(s = 5015 - 4985 = 30\) cents. The relative spread is \(30/5000 = 0.006\), which is 0.6 percent of the mid, on the basis of the quoted spread over the mid. Both numbers are exact, with no approximation.
  31: 2Closed sequences and the spread identity
  32: Assumptions A1 to A3 (the dealer's trading model)
  33: A1 (trades at the quote). Each trade with a customer happens at the dealer's quote in force at that moment, and the dealer has no size limit. A customer buy happens at the ask \(a = m + h\), and a customer sell at the bid \(b = m - h\), for the mid \(m\) at that moment. The half-spread \(h\) is the same at every trade of the sequence, so the quote is not revised within it: the mid may move between trades, but \(h\) does not change.
  34: A2 (no other flows). The dealer has no fees, no interest on cash, no inventory cost and no information. Its cash and its shares are the only two quantities.
  35: A3 (one mid per trade). Trade \(t\) happens at mid \(m_t\), for \(t = 1, \dots, n\), and \(m_n\) denotes the mid at the last trade, called the terminal mid.
  36: Definition 2.1 (sequence, closed sequence, mark-to-mid profit)
  37: A trade \(t\) has a sign \(\sigma_t = +1\) when the customer buys, so the dealer sells, and \(\sigma_t = -1\) when the customer sells, so the dealer buys. It has a whole number of shares \(q_t \ge 1\). The dealer's cash is \(C = \sum_t \sigma_t q_t p_t\), where \(p_t = m_t + \sigma_t h\) is the price of trade \(t\) under A1. Its inventory is \(I = -\sum_t \sigma_t q_t\), and its total shares traded are \(V = \sum_t q_t\). A sequence is closed when \(I = 0\). The mark-to-mid profit is \(P = C + I\,m_n\), the cash plus the inventory valued at the terminal mid. The volume of a closed sequence is \(Q = \sum_{\sigma_t = +1} q_t\), the shares customers buy from the dealer.
  38: Remark 2.2 (a closed sequence buys and sells the same volume)
  39: If \(I = 0\), then \(\sum_t \sigma_t q_t = 0\), so the shares customers buy equal the shares they sell. The volume \(Q\) is therefore the shares bought by customers and also the shares sold to them, and \(V = 2Q\).
  40: Proposition 2.3 (mark-to-mid decomposition)
  41: Under A1 to A3, for any finite sequence of trades, not necessarily closed,
  42: \[ P = h\,V + \sum_t \sigma_t q_t\,(m_t - m_n). \]
  43: The first term is half the quoted spread times the total shares traded. The second term is the mid-move term.
  44: Proof
  45: Fix a trade \(t\) and write \(p_t = m_t + \sigma_t h\) as in Definition 2.1. Then \(\sigma_t q_t\,p_t - \sigma_t q_t\,m_n = \sigma_t q_t\,(p_t - m_n)\), and \(p_t - m_n = (m_t - m_n) + \sigma_t h\). Multiplying out and using \(\sigma_t^2 = 1\) gives \(\sigma_t q_t\,p_t - \sigma_t q_t\,m_n = \sigma_t q_t\,(m_t - m_n) + h\,q_t\). Summing over \(t\) gives \(C - m_n \sum_t \sigma_t q_t = \sum_t \sigma_t q_t\,(m_t - m_n) + hV\), since \(\sum_t q_t = V\). Because \(I = -\sum_t \sigma_t q_t\), the left side equals \(C + I\,m_n = P\). \(\blacksquare\)
  46: Corollary 2.4 (a closed sequence at a fixed mid)
  47: Under A1 to A3, suppose a sequence is closed, \(I = 0\), and every trade happens at the same mid \(m\). Then \(P = C = s\,Q\), the quoted spread times the volume of the customer buys.
  48: Proof
  49: Every \(m_t = m_n = m\), so the mid-move term in Proposition 2.3 is zero and \(P = hV\). By Remark 2.2, \(V = 2Q\), so \(P = h \cdot 2Q = sQ\), using \(s = 2h\). Since \(I = 0\), \(P = C\). \(\blacksquare\)
  50: Example 2.5 (a moving mid breaks the spread identity)
  51: Let \(m_1 = 10000\) and \(m_2 = 9990\), with \(h = 2\). First a customer sells 100 shares to the dealer at the bid \(9998\), so \(\sigma_1 = -1\) and \(q_1 = 100\). Then a customer buys 100 shares from the dealer at the ask \(9992\), so \(\sigma_2 = +1\) and \(q_2 = 100\). The sequence is closed and \(m_n = 9990\). The cash is \(C = -9998 \cdot 100 + 9992 \cdot 100 = -600\) cents, and \(P = C = -600\) cents. Proposition 2.3 gives the same number. The spread term is \(hV = 2 \cdot 200 = 400\) cents, and the mid-move term is \((-1)(100)(10000 - 9990) + (+1)(100)(9990 - 9990) = -1000\) cents. Then \(400 - 1000 = -600\). The dealer earns the spread and still loses, because the mid fell after it bought.
  52: Remark 2.6 (what the decomposition does and does not say)
  53: Proposition 2.3 is an identity for given trades and mids. It does not say that the mid-move term is small or that it has a fixed sign. Its sign follows from the trade sides and the path of the mid relative to the terminal mid. Lab 1 Part B evaluates the term for one seeded path. That path is simulated and says nothing about any market.
  54: 3Asks in a two-dealer tick game
  55: A tick is the smallest allowed step in price. This section uses a tick of one cent. The following game is a discrete version of the Bertrand competition named in the Uses line of this lesson. It is stated and proved here on its own terms.
  56: Assumptions B1 to B4 (the two-dealer game)
  57: B1 (two dealers). Two dealers, 1 and 2, each post one ask for a fixed demand.
  58: B2 (common cost). Each dealer has the same cost \(c\) per share, a whole number of cents.
  59: B3 (demand and tie rule). Customers demand \(N\) shares, with \(N\) a positive even integer. They buy all \(N\) from the dealer with the lower ask. If the asks are equal, the shares split equally, \(N/2\) each.
  60: B4 (integer asks). Each ask is a whole number of cents, at least \(c\).
  61: An ask profile \((a_1, a_2)\) is an equilibrium when no dealer has a deviation that strictly raises its own profit. A deviation is any whole-cent ask \(d \ge c\) by the same dealer.
  62: Definition 3.1 (profit in the game)
  63: Under B1 to B4, dealer \(i\) with ask \(a_i\) facing a rival ask \(a_j\) earns \(\pi_i = (a_i - c)N\) if \(a_i < a_j\), \(\pi_i = (a_i - c)N/2\) if \(a_i = a_j\), and \(\pi_i = 0\) if \(a_i > a_j\). Each value is a whole number of cents, because \(N\) is even.
  64: Proposition 3.2 (equilibrium asks in the tick game)
  65: Under B1 to B4, the equilibria of the game are exactly the three symmetric profiles \((c, c)\), \((c+1, c+1)\) and \((c+2, c+2)\). Each symmetric profile \((c+k, c+k)\) with \(k \ge 3\) is not an equilibrium, and no asymmetric profile is an equilibrium.
  66: Proof
  67: Asymmetric profiles. Let \(a_1 < a_2\). Then dealer 2 earns \(\pi_2 = 0\). If \(a_1 > c\), dealer 2 can deviate to \(a_1\) and earn \((a_1 - c)N/2 > 0\), a strict gain, so the profile is not an equilibrium. If \(a_1 = c\), then \(a_2 > c\) because \(a_1 < a_2\), and dealer 1 earns 0. Its deviation to \(a_2\) is a tie and earns \((a_2 - c)N/2 > 0\), a strict gain. So no asymmetric profile is an equilibrium.
  68: Symmetric profiles. Let both asks equal \(a = c + k\) with \(k \ge 0\). Each dealer earns \(kN/2\). Take a deviation to a whole-cent ask \(d \ge c\) by one dealer, with the rival still at \(a\). If \(d < a\), the deviator wins all \(N\) shares and earns \((d - c)N\). If \(d = a\), it ties and earns \(kN/2\). If \(d > a\), it earns 0. Deviations below \(c\) are excluded by B4.
  69: For \(k = 0\), the profit is 0 and every deviation earns at most 0, so \((c, c)\) is an equilibrium. For \(k = 1\), the profit is \(N/2\). A deviation to \(c\) earns 0, a deviation to \(c + 1\) earns \(N/2\), which is no strict gain, and a deviation above \(c + 1\) earns 0. So \((c+1, c+1)\) is an equilibrium. For \(k = 2\), the profit is \(N\). The largest deviation below \(a\) is \(c + 1\), which earns \((1)N = N\), no strict gain. So \((c+2, c+2)\) is an equilibrium.
  70: For \(k \ge 3\), the deviation \(d = a - 1 = c + k - 1\) wins all \(N\) shares and earns \((k - 1)N\). This is strictly more than \(kN/2\) exactly when \(2(k - 1) > k\), that is \(k > 2\). So \((c+k, c+k)\) is not an equilibrium. \(\blacksquare\)
  71: Remark 3.3 (the tick, not a theorem about continuous prices)
  72: Proposition 3.2 is about whole-cent asks in this game. Its equilibrium asks sit 0, 1 or 2 cents above cost. The result does not say what happens with prices on a finer grid, with more dealers, or with demand that depends on the price. Problem R1 treats three dealers, for symmetric profiles only.
  73: Lab 1 · Closed sequences, the spread identity and the tick game
  74: The lab generates SIMULATED customer flow from seed 2026 and checks three things before printing. Part A has a fixed mid of 10000 cents, with customers buying 1000 shares and selling 1000 shares, so the dealer should end with zero inventory and cash equal to the spread times the volume, \(4 \cdot 1000 = 4000\) cents, by Corollary 2.4. Part B lets the mid move by an integer step of -3 to 3 cents before each of 14 trades, so the mark-to-mid profit should equal the spread capture \(hV\) plus the mid-move term, by Proposition 2.3, with \(V = 2000\) shares. Part C enumerates asks from 100 to 110 cents for two dealers with cost 100 cents and demand 1000 shares. The equilibria in that grid should be the three symmetric pairs listed in Proposition 3.2, and the grid has \(11 \cdot 11 = 121\) pairs.
  75: """FC1 lab L03: a dealer's quotes, closed sequences of trades and the bid-ask spread, in integer cents.
  76: Standard library only; no network. The customer order flow is generated from a fixed seed, so it is SIMULATED
  77: and every run prints the same numbers. Quotes are ask = mid + HALF and bid = mid - HALF, with mid and HALF in
  78: whole cents, so the quoted spread is 2 * HALF cents and every amount below is an exact integer. Each check is exact
  79: integer arithmetic, not a tolerance test. Part A and Part B check the identity of Proposition 2.3 and Corollary 2.4.
  80: Part C enumerates the asks of the two-dealer tick game of Proposition 3.2 on a finite grid.
  81: """
  82: from __future__ import annotations
  83: import random
  84: from dataclasses import dataclass
  85: SEED = 2026
  86: HALF = 2
  87: MID = 10000
  88: COST = 100
  89: SHARES_PER_SIDE = 1000
  90: TICK_GRID = 10
  91: @dataclass(frozen=True)
  92: class Trade:
  93: """One trade with a customer. sign is +1 when the customer buys (the dealer sells at its ask), -1 when it sells."""
  94: sign: int
  95: shares: int
  96: mid: int
  97: def trade_price(trade: Trade, half: int) -> int:
  98: """Price in cents at which the dealer trades: mid + half for a customer buy, mid - half for a customer sell."""
  99: return trade.mid + trade.sign * half
 100: def dealer_cash(trades: list[Trade], half: int) -> int:
 101: """Cash the dealer receives in cents: the sum of sign * price * shares over the trades."""
 102: return sum(t.sign * trade_price(t, half) * t.shares for t in trades)
 103: def dealer_inventory(trades: list[Trade]) -> int:
 104: """Shares the dealer holds: a customer buy lowers it and a customer sell raises it."""
 105: return -sum(t.sign * t.shares for t in trades)
 106: def mark_to_mid(trades: list[Trade], half: int, terminal_mid: int) -> int:
 107: """Dealer cash plus the inventory valued at the terminal mid, in cents."""
 108: return dealer_cash(trades, half) + dealer_inventory(trades) * terminal_mid
 109: def spread_capture(trades: list[Trade], half: int) -> int:
 110: """Half the quoted spread times the total shares traded, in cents."""
 111: return half * sum(t.shares for t in trades)
 112: def mid_move_term(trades: list[Trade], terminal_mid: int) -> int:
 113: """The sum of sign * shares * (mid at the trade - terminal mid), in cents."""
 114: return sum(t.sign * t.shares * (t.mid - terminal_mid) for t in trades)
 115: def partition(rng: random.Random, total: int, parts: int) -> list[int]:
 116: """Split total whole shares into parts positive amounts at random cut points."""
 117: cuts = sorted(rng.sample(range(1, total), parts - 1))
 118: bounds = [0, *cuts, total]
 119: return [bounds[i + 1] - bounds[i] for i in range(parts)]
 120: def closed_sequence(rng: random.Random, mid: int) -> list[Trade]:
 121: """Trades at one fixed mid that buy and sell SHARES_PER_SIDE shares each, so inventory ends at zero."""
 122: buys = partition(rng, SHARES_PER_SIDE, 8)
 123: sells = partition(rng, SHARES_PER_SIDE, 6)
 124: trades = [Trade(1, q, mid) for q in buys] + [Trade(-1, q, mid) for q in sells]
 125: rng.shuffle(trades)
 126: return trades
 127: def walking_mid_sequence(rng: random.Random, start: int) -> tuple[list[Trade], int]:
 128: """The closed sequence with a mid that moves by an integer step in -3 to 3 cents before each trade.
 129: Returns the trades and the terminal mid, which is the mid at the last trade.
 130: """
 131: mid = start
 132: walked: list[Trade] = []
 133: for trade in closed_sequence(rng, start):
 134: mid += rng.randint(-3, 3)
 135: walked.append(Trade(trade.sign, trade.shares, mid))
 136: return walked, mid
 137: def profit_twice(ask: int, rival: int, cost: int, shares: int) -> int:
 138: """Twice the dealer's profit in cents when it asks ask against a rival ask.
 139: Customers buy all shares from the lower ask. A tie splits the shares equally, so the profit is halved;
 140: doubling keeps the arithmetic in whole cents.
 141: """
 142: if ask < rival:
 143: return 2 * (ask - cost) * shares
 144: if ask == rival:
 145: return (ask - cost) * shares
 146: return 0
 147: def equilibria(cost: int, shares: int, asks: list[int]) -> list[tuple[int, int]]:
 148: """Ask pairs from the grid at which neither dealer has a deviation in the grid that strictly raises its profit."""
 149: found: list[tuple[int, int]] = []
 150: for a in asks:
 151: for b in asks:
 152: first = all(profit_twice(d, b, cost, shares) <= profit_twice(a, b, cost, shares) for d in asks)
 153: second = all(profit_twice(d, a, cost, shares) <= profit_twice(b, a, cost, shares) for d in asks)
 154: if first and second:
 155: found.append((a, b))
 156: return found
 157: def main() -> None:
 158: rng = random.Random(SEED)
 159: spread = 2 * HALF
 160: trades = closed_sequence(rng, MID)
 161: buy_q = sum(t.shares for t in trades if t.sign == 1)
 162: sell_q = sum(t.shares for t in trades if t.sign == -1)
 163: cash = dealer_cash(trades, HALF)
 164: print(f"Part A, mid fixed at {MID} cents: {len(trades)} trades")
 165: print(f"customer buys {buy_q} shares and customer sells {sell_q} shares")
 166: print(f"dealer inventory after the sequence: {dealer_inventory(trades)} shares")
 167: print(f"cash received: {cash} cents; quoted spread {spread} cents times {buy_q} shares: {spread * buy_q} cents")
 168: print(f"cash equals spread times volume: {cash == spread * buy_q}")
 169: walked, terminal = walking_mid_sequence(rng, MID)
 170: capture = spread_capture(walked, HALF)
 171: move = mid_move_term(walked, terminal)
 172: pnl = mark_to_mid(walked, HALF, terminal)
 173: total = sum(t.shares for t in walked)
 174: print(f"Part B, mid moves between trades: {len(walked)} trades, terminal mid {terminal} cents")
 175: print(f"dealer inventory after the sequence: {dealer_inventory(walked)} shares, total shares {total}")
 176: print(f"spread capture, half the spread times total shares: {capture} cents")
 177: print(f"mid-move term, sum of sign * shares * (mid - terminal mid): {move} cents")
 178: print(f"mark-to-mid P&L: {pnl} cents")
 179: print(f"mark-to-mid P&L equals spread capture plus mid-move term: {pnl == capture + move}")
 180: asks = list(range(COST, COST + TICK_GRID + 1))
 181: found = equilibria(COST, SHARES_PER_SIDE, asks)
 182: listed = ", ".join(f"({a}, {b})" for a, b in found)
 183: print(f"Part C, tick game with cost {COST} cents, asks {COST} to {COST + TICK_GRID} cents: {len(asks) ** 2} pairs")
 184: print(f"pairs with no strictly profitable deviation in the grid: {listed}")
 185: print(f"every pair found is symmetric: {all(a == b for a, b in found)}")
 186: main()

 202: 4Where the canon breaks
 203: The spread is not guaranteed profit. Proposition 2.3 has a mid-move term. Example 2.5 gives a closed sequence with a positive spread capture and a negative profit. The identity holds for every path, but the sign of the total depends on the path.
 204: The volume identity is exact only under its conditions. Corollary 2.4 needs a closed sequence and one mid. Remark 2.2 gives \(V = 2Q\) only for a closed sequence, and Proposition 2.3 gives the profit without one mid.
 205: The model excludes four items. Assumption A2 sets fees, interest on cash, inventory cost and information to zero. In this model \(P\) is the whole profit. A cost that A2 excludes would be subtracted from it, and this lesson does not analyse such costs. The unit's market-structure source is Harris, Trading and Exchanges, cited here by its registry key harris2002trading with the registry year 2002. No passage of it is confirmed for this lesson, and no claim above is attributed to one.
 206: The tick game is a model of one tick size. Proposition 3.2 depends on B1 to B4. It does not describe more dealers, a finer grid, or elastic demand.
 207: 5Misconceptions
 208: “The spread is profit on every share.”
 209: The spread earns \(h\) per share against the mid of the same trade, and Proposition 2.3 adds a mid-move term to it. Example 2.5 gives a closed sequence whose spread capture is 400 cents and whose profit is \(-600\) cents, because its mid-move term is \(-1000\) cents (100 shares times the 10-cent gap between the mid at the first trade, 10000, and the terminal mid, 9990).
 210: “A dealer earns the spread times the number of trades.”
 211: The spread is earned per share, and Proposition 2.3 uses total shares \(V\), not trade count. A 100-share trade contributes 100 times \(h\) to the sum and a 1-share trade contributes \(h\). Counting trades instead of shares gives the wrong sum unless every trade is one share.
 212: “Two dealers with equal costs always end up asking at cost.”
 213: Proposition 3.2 shows that with a one-cent tick the asks \((c+1, c+1)\) and \((c+2, c+2)\) are equilibria. Each dealer earns \(N/2\) or \(N\) at those asks, and no deviation strictly raises that profit. In this model, the symmetric asks \((c+1, c+1)\) and \((c+2, c+2)\) are equilibria, and \((c, c)\) is also an equilibrium, with zero markup.

 216: Mechanics
 217: M1 · A closed sequence at a fixed mid
 218: Let \(m = 10000\) and \(h = 2\). A customer buys 50 shares, and another customer sells 50 shares, both at mid 10000, and the mid stays at 10000. Find the dealer's cash in cents and the value Corollary 2.4 gives for it.
 219: Solution
 220: The customer buys at the ask \(10002\), so the dealer receives \(10002 \cdot 50 = 500100\) cents. The customer sells at the bid \(9998\), so the dealer pays \(9998 \cdot 50 = 499900\) cents. The cash is \(500100 - 499900 = 200\) cents. Corollary 2.4 gives \(sQ = 4 \cdot 50 = 200\) cents, with \(s = 2h = 4\) and \(Q = 50\). The two numbers agree.
 221: M2 · Profit with a moving mid
 222: Let \(h = 2\). First a customer sells 80 shares at mid 10010, then a customer buys 80 shares at mid 10004. The terminal mid is \(m_n = 10004\). Find the cash, the mark-to-mid profit, and the two terms of Proposition 2.3.
 223: Solution
 224: The first trade is a customer sale, so the dealer pays the bid \(10008\) for 80 shares, a payment of \(800640\) cents. The second is a customer buy, so the dealer receives the ask \(10006\) for 80 shares, \(800480\) cents. The cash is \(800480 - 800640 = -160\) cents. The sequence is closed, so \(P = C = -160\) cents. Proposition 2.3 gives \(hV = 2 \cdot 160 = 320\) and the mid-move term \((-1)(80)(10010 - 10004) + (+1)(80)(10004 - 10004) = -480\). Then \(320 - 480 = -160\), the same value.
 225: M3 · Quoted and relative spread
 226: A dealer posts a mid of 5000 cents with half-spread 15 cents. Find the bid, the ask, the quoted spread, and the relative spread as a percentage of the mid, on the basis of the quoted spread over the mid.
 227: Solution
 228: The bid is \(5000 - 15 = 4985\) and the ask is \(5000 + 15 = 5015\). The quoted spread is \(s = 5015 - 4985 = 30\) cents. The relative spread is \(30/5000 = 0.006\), which is 0.6 percent of the mid. This is the same calculation as Example 1.4.
 229: Proof
 230: P1 · The mid-move term vanishes at the terminal mid
 231: Prove that if every trade of a sequence happens at the terminal mid, then \(P = hV\), whether or not the sequence is closed.
 232: Solution
 233: If \(m_t = m_n\) for every \(t\), each term \(\sigma_t q_t (m_t - m_n)\) is zero. Proposition 2.3 then gives \(P = hV\) directly. The argument uses no property of the inventory, so it holds for any sequence, closed or not.
 234: P2 · The terminal mid does not matter for a closed sequence
 235: Take a closed sequence with fixed trades and mids \(m_t\). Show that the profit \(P\) does not change when the terminal mid is replaced by another value \(m_n'\).
 236: Solution
 237: Definition 2.1 gives \(P = C + I\,m_n\). For a closed sequence \(I = 0\), so \(P = C\), and \(C\) does not involve \(m_n\). The same conclusion follows from Proposition 2.3: changing \(m_n\) by \(d\) changes the mid-move term by \(-d\sum_t \sigma_t q_t = dI\), which is zero when \(I = 0\).
 238: P3 · The tie at one cent above cost
 239: In the game of Section 3 with cost \(c\) and demand \(N\) a positive even integer, show that \((c+1, c+1)\) is an equilibrium, and that no deviation to any whole-cent ask \(d \ge c\) strictly raises the deviator's profit.
 240: Solution
 241: At \((c+1, c+1)\) each dealer earns \(N/2\). A deviation to \(d < c\) is excluded by B4. A deviation to \(d = c\) gives \(0\). A deviation to \(d = c + 1\) is the same tie and gives \(N/2\), which is no strict gain. Any deviation to \(d \ge c + 2\) loses to the rival and earns 0. No deviation earns more than \(N/2\), so the profile is an equilibrium.

 247: E2 · The sign of the mid-move term
 248: Lab 1 Part B prints a mid-move term of -545 cents for a seeded path. Explain from Proposition 2.3 what determines that number, and why it is not a forecast.
 249: Solution
 250: The printed term is the sum of \(\sigma_t q_t (m_t - m_n)\) over the 14 trades of the seeded path, where \(m_n\) is the terminal mid printed by the lab, 10001 cents. The number depends on which trades are sales or purchases, how large they are, and where the mid stood relative to its final value at each trade. It is a value of one seeded path. Remark 2.6 states that this path is simulated and says nothing about any market.
 251: E3 · Relative spread of a quote
 252: A dealer quotes a mid of 20000 cents with a quoted spread of 6 cents. Find the relative spread, on the basis of the quoted spread over the mid, as a percentage.
 253: Solution
 254: The relative spread is \(6/20000 = 0.0003\), which is 0.03 percent of the mid. The percentage is exact, with no approximation.
 255: Research
 256: R1 · Three dealers, symmetric profiles
 257: Three dealers face cost \(c\) and demand \(N\), a multiple of 3. The customers buy all \(N\) from the lowest ask, and equal lowest asks split equally three ways. Among symmetric profiles, show that \((c, c, c)\) and \((c+1, c+1, c+1)\) are equilibria and that \((c+2, c+2, c+2)\) is not.
 258: Solution
 259: At \((c, c, c)\) every dealer earns 0. A deviation to \(c+1\) wins nothing from the others, who ask \(c\), so it earns 0 as well. The profile is an equilibrium. At \((c+1, c+1, c+1)\) each dealer earns \(N/3\). A deviation to \(c\) earns 0, a deviation to \(c+2\) earns 0, and a deviation to \(c+1\) is a tie that earns \(N/3\), which is no strict gain, so the profile is an equilibrium. At \((c+2, c+2, c+2)\) each dealer earns \(2N/3\). The deviation to \(c+1\) wins all \(N\) shares and earns \(N > 2N/3\), a strict gain, so the profile is not an equilibrium. This treats symmetric profiles only.
 260: R2 · Changing only the mid path
 261: Keep the same shares and sides as Example 2.5, a sale of 100 shares followed by a purchase of 100 shares, with \(h = 2\). Reverse the mid path so that the sale happens at mid 9990 and the purchase at mid 10000. Find the cash, the spread term and the mid-move term, and state which term changes.
 262: Solution
 263: The sale at mid 9990 is at the bid \(9988\), so the dealer pays \(9988 \cdot 100 = 998800\) cents. The purchase at mid 10000 is at the ask \(10002\), so the dealer receives \(10002 \cdot 100 = 1000200\) cents. The cash is \(1400\) cents, and the sequence is closed, so \(P = 1400\). The spread term is \(hV = 2 \cdot 200 = 400\) cents, which is the same as in Example 2.5. The mid-move term is \((-1)(100)(9990 - 10000) + (+1)(100)(10000 - 10000) = +1000\) cents, and \(400 + 1000 = 1400\). Only the mid-move term changes, from \(-1000\) to \(+1000\) cents.
 264: R3 · What a finite grid can show
 265: Lab 1 Part C checks 121 ask pairs. Explain what that finite check establishes about Proposition 3.2, and what it leaves to the proof.
 266: Solution
 267: The check establishes that, among the 121 pairs with asks from \(c\) to \(c + 10\), the equilibria under the strict-gain rule are exactly the three symmetric profiles. That is evidence about this finite set. The grid does not cover asks above \(c + 10\), and it cannot show that the symmetric profiles with \(k \ge 3\) fail for every \(k\). The proof of Proposition 3.2 covers every whole-cent ask at or above cost, through the deviation \(d = a - 1\) for \(k \ge 3\) and the case analysis for \(k \le 2\).
