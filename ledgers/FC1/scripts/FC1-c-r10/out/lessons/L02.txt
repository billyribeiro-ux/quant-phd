Markets, Exchanges and the Instrument Taxonomy

Introduces: exchange-traded and over-the-counter markets, and the instrument taxonomy across asset classes

Ttheorem

Bpublished, not settled

Dlore

An instrument is a claim with terms, and this lesson sorts each trade by one of two ways of setting those terms. Some trades run on a venue that fixes the rules for the instruments it lists. Others are negotiated directly between two parties. Before a student can classify an instrument or follow a trade, the classification has to be exact. This lesson defines the two kinds of market, sets out a three-axis taxonomy of instruments, and proves the counting rule that makes the taxonomy usable in a register (Definition 2.1). The register in the lab is typed in and describes no real market.

1Two ways to set the terms

A market is a set of arrangements by which buyers and sellers meet and agree a price and the terms of a trade. This lesson is concerned with one feature of that arrangement: who fixes the terms. The definition below draws the line on that feature alone.

Definition 1.1 (exchange-traded and over-the-counter)

A trade is exchange-traded when it is executed on a venue that fixes one set of trading rules and the standard terms of the instruments it lists. A trade is over-the-counter (OTC) when it is made bilaterally: the two parties negotiate its terms directly, and no venue fixes them.

Remark 1.2 (a classification of one feature)

Definition 1.1 says nothing about size, about who the parties are, or about how the price is formed. Those are separate questions, and this lesson does not answer them. The distinction is only about who sets the terms.

Assumption 1.3 (each trade has one venue type)

Every trade in the classification is executed either on a venue of Definition 1.1 or bilaterally, and not both. A set of trades is classified when each of its trades is. A trade with a venue that partly fixes its terms is not classified by this lesson, and the lesson makes no claim about such trades.

Proposition 1.4 (the venue split counts every trade once)

Under Assumption 1.3, for any finite set of \(n\) trades each of which is classified by Assumption 1.3, the number executed on exchanges plus the number executed OTC equals \(n\).

Proof

Let \(E\) be the set of exchange-traded trades and \(O\) the set of OTC trades. Each trade of the set is classified by Assumption 1.3, so it lies in exactly one of the two sets, and so \(E\) and \(O\) are disjoint and their union is the whole set of \(n\) trades. The cardinality of a disjoint union is the sum of the cardinalities, so \(|E| + |O| = n\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.5 (what the split leaves out)

Proposition 1.4 is bookkeeping. It holds for any set of trades each of which is classified by Assumption 1.3. It does not say how many trades take each venue type in any market, and it does not say which venue type is larger.

2The three-axis instrument taxonomy

An instrument is classified along three axes. Each axis is a list of values, and the rules for one entry are stated in Definition 2.1. The module uses a taxonomy with three axes, not a single list, for a reason given in Remark 2.2.

Definition 2.1 (the instrument taxonomy)

The instrument taxonomy of this module has three axes.

Exposure. The economic risk that the instrument's payoff depends on. The five values are equities, rates, credit, FX and commodities.

Form. Cash when the payoff is the holding of the claim itself, and derivative when the payoff is fixed by reference to another quantity or contract. The two values are cash and derivative.

Venue. The venue type of Definition 1.1: exchange or OTC.

An instrument's classification has exactly one value on each axis. An entry that has two values on one axis, or none, is not a classification under this definition. A register is a finite list of entries, each an instrument with its classification.

Remark 2.2 (why form is its own axis)

A single list that put derivatives beside the exposure classes would show only one of the two properties. This lesson treats form as a second axis, because a derivative has an exposure too. A futures contract on a government bond depends on bond prices and so has rates exposure, and it is also a derivative. Putting it under one heading would hide either the exposure or the form.

Remark 2.3 (the list is a convention)

The five exposure values are the module's list. The lesson does not claim that every instrument falls under one of them, and no result below depends on the list being complete. A different list would change which entries are counted under which heading, and it would need its own register.

Example 2.4 (classifying one instrument)

A futures contract on crude oil, traded on an exchange, is classified as follows. Its exposure is commodities, since its payoff depends on the price of oil. Its form is derivative, since its payoff is fixed by reference to the oil price and the contract. Its venue is exchange, by the terms of the example. Each axis has one value, so the classification meets Definition 2.1.

Proposition 2.5 (the counts on each axis reconcile)

Let a register hold \(n\) entries, each of which meets Definition 2.1. For each axis, the counts of entries over the values of that axis sum to \(n\).

Proof

Fix an axis. Each entry has exactly one value on that axis, and each value on that axis is one of the listed values. So each entry is counted under exactly one value, and the counts over the listed values partition the \(n\) entries. Their sum is \(n\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.6 (where the hypothesis is used)

The proof uses the rule that an entry has exactly one value on each axis. An entry with two exposures is counted twice on the exposure axis, so the total exceeds \(n\) by one less than the number of exposure values that entry carries. Lab 2, Part D, shows that the check rejects such an entry.

Lab 2 · A consistency check on a typed-in register

Predicted from Definition 2.1 and Proposition 2.5 before running. Part A: all 12 entries of the typed-in register are well formed. Part B: the counts on each of the three axes reconcile to 12, with exposure counts 3, 3, 2, 2 and 2, form counts 6 and 6, and venue counts 4 and 8. Part C: the four exchange entries include one cash entry and three derivatives, and they span three exposure classes. Part D: a deliberately malformed entry with two exposures is rejected, and the exposure total then exceeds the entry count by one. This is a consistency check on bookkeeping. The register is typed in, and no input is drawn from data, so no seed is used.

"""FC1 lab L02: a consistency check on a typed-in instrument register.

This is a consistency check on bookkeeping, not evidence about any market. The register below is typed in for the
lab. Each entry is given three classifications, one per axis: exposure (the economic risk its payoff depends on),
form (cash or derivative) and venue (exchange or over-the-counter). The venue assigned to an entry is an illustration
of the classification, not a statement about where that instrument trades. The checks test the register's own counts:
each entry has exactly one value on each axis, the counts on each axis reconcile to the number of entries, and a
deliberately malformed entry is rejected. No random numbers; standard library only; no network.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from collections import Counter

EXPOSURES = ("equities", "rates", "credit", "fx", "commodities")
FORMS = ("cash", "derivative")
VENUES = ("exchange", "otc")
AXES: dict[str, tuple[str, ...]] = {"exposure": EXPOSURES, "form": FORMS, "venue": VENUES}

Entry = tuple[str, dict[str, tuple[str, ...]]]

REGISTER: list[Entry] = [
("share listed on an exchange", {"exposure": ("equities",), "form": ("cash",), "venue": ("exchange",)}),
("share sold in a private negotiated trade", {"exposure": ("equities",), "form": ("cash",), "venue": ("otc",)}),
(
"futures contract on an equity index",
{"exposure": ("equities",), "form": ("derivative",), "venue": ("exchange",)},
),
("bond with a fixed coupon", {"exposure": ("rates",), "form": ("cash",), "venue": ("otc",)}),
(
"futures contract on a government bond",
{"exposure": ("rates",), "form": ("derivative",), "venue": ("exchange",)},
),
("interest rate swap", {"exposure": ("rates",), "form": ("derivative",), "venue": ("otc",)}),
("corporate bond", {"exposure": ("credit",), "form": ("cash",), "venue": ("otc",)}),
("credit default swap", {"exposure": ("credit",), "form": ("derivative",), "venue": ("otc",)}),
("spot purchase of a currency", {"exposure": ("fx",), "form": ("cash",), "venue": ("otc",)}),
("forward contract on a currency", {"exposure": ("fx",), "form": ("derivative",), "venue": ("otc",)}),
("futures contract on crude oil", {"exposure": ("commodities",), "form": ("derivative",), "venue": ("exchange",)}),
("physical quantity of a metal", {"exposure": ("commodities",), "form": ("cash",), "venue": ("otc",)}),
]

def well_formed(entry: Entry) -> bool:
"""True when the entry has exactly one value on each axis and that value is an allowed one."""
_, values = entry
if set(values) != set(AXES):
return False
return all(len(values[axis]) == 1 and values[axis][0] in allowed for axis, allowed in AXES.items())

def axis_counts(entries: list[Entry], axis: str) -> Counter[str]:
"""Count the entries carrying each value on one axis (an entry with two values counts under both)."""
counts: Counter[str] = Counter()
for _, values in entries:
counts.update(values.get(axis, ()))
return counts

def main() -> None:
print("A. Each entry has exactly one value on each axis")
results = [well_formed(entry) for entry in REGISTER]
print(f"entries in the register: {len(REGISTER)}; well formed: {sum(results)}")
print("every entry is well formed:", all(results))

print()
print("B. Counts on each axis reconcile to the number of entries")
for axis, allowed in AXES.items():
counts = axis_counts(REGISTER, axis)
line = ", ".join(f"{value} {counts[value]}" for value in allowed)
total = sum(counts[value] for value in allowed)
print(f"{axis}: {line}; total {total}; reconciles: {total == len(REGISTER)}")

print()
print("C. The venue axis is separate from the form axis: exchange entries by form")
exchange = [entry for entry in REGISTER if entry[1]["venue"] == ("exchange",)]
by_form = Counter(entry[1]["form"][0] for entry in exchange)
print(f"exchange entries: {len(exchange)}; cash {by_form['cash']}; derivative {by_form['derivative']}")
exposures = sorted({entry[1]["exposure"][0] for entry in exchange})
print(f"exposure classes with at least one exchange entry: {len(exposures)} ({', '.join(exposures)})")

print()
print("D. A malformed entry is rejected by the same check")
malformed: Entry = (
"entry with two exposures",
{"exposure": ("rates", "credit"), "form": ("cash",), "venue": ("otc",)},
)
print("malformed entry rejected:", not well_formed(malformed))
extended = [*REGISTER, malformed]
total_with_malformed = sum(axis_counts(extended, "exposure").values())
print(f"exposure total with the malformed entry added: {total_with_malformed} for {len(extended)} entries")
print("the exposure total no longer reconciles to the entry count:", total_with_malformed != len(extended))

main()

entries in the register: 12; well formed: 12
every entry is well formed: True

B. Counts on each axis reconcile to the number of entries
exposure: equities 3, rates 3, credit 2, fx 2, commodities 2; total 12; reconciles: True
form: cash 6, derivative 6; total 12; reconciles: True
venue: exchange 4, otc 8; total 12; reconciles: True

C. The venue axis is separate from the form axis: exchange entries by form
exchange entries: 4; cash 1; derivative 3
exposure classes with at least one exchange entry: 3 (commodities, equities, rates)

D. A malformed entry is rejected by the same check
malformed entry rejected: True
exposure total with the malformed entry added: 14 for 13 entries

This lab checks the register's own counts. It is a consistency check, not evidence about any market: the venue given to each register entry illustrates the classification and is not a statement about where that instrument trades. Part B shows that the counts reconcile, which is what Proposition 2.5 requires. Part C shows that the exchange entries include both cash and derivative forms and span three exposure classes, and it says nothing about the proportion of derivatives on any exchange. Part D shows that the check does its job on a bad entry.

3Reading a classification

The taxonomy is used in two directions. Given an instrument, the classification states its three values. Given a register, the counts state how the entries spread across the values. The section fixes how a classification is read, using the entries of Lab 2.

Example 3.1 (the four combinations of form and venue)

The register of Lab 2 contains entries with each of the four combinations of form and venue: entries 1, 3, 4 and 6 of the register in the lab code, read off by hand, not printed by the lab. A share listed on an exchange is cash on an exchange. A futures contract on an equity index is a derivative on an exchange. A bond with a fixed coupon is cash OTC. An interest rate swap is a derivative OTC. The combinations show that the form and venue axes can be read independently. A register with only one combination would not show that.

Remark 3.2 (a classification is not a market statistic)

A count in a register is a count of the entries the register holds. Lab 2 holds twelve illustrative entries, chosen to show each value of each axis. Its counts describe that register, and they say nothing about how many instruments of each kind exist or trade.

4Where the canon breaks

The venue split and the taxonomy are exact inside their definitions and conventions outside them. Four places where the reader must act:

The venue split is binary by assumption. Assumption 1.3 requires that each trade be on a venue or bilateral. A venue that fixes only part of the terms falls outside Definition 1.1, and the results of Section 1 do not cover it.

The exposure list is the module's choice. Five exposure values are used here. Remark 2.3 says what changes if the list changes: the entries move under different headings and the register must be rebuilt. No result of the lesson shows that the list is the right one.

The taxonomy does not describe the market. It says how an instrument is labelled. It does not say how the instrument is priced, how its trades are matched, or how they are settled. Lesson 4 defines the order types and the limit order book, and Lesson 5 defines clearing and settlement. Until then, this lesson says nothing about those steps.

The lab is a register check. Part B's reconciliation is an identity of counting, guaranteed by Proposition 2.5 for any register that meets Definition 2.1. It tests the bookkeeping of the typed-in register and not the composition of any market.

5Misconceptions

“An OTC trade has no price.”

Definition 1.1 concerns who fixes the terms, not whether a price exists. In an OTC trade the parties agree a price as part of the terms they negotiate. What makes the trade OTC is that no venue fixes those terms. The definition is the test, and it is stated on this page, so the misreading can be checked against it.

“Derivatives are a separate asset class, so they have no exposure of their own.”

Remark 2.2 gives the counter-example: a futures contract on a government bond is a derivative with rates exposure. Lab 2 lists six derivative entries, Part B prints that count, and they carry all five exposure values (entries 3, 5, 6, 8, 10 and 11 of the register in the lab code, read by hand); rates carries two of them, the futures contract on a government bond and the interest rate swap. The form axis and the exposure axis are separate.

“Exchange-traded instruments are all equities.”

The venue of Definition 1.1 is a separate axis from the exposure, so the venue does not fix the exposure. Lab 2, Part C, prints that the four exchange entries span three exposure classes: commodities, equities and rates. So the belief fails for this register.

6Problem set

Attempt each problem before opening its solution. Problems marked “Lab 2” use the outputs printed above.

Mechanics

M1 · Classify an instrument

Classify a futures contract on a government bond, traded on an exchange, on the three axes of Definition 2.1.

Solution

Exposure is rates, because the payoff depends on bond prices. Form is derivative, because the payoff is fixed by reference to the contract's underlying bond. Venue is exchange. The entry has one value on each axis, so it meets Definition 2.1.

M2 · Count the exposures

A register holds five entries with exposures rates, rates, credit, FX and equities. Give the count on each exposure value and check that the counts reconcile.

Solution

The counts are rates 2, credit 1, FX 1, equities 1, and commodities 0. Their sum is 5, which equals the number of entries, as Proposition 2.5 requires. The commodities value has a count of zero, which is allowed, since a value with no entries still appears on the axis.

M3 · A malformed entry

An entry has exposure rates and form cash, but no venue. Is it a classification under Definition 2.1? Give the reason.

Solution

No. Definition 2.1 requires exactly one value on each axis, and this entry has none on the venue axis. Its counts would be missing from the venue total, so the venue counts would not reconcile to the number of entries.

Proof

P1 · Proposition 2.5 for one axis

Let \(n\) entries each have exactly one value on the form axis, which takes the values cash and derivative. Prove that the two counts sum to \(n\).

Solution

Let \(c\) be the number of cash entries and \(v\) the number of derivative entries. Each entry is counted under exactly one of the two values, because it has exactly one form value and that value is cash or derivative. Hence the entries split into two disjoint groups, so \(c + v = n\). This is the proof of Proposition 2.5 applied to one axis.

P2 · The counterexample to double counting

Let a trade be recorded as both exchange-traded and OTC. Show that Proposition 1.4 then fails.

Solution

Assumption 1.3 excludes this case. If it were allowed, the trade would be counted in both the exchange set and the OTC set, so \(|E| + |O|\) would equal \(n + 1\) for a set of \(n\) trades containing that one trade. The identity \(|E| + |O| = n\) then fails by one, which shows that Assumption 1.3 is needed.

P3 · Two exposures in one entry

Suppose an entry has exposure set \(S\) with \(|S| \ge 2\), and the register otherwise satisfies Definition 2.1. Show that the exposure total exceeds the number of entries by at least \(|S| - 1\).

Solution

The exposure total counts the entry once for each exposure value it carries, so it contributes \(|S|\) rather than 1. Every other entry contributes 1, as Proposition 2.5 requires. The total is therefore \(n + (|S| - 1)\), which exceeds \(n\) by \(|S| - 1 \ge 1\).

Empirical

E1 · The venue counts

Using only the printed output of Lab 2, Part B, give the number of OTC entries and the number of exchange entries, and their sum.

Solution

Part B prints venue exchange 4 and otc 8, with a total of 12. The sum is 12, which equals the number of entries, so the venue counts reconcile as Proposition 2.5 requires.

E2 · The derivative share of the exchange entries

Using only the printed output of Lab 2, Part C, find the share of the exchange entries that are derivatives. State the base.

Solution

The base is the four exchange entries that Part C prints. Three of them are derivatives, so the share is 3 out of 4, or 75 percent. The base is the exchange entries and not all twelve entries of the register.

E3 · The exposure classes on the exchange

Using only the printed output of Lab 2, Part C, name the exposure classes that carry an exchange entry.

Solution

Part C prints three exposure classes with at least one exchange entry: commodities, equities and rates. Credit and FX have no exchange entry in this register. The printed list is a fact about the register and not about any market.

Research

R1 · A finer exposure list

Split the rates exposure into two values, short rates and long rates. State which of the twelve register entries would change, and whether Proposition 2.5 still holds.

Solution

The entries with rates exposure are the bond with a fixed coupon, the futures contract on a government bond and the interest rate swap, so three entries would need a sub-class. The rule that each entry has exactly one value is unchanged, so Proposition 2.5 still holds with the new list, provided every rates entry is given one of the two sub-values. The split is a choice, and the lesson does not say which split is right.

R2 · A venue that partly fixes terms

Describe a venue that fixes some terms of its instruments and leaves others to the parties. Explain whether it fits Definition 1.1, and what Assumption 1.3 would need to say to include it.

Solution

Such a venue fixes some terms, which is part of the exchange test, and leaves others to the parties, which is part of the OTC test. It fits neither clause of Definition 1.1 in full. To include it, Assumption 1.3 would need a third venue type, or a rule that assigns the trade by the terms the venue fixes. Either change would also require a new proof of Proposition 1.4.

R3 · A derivative with two exposures

Give an example of a derivative whose payoff depends on two exposures at once, and state how it would be classified under Definition 2.1.

Solution

A contract whose payoff is the product of an FX rate and an equity price depends on both the FX and the equity exposures. Under Definition 2.1 it has exactly one exposure value, so the classification must pick one, or the register must add a rule for multi-exposure entries. The second route changes Proposition 2.5, which assumes one value per entry, so a new proof would be needed.
