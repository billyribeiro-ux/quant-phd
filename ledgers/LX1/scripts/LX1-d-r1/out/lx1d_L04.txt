  14 A rate held at a parity does not move, so a model of that rate has no month-to-month dispersion. A recorded rate is not a model, and its monthly changes are generally nonzero. This lesson defines the model class that the name Bretton Woods system refers to here, proves that the model has zero dispersion, and then measures the dispersion of one recorded column, the dollar price of sterling, in two windows. The recorded column is not zero, so it is not the model. The lesson says what that comparison does and does not show. It states no fact about the historical arrangement, because no source for that arrangement is in the course's reference list yet.
  15 1Monthly log changes and dispersion
  16 Lab 1 measures a recorded series, so it needs a measure of how far the series moves from month to month. This section defines that measure and proves the identity that Lab 1 checks on the data.
  17 Definition 1.1 (monthly log change, dispersion)
  18 Let \(x_t > 0\) be a rate at each month \(t\). The [DFN ]monthly log change at month \(t\) is \(\ell_t = \ln(x_t / x_{t-1})\), the natural logarithm of the ratio of two consecutive rates. It is a change per month and is not annualized. A window of \(n\) monthly log changes has [DFN ]dispersion \(s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k}(\ell_k - \overline{\ell})^{2}}\), the sample standard deviation of the \(n\) changes with denominator \(n-1\), where \(\overline{\ell}\) is their mean. The dispersion is defined only when \(n \ge 2\).
  19 Log changes add across months, and simple changes do not add in general. A rise of 10 percent followed by a rise of 10 percent has simple changes that sum to 20 percent, but the total change is 21 percent, because \(1.1 \times 1.1 = 1.21\). Proposition 1.2 gives the additive property of log changes.
  20 Proposition 1.2 (telescoping of log changes)
  21 Assuming (A1): the rates \(x_t\) are positive for every month \(t\) from \(a\) to \(b\), where \(a < b\). Then \(\displaystyle\sum_{t=a+1}^{b} \ell_t = \ln\frac{x_b}{x_a}\). The sum has \(b-a\) terms.
  22 Proof
  23 By (A1), each ratio \(x_t / x_{t-1}\) is positive, and the logarithm of a quotient is the difference of the logarithms. So \(\ell_t = \ln x_t - \ln x_{t-1}\) for each \(t\) with \(a < t \le b\). Summing over \(t = a+1, \dots, b\), each interior term \(\ln x_t\) with \(a < t < b\) appears once with a plus sign, in \(\ell_t\), and once with a minus sign, in \(\ell_{t+1}\), so these terms cancel. What remains is \(\ln x_b - \ln x_a = \ln(x_b / x_a)\). \(\blacksquare\)
  24 Remark 1.3 (exact in real numbers, checked to a tolerance)
  25 Proposition 1.2 is an exact identity in real numbers. Lab 1 checks it in floating-point arithmetic, to a tolerance of \(10^{-12}\). That check confirms the floating-point computation to that tolerance, and nothing more.
  26 2A fixed-parity model
  27 A Bretton Woods system, in this lesson, is a model class. The name is used for the class only. Definition 2.1 states what the model contains. It does not state what any historical arrangement had: its parities, its participants or its end. No source for that arrangement is in the course's reference list, so this lesson states none, and Section 4 lists what is left open.
  28 Definition 2.1 (Bretton Woods system, as a model)
  29 The model has an [DFN ]anchor currency, in which every rate is quoted, and one foreign currency. Its [DFN ]parity is a number \(\kappa > 0\) of anchor units per unit of the foreign currency. Its market rate at each month \(t\) is \(x_t > 0\), in the same units. A [DFN def-bretton-woods-system]Bretton Woods system, in this lesson, is this model together with Assumption (A2).
  30 Assumption (A2), parity held. At every month \(t\) of the window, \(x_t = \kappa\).
  31 Proposition 2.2 (zero dispersion in the model)
  32 (A2) as stated above, for the model of Definition 2.1, with \(\kappa > 0\), and with a window of \(n \ge 2\) monthly changes, so that the rates are defined at the window's first date and at each later date.
  33 Assuming (A2): (a) every monthly log change in the window is \(0\); (b) the dispersion of the window is \(0\).
  34 Proof
  35 (a) By (A2), \(x_t = \kappa\) and \(x_{t-1} = \kappa\) for each change in the window. So \(\ell_t = \ln(\kappa / \kappa) = \ln 1 = 0\), using \(\ln 1 = 0\).
  36 (b) Every change is \(0\), so their mean \(\overline{\ell}\) is \(0\), each deviation \(\ell_k - \overline{\ell}\) is \(0\), and the dispersion is \(\sqrt{0 / (n-1)} = 0\). The denominator \(n-1\) is at least \(1\), because \(n \ge 2\). \(\blacksquare\)
  37 Remark 2.3 (what Proposition 2.2 does not say)
  38 Proposition 2.2 is a statement about the model. It says nothing about a recorded rate, which need not equal its parity at any month. Section 3 measures a recorded column against the model. It does not derive the column from the model. The parity here plays the part that the gold parity plays in the gold standard of L02. This model contains no gold and no metal, and nothing in Proposition 2.2 depends on either.

  46 4Where the canon breaks
  47 Proposition 2.2 holds in the model whatever the data show. The comparison in Lab 1 is between that model and one recorded column, and the following limits apply to it.
  48 One foreign currency, one anchor. The model has one foreign currency. Lab 1 reads one column. The dispersion of one column does not describe how several currencies move together, and no statement here covers a second currency.
  49 Spot rates only. The snapshot holds spot rates and no interest rates or forward rates. The lab cannot test a relation between interest rates and exchange rates, such as covered interest parity, which this lesson names but does not state or test.
  50 Monthly sampling. Each value is the last noon rate of its month. A monthly dispersion does not show the path inside a month, and the lab computes no daily figure.
  51 The window start is a convention. Window two starts where the other embedded snapshots begin. A different start gives a different dispersion. The lab does not test whether the difference between the two windows survives another start.
  52 No lender of last resort. Proposition 2.2 contains no central bank. A lender of last resort lends against collateral to institutions that are solvent but illiquid. The model has no such lender and no lending, so nothing in this lesson measures a lender's effect on any rate.
  53 The historical arrangement. The lesson states no fact about the parities, participants, dates or end of the Bretton Woods arrangement. The course's reference list has no source for them yet. Any dated account of the system's end needs a source that is admitted to that list first.

  55 “A currency with a parity has no monthly change in its recorded rate.”
  56 Proposition 2.2 gives zero change under (A2), and (A2) describes the model, not a recorded series. Lab 1, Part B, counts three exactly zero changes among 668 in window one, so 665 changes are nonzero in the recorded GBP column.
  57 “A smaller dispersion in window two shows that the currency became calmer because of a regime change.”
  58 Part D prints a difference of -0.003236 between two sample standard deviations of one column. The lab computes no test and attaches no inference to the difference. The window start is a convention, and the lab names no cause for any change.
  59 “The monthly log change and the simple percent change are the same number.”
  60 For a rise from 1.00 to 1.10, the simple change is 0.10 exactly, and the log change is \(\ln 1.1 = 0.095310\) to six decimals. The two differ by 0.004690 (Problem M1 gives the calculation).
  61 “Calling the model a Bretton Woods system says what the historical arrangement was.”
  62 Definition 2.1 defines a model class. This lesson states no historical fact about the arrangement's parities, participants or end, and Section 4 lists the open items.

  65 Mechanics
  66 M1 · A log change and a simple change
  67 A rate moves from 1.2500 to 1.3750 dollars per pound in one month. Compute the monthly log change and the simple percent change, each to six decimals, and state their difference.
  68 Solution
  69 The ratio is \(1.3750 / 1.2500 = 1.1\). The log change is \(\ell = \ln 1.1 = 0.0953101798\ldots\), which is 0.095310 to six decimals. The simple change is \(1.1 - 1 = 0.100000\) exactly. The difference, computed from the unrounded values, is \(0.1 - 0.0953101798\ldots = 0.0046898\ldots\), which is 0.004690 to six decimals.
  70 M2 · The dispersion of three changes
  71 A window holds three monthly log changes, 0.01, 0.03 and -0.02. Compute their mean and their dispersion with denominator \(n-1\), each to six decimals.
  72 Solution
  73 The mean is \(0.02 / 3 = 1/150 = 0.006667\) to six decimals. The sum of squares of the changes is \(0.0001 + 0.0009 + 0.0004 = 0.0014\). The sum of squared deviations is \(0.0014 - 3 (1/150)^{2} = 0.0014 - 0.000133\ldots = 19/15000\). With \(n - 1 = 2\), the dispersion is \(\sqrt{19/30000} = 0.025166\) to six decimals.
  74 M3 · Counting the changes in a window
  75 How many monthly log changes does a window from 2000-01 to 2026-09 contain, and which rate does its first change use?
  76 Solution
  77 Each month from 2000-01 to 2026-09 carries one change, dated by that month. The months from 2000-01 to 2025-12 are 26 full years, which is \(26 \times 12 = 312\) months. The months from 2026-01 to 2026-09 are 9 more, so the window has \(312 + 9 = 321\) changes. The first change is dated 2000-01 and uses the rate of 1999-12, the month before the window. Lab 1, Part C, prints 321 changes for window two.
  78 Proof
  79 P1 · Telescoping by induction
  80 Prove Proposition 1.2 for a fixed starting month \(a\) by induction on \(b\), and state the step where (A1) is used.
  81 Solution
  82 Base case \(b = a + 1\): the sum has one term, \(\ell_{a+1} = \ln x_{a+1} - \ln x_a = \ln(x_{a+1} / x_a)\). Inductive step: assume \(\sum_{t=a+1}^{b} \ell_t = \ln(x_b / x_a)\) for some \(b \ge a + 1\). Adding \(\ell_{b+1} = \ln x_{b+1} - \ln x_b\) gives \(\ln x_b - \ln x_a + \ln x_{b+1} - \ln x_b = \ln(x_{b+1} / x_a)\), since the \(\ln x_b\) terms cancel. Assumption (A1) is used at every step, because the logarithms of \(x_t\) exist only for positive rates.
  83 P2 · A band bound on the log change
  84 Assume (A3): for some \(\kappa > 0\) and some \(h\) with \(0 \le h < 1\), the rate satisfies \(\kappa(1-h) \le x_t \le \kappa(1+h)\) at every month \(t\) of the window. Prove that \(\lvert \ell_t \rvert \le \ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\) for each change in the window, and state the bound when \(h = 0\).
  85 Solution
  86 Take a change dated \(t\). Upper bound on the ratio: \(x_t \le \kappa(1+h)\) and \(x_{t-1} \ge \kappa(1-h) > 0\), since \(h < 1\). So \(x_t / x_{t-1} \le (1+h)/(1-h)\). Lower bound: \(x_t \ge \kappa(1-h)\) and \(x_{t-1} \le \kappa(1+h)\), so \(x_t / x_{t-1} \ge (1-h)/(1+h)\). The logarithm is increasing, so \(\ln\frac{1-h}{1+h} \le \ell_t \le \ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\). Since \(\ln\frac{1-h}{1+h} = -\ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\), this is \(\lvert \ell_t \rvert \le \ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\). When \(h = 0\) the bound is \(\ln 1 = 0\), which is the conclusion of Proposition 2.2.
  87 P3 · The log change is at most the simple change
  88 Prove that \(\ln(1+u) \le u\) for every \(u > -1\), with equality only at \(u = 0\). Deduce that \(\ell_t \le x_t / x_{t-1} - 1\) for every positive pair of consecutive rates.
  89 Solution
  90 Let \(f(u) = \ln(1+u) - u\) on \((-1, \infty)\). Then \(f(0) = 0\) and \(f'(u) = \frac{1}{1+u} - 1 = -\frac{u}{1+u}\). On \((-1, 0)\) the derivative is positive, so \(f\) is strictly increasing there, and \(f(u) < f(0) = 0\). On \((0, \infty)\) the derivative is negative, so \(f\) is strictly decreasing there, and \(f(u) < f(0) = 0\). Hence \(f(u) < 0\) for \(u \ne 0\) and \(f(0) = 0\). For the deduction, put \(u = x_t / x_{t-1} - 1 > -1\), so that \(\ell_t = \ln(1+u) \le u\).

 104 Research
 105 R1 · A test and its assumptions
 106 State a test of whether the dispersion of window two differs from that of window one. Name the statistic, name every assumption it needs, and say which of those assumptions Lab 1 checks.
 107 Solution
 108 A natural statistic is the ratio of the two sample variances, \(s_2^{2} / s_1^{2}\), with the window-two variance in the numerator. A null distribution for this ratio depends on assumptions about the changes, such as their distribution and their independence across months. This lesson does not derive that null distribution, and no p-value is reported here. Lab 1 checks none of these assumptions. It checks the telescoping identity and the exact-zero tests of Parts A and B. A test that reports a p-value needs the null distribution derived or cited from a source admitted to the reference list first.
 109 R2 · The band implied by the largest move
 110 Window one has a largest absolute monthly log change of 0.135846 (Lab 1, Part C). Under (A3), find the smallest half-width \(h\) for which the bound of Problem P2 allows a change of that size. Give \(h\) in closed form and evaluate it to four decimals.
 111 Solution
 112 Set \(\ln\frac{1+h}{1-h} = L\), where \(L = 0.135846\ldots\) is the unrounded change. Then \(\frac{1+h}{1-h} = e^{L}\), so \(1 + h = e^{L}(1 - h)\), which gives \(h = \frac{e^{L}-1}{e^{L}+1} = \tanh\frac{L}{2}\). The function \(h \mapsto \ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\) is strictly increasing on \([0, 1)\), because its derivative is \(\frac{2}{1-h^{2}} > 0\), so this \(h\) is the smallest one whose bound reaches \(L\). Evaluating, \(\tanh(0.0679228\ldots) = 0.067819\ldots\), so the exact value is 0.067819 to six decimals. Rounded to four decimals it is 0.0678, which lies slightly below the exact value, so a band of half-width 0.0678 gives a bound of 0.135808 and does not quite reach 0.135846. The value is derived here and is not printed by the lab.
 113 R3 · Why the window start is a convention
 114 Explain why the start of window two is a convention, and list what a finding about the difference between the windows would need before it could be stated.
 115 Solution
 116 The start 2000-01 is the first month from which the other embedded snapshots have data. That reason concerns the data files, not the GBP rate, so any other start is an equally admissible choice. A finding about the difference would need four things. It needs a stated test of the difference, which Section 3 shows the lab does not supply. It needs a sensitivity check over several starts, since the printed difference depends on the start. It needs a source for any cause of a difference. And it needs the claim limited to this column, because a second column is a second sample. This solution states no number for a new start.
