  15  A price above a benchmark is a gap only relative to that benchmark, and the sign of the gap can change with the benchmark's assumed growth rate (Proposition 2.2 of this lesson). This lesson states the simplest benchmark, the present value of a constant-growth dividend stream, proves its closed form under stated assumptions, and shows that one price can sit above the benchmark under one growth assumption and below it under another. It then defines a financial panic by its mechanism, a sudden demand for cash, and states no claim about how often panics occur. The lab computes the closed form and its finite sums exactly, and prints the gaps for one seeded simulated price path.
  16  1The fundamental benchmark
  17  Assumption 1.1 (constant growth and a constant discount rate)
  18  Dividends are paid at the end of each period \(s = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\). Let \(r\) and \(g\) be fixed real numbers with
  19  (A0) \(r > g\) and \(1 + g > 0\), and
  20  (A1) \(d_s = d_1 (1 + g)^{s-1}\) for every \(s \ge 1\), where \(d_1\) is a real number.
  21  Future cash flows are discounted at the constant per-period rate \(r\), so a payment \(k\) periods ahead has discount factor \(1/(1 + r)^k\). Under (A0), \(r > g > -1\), so \(1 + r > 0\) and every discount factor is positive. The rate \(r\) is a per-period rate, and \(g\) is the per-period growth rate of the dividend, both in the simple convention.
  22  Definition 1.2 (fundamental value at date t)
  23  For an integer \(t \ge 0\), the fundamental value at date \(t\) is the present value at \(t\) of the dividends paid after \(t\):
  24  \[ F_t = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \frac{d_{t+k}}{(1 + r)^{k}}, \]
  25  whenever the series converges. Its partial sums are \(S_N(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{N} d_{t+k}/(1 + r)^{k}\). The fundamental value is a benchmark: it is computed from the dividends and the discount rate, and it is not a forecast of any price.
  26  Theorem 1.3 (closed form of the constant-growth benchmark)
  27  Assuming Assumption 1.1 (A0 and A1), for every integer \(t \ge 0\) the series in Definition 1.2 converges, and
  28  \[ F_t = \frac{d_1 (1 + g)^{t}}{r - g} = \frac{d_{t+1}}{r - g}. \]
  29  In particular \(F_0 = d_1 / (r - g)\). The partial sums satisfy \(S_N(t) = F_t\,(1 - q^{N})\) exactly, for \(q = (1 + g)/(1 + r)\) and every \(N \ge 1\).
  30  Proof
  31  Fix \(t \ge 0\). By (A1), \(d_{t+k} = d_1 (1 + g)^{t+k-1} = d_1 (1 + g)^{t} (1 + g)^{k-1}\). Dividing by \((1 + r)^{k}\) and writing \((1 + g)^{k-1}/(1 + r)^{k-1} = q^{k-1}\) gives
  32  \[ \frac{d_{t+k}}{(1 + r)^{k}} = \frac{d_1 (1 + g)^{t}}{1 + r}\, q^{k-1}. \]
  33  Summing over \(k = 1, \ldots, N\) and using the finite geometric sum \(\sum_{j=0}^{N-1} q^{j} = (1 - q^{N})/(1 - q)\), valid because \(q \ne 1\) (shown below), gives
  34  \[ S_N(t) = \frac{d_1 (1 + g)^{t}}{1 + r}\cdot\frac{1 - q^{N}}{1 - q}. \]
  35  Under (A0), \(1 - q = \bigl((1 + r) - (1 + g)\bigr)/(1 + r) = (r - g)/(1 + r)\), which is positive. So \(q \ne 1\), and the expression becomes \(S_N(t) = d_1 (1 + g)^{t}(1 - q^{N})/(r - g) = F^{\ast}_t (1 - q^{N})\), where \(F^{\ast}_t = d_1 (1 + g)^{t}/(r - g)\) is the closed form on the right of the statement.
  36  Under (A0), \(1 + g > 0\) and \(1 + r > 1 + g\), so \(0 < q < 1\). Hence \(q^{N} \to 0\) as \(N \to \infty\), and \(S_N(t) \to F^{\ast}_t\). The series converges to \(F^{\ast}_t\), so \(F_t = F^{\ast}_t\). The identity \(d_{t+1} = d_1(1 + g)^{t}\) is (A1) with \(s = t+1\). \(\blacksquare\)
  37  Part A of Lab 1 checks the identity \(S_N(0) = F_0 (1 - q^N)\) exactly, with rational arithmetic, for \(N = 10\), \(60\) and \(200\). The check is an algebraic identity of finite sums and does not depend on the size of \(N\).
  38  Proposition 1.4 (no finite benchmark when growth reaches the discount rate)
  39  Assume (A1) with \(d_1 > 0\) and \(1 + g > 0\), and (A2) \(r > -1\) and \(g \ge r\). Then \(S_N(0) \to +\infty\) as \(N \to \infty\), so the series in Definition 1.2 does not converge and Theorem 1.3 does not apply.
  40  Proof
  41  Under (A1) and (A2), \(q = (1 + g)/(1 + r)\) satisfies \(q \ge 1\), because \(g \ge r\) gives \(1 + g \ge 1 + r > 0\). Each term of \(S_N(0)\) equals \(d_1 q^{k-1}/(1 + r)\) by the identity in the proof of Theorem 1.3, and \(q^{k-1} \ge 1\) for every \(k \ge 1\). Since \(d_1 > 0\), every term is at least \(d_1/(1 + r) > 0\). Therefore \(S_N(0) \ge N\,d_1/(1 + r)\), which tends to \(+\infty\). \(\blacksquare\)
  42  The proposition removes the closed form from the cases where growth equals or exceeds the discount rate. A benchmark that depends on a growth rate above the discount rate is not a finite present value, and this lesson does not use one.
  43  2Speculative bubbles as a gap above the benchmark
  44  Definition 2.1 (speculative bubble, relative to a benchmark)
  45  Assume Assumption 1.1 and let \(P_t\) be an observed price at date \(t\). The speculative bubble at date \(t\), relative to the benchmark \(F_t\), is the gap \(B_t = P_t - F_t\) when \(B_t > 0\). The gap is measured in cents, the currency unit of the dividends; Lab 1 reports it rounded to whole cents. A price with \(B_t \le 0\) has no bubble on this definition.
  46  Proposition 2.2 (the same price can be a bubble under one growth rate and not under another)
  47  (A3) \(d_1 > 0\).
  48  (A4) Assumption 1.1 holds with growth rate \(g\), and also with growth rate \(g'\) in place of \(g\), each with the same discount rate \(r\), where \(r > g\), \(r > g'\), \(1 + g > 0\), \(1 + g' > 0\) and \(g < g'\).
  49  Conclusion: \(F_0(g) < F_0(g')\). Consequently, for every price \(P_0\) with \(F_0(g) < P_0 \le F_0(g')\), the gap is positive under \(g\) and non-positive under \(g'\).
  50  Proof
  51  By Theorem 1.3, \(F_0(g) = d_1/(r - g)\) and \(F_0(g') = d_1/(r - g')\). If \(g < g'\), then \(r - g > r - g' > 0\). With \(d_1 > 0\), the reciprocal of a smaller positive number is larger, so \(d_1/(r - g) < d_1/(r - g')\). A price with \(F_0(g) < P_0 \le F_0(g')\) then satisfies \(P_0 - F_0(g) > 0\) and \(P_0 - F_0(g') \le 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
  52  A numerical witness: with \(d_1 = 400\) cents, \(r = 9\%\), \(g = 1\%\) and \(g' = 3\%\), the benchmarks are \(F_0(1\%) = 400/0.08 = 5000\) cents and \(F_0(3\%) = 20000/3\) cents, which is about \(6666.67\) cents. At \(P_0 = 6000\) cents the gap is \(+1000\) cents under \(1\%\) and \(-2000/3\) cents under \(3\%\).
  53  Proposition 2.2 is an exact statement about the benchmark, not about any market. A gap is a gap relative to a benchmark, and the benchmark rests on the growth rate, the discount rate and the dividend path, each of which is an assumption. Remark 2.3 states the consequence for Definition 2.1.
  54  Remark 2.3 (what the definition does not establish)
  55  Definition 2.1 does not say that a price above the benchmark is wrong, and it does not say which benchmark is correct. A positive gap under one assumed growth rate can be a non-positive gap under another, by Proposition 2.2. The definition therefore reports a relation between a price and a stated benchmark. Whether the relation reflects a bubble in the everyday sense depends on the benchmark's assumptions, which are the subject of Section 5.

  61  3Financial panics
  62  Definition 3.1 (financial panic, by mechanism)
  63  A financial panic is a sudden, collective demand by holders of financial claims to convert those claims into money or into assets regarded as safe, in which the prices of many claims fall together or trading in them stops. The definition names the mechanism only. It does not say how often a panic occurs, what triggers one, or how large one is.
  64  Remark 3.2 (a panic is not a gap)
  65  A panic is defined by a change in the demand for liquidity. A speculative bubble, by Definition 2.1, is defined by a gap between a price and a benchmark. The two definitions use different objects. This lesson states no claim that one causes the other, and no claim about their order in time.
  66  Remark 3.3 (the benchmark does not apply to a panic)
  67  Theorem 1.3 concerns the present value of dividends and says nothing about the withdrawal of funding. A fall in the price of a claim during a panic is therefore not a gap of the kind in Definition 2.1 unless a benchmark for the claim is also stated and its assumptions are named.

  71  5Where the canon breaks
  72  The benchmark is not observed. \(F_t\) depends on \(d_1\), \(g\) and \(r\), and none of them is read from a price. Proposition 2.2 shows that, for some prices, the sign of the gap changes with \(g\).
  73  Constant growth is a restriction. Assumption (A1) fixes every dividend from \(d_1\) and \(g\). A path with a different shape breaks Theorem 1.3. For example, if \(d_1 = 400\) cents, \(d_2 = 0\) and \(d_s = 0\) for \(s \ge 3\), with \(r = 9\%\), the present value is \(400/1.09 = 40000/109\) cents, which rounds to 367 cents. The closed form at the same \(d_1\) and \(g = 3\%\) gives 6667 cents.
  74  A constant discount rate is a restriction. If \(r\) varies with date or with risk, Definition 1.2 needs a different discount factor, and the closed form no longer applies.
  75  Growth at or above the discount rate leaves the series without a finite sum. With \(d_1 > 0\) and \(g \ge r\), Proposition 1.4 shows that the partial sums grow without bound, so Theorem 1.3 does not apply. Any benchmark used outside (A0) needs a separate argument.
  76  The lab's price path is a draw, not a model. The wedges in Lab 1 are seeded random numbers. They are not estimated from prices, and their counts describe the draw.
  77  The sign of a gap is a claim about the benchmark and the price together. Any claim that a price contains a bubble must name the benchmark and its assumptions, and must say what evidence would reject the benchmark. The reading list names sources for that debate. This lesson has not read them, and it does not settle the debate.
  78  6Misconceptions
  79  “A price above the present value of the dividends is a bubble.”
  80  It is a bubble only relative to the stated benchmark. Proposition 2.2 gives a price of 6000 cents that is above the benchmark under 1 percent growth and below it under 3 percent growth, with \(d_1 = 400\) cents and \(r = 9\%\).
  81  Evidence: the witness in the proof of Proposition 2.2, and the change in the gap count between Part B and Part C of Lab 1.
  82  “The formula \(d_1/(r - g)\) applies to any dividend stream.”
  83  Theorem 1.3 uses (A1), which fixes every dividend from \(d_1\) and \(g\). A stream with \(d_1 = 400\) cents and no later dividends has a present value of \(40000/109\) cents, which rounds to 367 cents, while the formula gives 6667 cents at \(g = 3\%\).
  84  Evidence: the calculation in the Section 5 list, and Theorem 1.3's proof, which uses (A1) at every step.
  85  “When growth exceeds the discount rate, the benchmark just gets larger and stays finite.”
  86  Under \(g \ge r\) with \(d_1 > 0\), each discounted dividend is at least \(d_1/(1 + r)\), so the partial sums grow without bound. The closed form is not a finite value in that case.
  87  Evidence: Proposition 1.4, whose proof bounds \(S_N(0)\) from below by \(N d_1/(1 + r)\).
  88  7Problem set
  89  Mechanics
  90  Mechanics 1 · The closed form at date zero
  91  Let \(d_1 = 250\) cents, \(r = 10\%\) and \(g = 4\%\), all per period. Compute \(F_0\) exactly in cents, and give the value rounded half up to the whole cent.
  92  Solution
  93  By Theorem 1.3, \(F_0 = d_1/(r - g) = 250/(0.10 - 0.04) = 250/0.06 = 12500/3\) cents. Its decimal value is 4166.67 cents to two places, and the half-up rounding gives 4167 cents. Assumption 1.1 holds because \(r = 0.10 > 0.04 = g\) and \(1 + g > 0\).
  94  Mechanics 2 · The closed form at a later date
  95  With the parameters of Mechanics 1, compute \(F_3\) exactly, rounded half up to the whole cent.
  96  Solution
  97  Theorem 1.3 gives \(F_3 = d_1 (1 + g)^3/(r - g) = 250 \cdot 1.04^3/0.06\). Since \(1.04^3 = 1.124864\), the numerator is \(281.216\) cents, and \(281.216/0.06 = 4686.9\overline{3}\) cents. Rounded half up, this is 4687 cents.
  98  Mechanics 3 · A stream with growth equal to the discount rate
  99  Let \(d_1 = 100\) cents, \(r = 5\%\) and \(g = 5\%\). Compute \(S_{10}(0)\) exactly, and state whether the closed form of Theorem 1.3 applies.
 100  Solution
 101  Here \(q = 1\), so each discounted term is \(d_1/(1 + r) = 100/1.05 = 2000/21\) cents. Ten terms sum to \(S_{10}(0) = 1000/1.05 = 20000/21\) cents, about 952.38 cents. The closed form does not apply, because (A0) requires \(r > g\), and the partial sums grow linearly without a finite limit, as Proposition 1.4 states.
 102  Proof
 103  Proof 1 · Strict monotonicity of the benchmark in growth
 104  Assume \(d_1 > 0\), \(r > g\) and \(r > g'\), with \(1 + g > 0\) and \(1 + g' > 0\). Show that \(g \mapsto F_0(g) = d_1/(r - g)\) is strictly increasing on \(g < r\).
 105  Solution
 106  Let \(g < g'\) with both below \(r\), and \(1 + g > 0\), \(1 + g' > 0\). Then \(r - g > r - g' > 0\), because subtracting the larger number \(g'\) from \(r\) leaves the smaller result. For positive numbers \(a > b > 0\) the reciprocals satisfy \(1/a < 1/b\). Taking \(a = r - g\) and \(b = r - g'\) gives \(1/(r - g) < 1/(r - g')\). Multiplying by \(d_1 > 0\) gives \(F_0(g) < F_0(g')\). Since \(g\) and \(g'\) were arbitrary in \((-1, r)\), the map is strictly increasing there.
 107  Proof 2 · The benchmark recursion
 108  Assume Assumption 1.1. Show that \(F_t = (1 + g) F_{t-1}\) for every \(t \ge 1\), and state what this recursion says about the price of the stream after one period.
 109  Solution
 110  By Theorem 1.3, \(F_t = d_1 (1 + g)^t/(r - g)\) and \(F_{t-1} = d_1 (1 + g)^{t-1}/(r - g)\). Dividing gives \(F_t/F_{t-1} = 1 + g\), and the division is valid when \(d_1 \ne 0\), because then \(F_{t-1} \ne 0\). Multiplying out gives \(F_t = (1 + g) F_{t-1}\). The benchmark grows at the dividend growth rate from one date to the next, which is the one-period version of the constant-growth assumption.
 111  Proof 3 · The ratio of terms at growth above the discount rate
 112  Assume \(d_1 > 0\), \(1 + g > 0\), \(r > -1\) and \(g > r\). Show that the terms \(d_{k}/(1 + r)^{k}\) of the series in Definition 1.2 (at \(t = 0\)) increase without bound in \(k\).
 113  Solution
 114  Under (A1), \(d_k/(1 + r)^{k} = d_1 q^{k-1}/(1 + r)\) with \(q = (1 + g)/(1 + r)\). Since \(g > r\), \(1 + g > 1 + r > 0\), so \(q > 1\). Then \(q^{k-1} \to \infty\) as \(k \to \infty\), and \(d_1/(1 + r) > 0\), so each term tends to \(+\infty\). A series whose terms do not tend to zero diverges, which is consistent with Proposition 1.4.

 120  Empirical 2 · Compare the two growth assumptions
 121  Using Lab 1, Parts B and C, state the difference in the count of positive-gap periods between the two growth assumptions, and explain what the difference shows and what it does not show.
 122  Solution
 123  Under 3 percent growth the count is 9 of 13. Under 1 percent growth it is 13 of 13. The difference is \(13 - 9 = 4\) periods. The difference shows that the count can change with the benchmark's growth assumption, for the same prices. It does not show which growth rate is right, and it does not show that any real price series has a bubble.
 124  Empirical 3 · The largest gap as a share of the benchmark
 125  Lab 1 prints a largest gap of 2175 cents at \(t = 9\), where the fundamental is 8698 cents. Express the gap as a percentage of the fundamental, to two decimal places, first from the printed integers and then from the unrounded values, and state the base of each percentage.
 126  Solution
 127  Printed integers: the base is the printed fundamental, 8698 cents, so the ratio is \(2175/8698 = 0.250057\) to six decimal places, which is 25.01 percent to two decimal places. Unrounded values: the fundamental at \(t = 9\) is \(400 \cdot 1.03^9/0.06 = 8698.49\) cents to two places, and the price is that fundamental times \(1 + 0.25\).  128  Research
 129  Research 1 · The growth threshold for a given price
 130  Take \(d_1 = 400\) cents and \(r = 9\%\). For a price \(P_0 = 6000\) cents, find the growth rate \(g^{\ast}\) at which the benchmark equals the price. State how the sign of the gap depends on \(g\) relative to \(g^{\ast}\), and discuss what this means for a bubble claim.
 131  Solution
 132  The benchmark equals the price when \(400/(0.09 - g^{\ast}) = 6000\), so \(0.09 - g^{\ast} = 400/6000 = 1/15\), and \(g^{\ast} = 0.09 - 0.066667 = 0.023333\), or 2.33 percent. For \(g < g^{\ast}\) the benchmark is below the price, and the gap is positive. For \(g > g^{\ast}\) it is above the price, and the gap is negative. A bubble claim at this price is therefore equivalent to an assumption that growth is below 2.33 percent, and the claim is only as strong as the evidence for that assumption.
 133  Research 2 · A test that could reject the constant-growth benchmark
 134  Describe a test that uses only dividends and prices, and that could reject Assumption 1.1 (A1) for a given stream. State the statistic and the rejection rule, and state what the test cannot establish.
 135  Solution
 136  One test compares the realised dividends with the path \(d_s = d_1 (1 + g)^{s-1}\) for the growth rate that best fits the observed dividends. If the residual dividends are large relative to their sampling error, (A1) is rejected for that stream. The rule must fix the sampling error in advance and the significance level, and the test is valid only under the stated sampling model. It cannot establish the discount rate, and it cannot show that a price is or is not a bubble, because the price side needs its own benchmark.
 137  Research 3 · Which benchmark would a claim of a bubble need
 138  State the three inputs that a bubble claim relative to Definition 2.1 needs, and for each, the one assumption that would have to be defended before the claim can be assessed.
 139  Solution
 140  The three inputs are the dividend path, the discount rate and the growth rate. For the dividend path, the assumption is (A1), or the dividend forecast that replaces it. For the discount rate, the assumption is that a single constant rate \(r\) prices the stream. For the growth rate, the assumption is that the same \(g\) governs all future dividends. Each assumption must be stated, and the claim is only as strong as the weakest of the three. Proposition 2.2 shows that the growth input alone can reverse the sign of the gap.
