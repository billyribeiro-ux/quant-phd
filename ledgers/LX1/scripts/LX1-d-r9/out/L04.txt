53: 1Monthly log changes and dispersion
55: Lab 1 measures a recorded series, so it needs a measure of how far the series moves from month to month. This section defines that measure and proves the identity that Lab 1 checks on the data.
58: Definition 1.1 (monthly log change, dispersion)
59: Let \(x_t > 0\) be a rate at each month \(t\). The monthly log change at month \(t\) is \(\ell_t = \ln(x_t / x_{t-1})\), the natural logarithm of the ratio of two consecutive rates. It is a change per month and is not annualized. A window of \(n\) monthly log changes has dispersion \(s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{k}(\ell_k - \overline{\ell})^{2}}\), the sample standard deviation of the \(n\) changes with denominator \(n-1\), where \(\overline{\ell}\) is their mean. The dispersion is defined only when \(n \ge 2\).
62: The simple change in a month is \(x_t / x_{t-1} - 1\). Log changes add across months, and simple changes do not add in general. A rise of 10 percent followed by a rise of 10 percent has simple changes that sum to 20 percent, but the total change is 21 percent, because \(1.1 \times 1.1 = 1.21\). Proposition 1.2 gives the additive property of log changes.
65: Proposition 1.2 (telescoping of log changes)
66: Assuming (A1): the rates \(x_t\) are positive for every month \(t\) from \(a\) to \(b\), where \(a < b\). Then \(\displaystyle\sum_{t=a+1}^{b} \ell_t = \ln\frac{x_b}{x_a}\). The sum has \(b-a\) terms.
70: Proof
71: By (A1), each ratio \(x_t / x_{t-1}\) is positive, and the logarithm of a quotient is the difference of the logarithms. So \(\ell_t = \ln x_t - \ln x_{t-1}\) for each \(t\) with \(a < t \le b\). Summing over \(t = a+1, \dots, b\), each interior term \(\ln x_t\) with \(a < t < b\) appears once with a plus sign, in \(\ell_t\), and once with a minus sign, in \(\ell_{t+1}\), so these terms cancel. What remains is \(\ln x_b - \ln x_a = \ln(x_b / x_a)\). \(\blacksquare\)
75: Remark 1.3 (exact in real numbers, checked to a tolerance)
76: Proposition 1.2 is an exact identity in real numbers. Lab 1 checks it in floating-point arithmetic, to a tolerance of \(10^{-12}\). That check confirms the floating-point computation to that tolerance, and nothing more.

84: Definition 2.1 (fixed-parity model)
85: The model has an anchor currency, in which every rate is quoted, and one foreign currency. Its parity is a number \(\kappa > 0\) of anchor units per unit of the foreign currency. Its market rate at each month \(t\) is \(x_t > 0\), in the same units. A fixed-parity model is this model together with Assumption (A2), parity held: at every month \(t\) of the window, \(x_t = \kappa\).

90: Proposition 2.2 (zero dispersion in the model)
92: (A2) as stated above, for the model of Definition 2.1, with \(\kappa > 0\), and with a window of \(n \ge 2\) monthly changes, so that the rates are defined at the window's first date and at each later date.
94: Assuming (A2): (a) every monthly log change in the window is \(0\); (b) the dispersion of the window is \(0\).
98: Proof
99: (a) By (A2), \(x_t = \kappa\) and \(x_{t-1} = \kappa\) for each change in the window. So \(\ell_t = \ln(\kappa / \kappa) = \ln 1 = 0\), using \(\ln 1 = 0\).
100: (b) Every change is \(0\), so their mean \(\overline{\ell}\) is \(0\), each deviation \(\ell_k - \overline{\ell}\) is \(0\), and the dispersion is \(\sqrt{0 / (n-1)} = 0\). The denominator \(n-1\) is at least \(1\), because \(n \ge 2\). \(\blacksquare\)
104: Remark 2.3 (what Proposition 2.2 does not say)
105: Proposition 2.2 is a statement about the model. It says nothing about a recorded rate, which need not equal its parity at any month. Section 3 measures the dispersion of a recorded column, and Lab 1, Part A runs the model beside it. The lab does not derive the column from the model and does not test the two against each other. The parity here plays the part that the gold parity plays in the gold standard of L02. This model contains no gold and no metal, and nothing in Proposition 2.2 depends on either.

324: 4Where the canon breaks
325: Proposition 2.2 holds in the model whatever the data show. The comparison in Lab 1 is between that model and one recorded column, and the following limits apply to it.
327: One foreign currency, one anchor. The model has one foreign currency. Lab 1 reads one column. The dispersion of one column does not describe how several currencies move together, and no statement here covers a second currency.
328: Exchange rates only. The snapshot holds exchange rates and no interest-rate column. The course's data register note for fx_monthly states that the series is spot-only, so each value is a spot rate (a rate for exchange at once) and the series holds no forward rate (a rate for exchange at a later date). The lab cannot test a relation between interest rates and exchange rates, such as covered interest parity, which this lesson names but does not state or test.
329: Monthly sampling. Each value is the column's last value in its month. A monthly dispersion does not show the path inside a month, and the lab computes no daily figure.
330: The window start is a convention. Window two starts where the other embedded snapshots begin. A different start can give a different dispersion, and the lab computes no other start. The lab does not test whether the difference between the two windows survives another start.
331: No lender of last resort. Proposition 2.2 contains no central bank. A lender of last resort lends against collateral to institutions that are solvent but illiquid. The model has no such lender and no lending, so nothing in this lesson measures a lender's effect on any rate.
332: The historical arrangement. The lesson states no fact about the parities, participants, dates or end of the Bretton Woods arrangement. The course's reference list has no source for them yet. Any dated account of the system's end needs a source that is admitted to that list first.
337: 5Misconceptions
340: “A currency with a parity has no monthly change in its recorded rate.”
341: Proposition 2.2 gives zero change under (A2), and (A2) describes the model, not a recorded series. Lab 1, Part B, counts three exactly zero changes among 668 in window one, so 665 changes are nonzero in the recorded GBP column.
344: “A smaller dispersion in window two shows that the currency became calmer because of a regime change.”
345: Part D prints a difference of -0.003236 between two sample standard deviations of one column. The lab computes no test and attaches no inference to the difference. The window start is a convention, and the lab names no cause for any change.
348: “The monthly log change and the simple percent change are the same number.”
349: For a rise from 1.00 to 1.10, the simple change is 0.10 exactly, and the log change is \(\ln 1.1 = 0.095310\) to six decimals. The two differ by 0.004690 (Problem M1 gives the calculation).
352: “The fixed-parity model describes what the historical Bretton Woods arrangement was.”
353: Definition 2.1 defines a model with an anchor currency, a parity and a market rate. This lesson states no historical fact about the arrangement's parities, participants or end, and Section 4 lists what is still open.

363: Mechanics
365: M1 · A log change and a simple change
366: A rate moves from 1.2500 to 1.3750 dollars per pound in one month. Compute the monthly log change and the simple percent change, each to six decimals, and state their difference.
367: Solution
368: The ratio is \(1.3750 / 1.2500 = 1.1\). The log change is \(\ell = \ln 1.1 = 0.0953101798\ldots\), which is 0.095310 to six decimals. The simple change is \(1.1 - 1 = 0.100000\) exactly. The difference, computed from the unrounded values, is \(0.1 - 0.0953101798\ldots = 0.0046898\ldots\), which is 0.004690 to six decimals.
372: M2 · The dispersion of three changes
373: A window holds three monthly log changes, 0.01, 0.03 and -0.02. Compute their mean and their dispersion with denominator \(n-1\), each to six decimals.
374: Solution
375: The mean is \(0.02 / 3 = 1/150 = 0.006667\) to six decimals. The sum of squares of the changes is \(0.0001 + 0.0009 + 0.0004 = 0.0014\). The sum of squared deviations is \(0.0014 - 3 (1/150)^{2} = 0.0014 - 0.000133\ldots = 19/15000\). With \(n - 1 = 2\), the dispersion is \(\sqrt{19/30000} = 0.025166\) to six decimals.

382: Each month from 2000-01 to 2026-09 carries one change, dated by that month. The months from 2000-01 to 2025-12 are 26 full years, which is \(26 \times 12 = 312\) months. 390: P1 · Telescoping by induction
391: Prove Proposition 1.2 for a fixed starting month \(a\) by induction on \(b\), and state the step where (A1) is used.
392: Solution
393: Base case \(b = a + 1\): the sum has one term, \(\ell_{a+1} = \ln x_{a+1} - \ln x_a = \ln(x_{a+1} / x_a)\). Inductive step: assume \(\sum_{t=a+1}^{b} \ell_t = \ln(x_b / x_a)\) for some \(b \ge a + 1\). Adding \(\ell_{b+1} = \ln x_{b+1} - \ln x_b\) gives \(\ln x_b - \ln x_a + \ln x_{b+1} - \ln x_b = \ln(x_{b+1} / x_a)\), since the \(\ln x_b\) terms cancel. Assumption (A1) is used at every step, because the logarithms of \(x_t\) exist only for positive rates.
397: P2 · A band bound on the log change
398: Assume (A3): for some \(\kappa > 0\) and some \(h\) with \(0 \le h < 1\), the rate satisfies \(\kappa(1-h) \le x_t \le \kappa(1+h)\) at every month \(t\) from the month before the window's first change to its last change. Prove that \(\lvert \ell_t \rvert \le \ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\) for each change in the window, and state the bound when \(h = 0\).
399: Solution
400: Take a change dated \(t\). Upper bound on the ratio: \(x_t \le \kappa(1+h)\) and \(x_{t-1} \ge \kappa(1-h) > 0\), since \(h < 1\). So \(x_t / x_{t-1} \le (1+h)/(1-h)\). Lower bound: \(x_t \ge \kappa(1-h)\) and \(x_{t-1} \le \kappa(1+h)\), so \(x_t / x_{t-1} \ge (1-h)/(1+h)\). The logarithm is increasing, so \(\ln\frac{1-h}{1+h} \le \ell_t \le \ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\). Since \(\ln\frac{1-h}{1+h} = -\ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\), this is \(\lvert \ell_t \rvert \le \ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\). When \(h = 0\) the bound is \(\ln 1 = 0\), which is the conclusion of Proposition 2.2.
404: P3 · The log change is at most the simple change
405: Prove that \(\ln(1+u) \le u\) for every \(u > -1\), with equality only at \(u = 0\). Deduce that \(\ell_t \le x_t / x_{t-1} - 1\) for every positive pair of consecutive rates.
406: Solution
407: Let \(f(u) = \ln(1+u) - u\) on \((-1, \infty)\). Then \(f(0) = 0\) and \(f'(u) = \frac{1}{1+u} - 1 = -\frac{u}{1+u}\). On \((-1, 0)\) the derivative is positive, so \(f\) is strictly increasing there, and \(f(u) < f(0) = 0\). On \((0, \infty)\) the derivative is negative, so \(f\) is strictly decreasing there, and \(f(u) < f(0) = 0\). Hence \(f(u) < 0\) for \(u \ne 0\) and \(f(0) = 0\). For the deduction, put \(u = x_t / x_{t-1} - 1 > -1\), so that \(\ell_t = \ln(1+u) \le u\).

440: R1 · A test and its assumptions
441: State a test of whether the dispersion of window two differs from that of window one. Name the statistic, name every assumption it needs, and say which of those assumptions Lab 1 checks.
442: Solution
443: A natural statistic is the ratio of the two sample variances, \(s_2^{2} / s_1^{2}\), with the window-two variance in the numerator. A null distribution for this ratio depends on assumptions about the changes, such as their distribution and their independence across months. This lesson does not derive that null distribution, and no p-value is reported here. Lab 1 checks none of these assumptions. It checks the telescoping identity, and Part A's exact-zero result; Part B only counts the exact zeros. A test that reports a p-value needs the null distribution derived or cited from a source admitted to the reference list first.
447: R2 · The band implied by the largest move
448: Window one has a largest absolute monthly log change of 0.135846 (Lab 1, Part C). Under (A3), find the smallest half-width \(h\) for which the bound of Problem P2 allows a change of that size. Give \(h\) in closed form and evaluate it to four decimals.
449: Solution
450: Set \(\ln\frac{1+h}{1-h} = L\), where \(L = 0.135846\ldots\) is the unrounded change. Then \(\frac{1+h}{1-h} = e^{L}\), so \(1 + h = e^{L}(1 - h)\), which gives \(h = \frac{e^{L}-1}{e^{L}+1} = \tanh\frac{L}{2}\). The function \(h \mapsto \ln\frac{1+h}{1-h}\) is strictly increasing on \([0, 1)\), because its derivative is \(\frac{2}{1-h^{2}} > 0\), so this \(h\) is the smallest one whose bound reaches \(L\). Evaluating, \(\tanh(0.0679228\ldots) = 0.067819\ldots\), so the exact value is 0.067819 to six decimals. Rounded to four decimals it is 0.0678, which lies slightly below the exact value, so a band of half-width 0.0678 gives a bound of 0.135808 and does not quite reach 0.135846. The value is derived here and is not printed by the lab.
454: R3 · Why the window start is a convention
455: Explain why the start of window two is a convention, and list what a finding about the difference between the windows would need before it could be stated.
456: Solution
457: The start 2000-01 is the first month from which the other embedded snapshots have data. That reason concerns the data files, not the GBP rate, so any other start is an equally admissible choice. A finding about the difference would need four things. It needs a stated test of the difference, which Problem E3 shows the lab does not supply. It needs a sensitivity check over several starts, because the lab computes one start and so does not show whether the printed difference changes with it. It needs a source for any cause of a difference. And it needs the claim limited to this column, because a second column is a second sample. This solution states no number for a new start.
