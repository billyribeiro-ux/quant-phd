A trade is paid for when the payment behind it settles, and, in the model used here, a bank that sends a payment needs money in its account to make it. This lesson defines a payment system and the settlement cycle, two terms the unit uses throughout. It compares two designs on one day of payments: gross real-time settlement, where each payment settles when it is sent, and deferred net settlement, where each bank settles only its net position at the end of the day. The liquidity arithmetic is exact, and Lab 1 checks it on a seeded SIMULATED day. The lesson describes no real payment system.

1Payments, settlement and the settlement cycle

A payment moves money from one bank's account to another's. A trade recorded at one date is settled when the money for it has moved. FC1 Lesson 5 followed one trade through that sequence. This lesson looks at what the banks must hold while the payments are made. Amounts are in cents throughout, and every balance is an integer number of cents, so no rounding is needed.

Definition 1.1 (payment system)

In this lesson a payment system is an arrangement that moves money between the accounts of banks. It has two designs, which the lesson defines in Section 2. In real-time gross settlement (RTGS) each payment is applied to the sender's and the receiver's accounts when it is sent. In deferred net settlement (DNS) payments are held until the end of the day, and each bank settles only its net position, which is the sum of what it received minus the sum of what it sent.

Definition 1.2 (settlement cycle)

The settlement cycle of a trade is the number d of business days from its trade date to its settlement date, where \(d\) is a whole number and \(d \ge 0\). The unit treats \(d\) as a parameter. The curriculum names a T+1 rule for the settlement cycle. This lesson does not state that rule, because no source for it is on this program's reference list yet. Every settlement statement in this lesson holds for any value of \(d\), and the lab prints the value it uses.

Assumption 1.3 (the model of one day)

A day consists of \(m\) payments, numbered \(1, \dots, m\) in sending order. The banks are a finite set, and each payment has one sender and one receiver, which are different banks in that set. Two further assumptions apply to the designs:

A1 (fixed payments). The payments of the day are given. None is added, cancelled or changed during the day.

A2 (no intraday credit). A bank's only funds are its opening balance \(o_i \ge 0\). Under RTGS its balance after each payment must stay nonnegative, and under DNS the net debit must be covered in full from the opening balance at settlement.

2Liquidity under the two designs

For a bank \(i\) and a count \(k\) from 0 to \(m\), write \(S_{i,k}\) for the sum of payments received by \(i\) minus the sum sent by \(i\) among the first \(k\) payments in sending order, with \(S_{i,0} = 0\). Write \(\mathrm{Net}_i = S_{i,m}\) for the net position of \(i\) over the whole day.

Definition 2.1 (peak intraday need and net debit)

The peak intraday need of bank \(i\) under RTGS is the least \(o_i \ge 0\) such that \(o_i + S_{i,k} \ge 0\) for every \(k = 0, \dots, m\). It is the least opening balance that keeps the running balance nonnegative after every payment. The net debit of bank \(i\) under DNS is \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = \max(0, -\mathrm{Net}_i)\), the amount it must fund at settlement.

Proposition 2.2 (the peak need is a running minimum)

Under Assumption 1.3 (A1 and A2), the peak intraday need of bank \(i\) is \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max\bigl(0,\, -\min_{0 \le k \le m} S_{i,k}\bigr)\).

Proof

Let \(\mu = \min_{0 \le k \le m} S_{i,k}\). The constraint \(o_i + S_{i,k} \ge 0\) for all \(k\) is equivalent to \(o_i \ge -S_{i,k}\) for all \(k\), which is equivalent to \(o_i \ge \max_k(-S_{i,k}) = -\mu\). Together with \(o_i \ge 0\), the feasible opening balances are the \(o_i \ge \max(0, -\mu)\). Their least element is \(\max(0,-\mu)\), which is the stated need. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.3 (net positions sum to zero)

Under Assumption 1.3, the net positions of the banks sum to zero: \(\sum_i \mathrm{Net}_i = 0\).

Proof

Take a payment of \(a\) cents from bank \(s\) to bank \(r\), with \(s \ne r\) by Assumption 1.3. It adds \(+a\) to the net position of \(r\) and \(-a\) to that of \(s\). No other bank changes. Summing over all payments, each payment contributes \(+a - a = 0\) to the total, so the sum of the net positions is zero. \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.4 (the peak need is never below the net debit)

Under Assumption 1.3, for every bank \(i\), \(\mathrm{Need}_i \ge \mathrm{Debit}_i\). The inequality can be strict.

Proof

The index \(k = m\) is one of the indices in the minimum of Proposition 2.2, so \(\mu \le S_{i,m} = \mathrm{Net}_i\), and therefore \(-\mu \ge -\mathrm{Net}_i\). Since \(\max(0,\cdot)\) is nondecreasing, \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max(0,-\mu) \ge \max(0,-\mathrm{Net}_i) = \mathrm{Debit}_i\). For strictness, Example 2.6 has a bank with \(\mathrm{Need}_i = 100\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = 50\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.5 (what the order changes)

The net position \(\mathrm{Net}_i\) and the net debit \(\mathrm{Debit}_i\) do not depend on the sending order. The peak need does, because it depends on the running minimum of \(S_{i,k}\). Proposition 2.4 holds for every order. The order of payments affects only how large the gap between the two can be. Lab 1 prints one order, and nothing it prints shows a different order.

Example 2.6 (three payments, worked by hand)

Three banks, in sending order: payment 1, A pays B 100 dollars; payment 2, B pays C 80 dollars; payment 3, C pays A 50 dollars. The running sums are: for A, \(S_{A,k} = 0, -100, -100, -50\); for B, \(0, 100, 20, 20\); for C, \(0, 0, 80, 30\). The minima are \(-100\), \(0\) and \(0\). So \(\mathrm{Need}_A = 100\), \(\mathrm{Need}_B = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Need}_C = 0\) dollars. The net positions are \(\mathrm{Net}_A = -50\), \(\mathrm{Net}_B = 20\) and \(\mathrm{Net}_C = 30\), so \(\mathrm{Debit}_A = 50\) and the other two debits are 0. The net positions sum to \(-50 + 20 + 30 = 0\), as Proposition 2.3 requires. Under DNS the banks settle 50 dollars in total. The day's gross value is 230 dollars.

Remark 2.7 (the same three payments, sent in another order)

Send payment 3 first, so that C pays A 50 dollars, and keep the other two in place. Then the running sums for A are \(0, 50, -50, -50\). The minimum is \(-50\), so \(\mathrm{Need}_A = 50\) dollars, half the 100 dollars of Example 2.6. The net debit of A is still 50 dollars, because the net position did not change. Remark 2.5 is the general statement of this.

Lab 1 · One seeded day under the two designs (SIMULATED)

The day is SIMULATED: twelve payments among Bank A, Bank B and Bank C, drawn from seed 20260, in whole dollars. Expected from Propositions 2.3 and 2.4, before running: for every bank the RTGS peak need is at least its DNS net debit, and the net positions sum to zero. Part C also counts the banks with a positive DNS net debit and the banks whose need exceeds their debit. Part D prints the settlement lag, the value of \(d\), as a lab parameter in business days. No network and no real data are used.

"""BK4 lab L01: two settlement designs for one seeded day of payments among three banks.

SIMULATED. The twelve payments come from a fixed seed. They describe no real bank and no real payment system. Every
amount and every balance is an integer number of cents, so no rounding is used. The lab compares two designs:

* gross real-time settlement (RTGS): each payment settles when it is sent. A bank's peak intraday liquidity need is the
least opening balance that keeps its running balance nonnegative after every payment, taken in sending order.
* deferred net settlement (DNS): payments wait until the end of the day, and each bank settles only its net position.
Its net debit is the larger of zero and minus its net position. It is the amount the bank must fund at settlement.

The settlement lag in Part D is a lab parameter in business days. It is not a rule of any market. Standard library only;
no network; the only random numbers come from the seeded generator.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass

SEED = 20260
BANKS = ("Bank A", "Bank B", "Bank C")
PAYMENTS = 12
MIN_DOLLARS = 100
MAX_DOLLARS = 5000
SETTLEMENT_LAG_DAYS = 1

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Payment:
"""One payment: its place in the sending order (from 1), the sending and receiving bank, and its amount in cents."""

seq: int
sender: int
receiver: int
amount_cents: int

def make_day(seed: int) -> list[Payment]:
"""The seeded day: PAYMENTS payments between two distinct banks, in whole dollars within the bounds above."""
rng = random.Random(seed)
day: list[Payment] = []
for seq in range(1, PAYMENTS + 1):
sender, receiver = rng.sample(range(len(BANKS)), 2)
dollars = rng.randint(MIN_DOLLARS, MAX_DOLLARS)
day.append(Payment(seq, sender, receiver, 100 * dollars))
return day

def net_position(day: list[Payment], bank: int) -> int:
"""Cents received minus cents sent by the bank over the whole day. A positive value is a net credit."""
received = sum(p.amount_cents for p in day if p.receiver == bank)
sent = sum(p.amount_cents for p in day if p.sender == bank)
return received - sent

def rtgs_peak_need(day: list[Payment], bank: int) -> int:
"""Least opening balance in cents that keeps the running balance nonnegative after each payment, in sending order.

The list is in sending order, so each payment is applied after the ones before it.
"""
balance = 0
lowest = 0
for p in day:
if p.sender == bank:
balance -= p.amount_cents
elif p.receiver == bank:
balance += p.amount_cents
lowest = min(lowest, balance)
return -lowest

def dns_net_debit(day: list[Payment], bank: int) -> int:
"""Cents the bank must fund at settlement under deferred net settlement: max(0, minus its net position)."""
return max(0, -net_position(day, bank))

def settlement_day(trade_day: int, lag: int) -> int:
"""Business day on which a trade made on trade_day settles, given a lag in business days."""
return trade_day + lag

def main() -> None:
day = make_day(SEED)
print("A. The day: payments in sending order, SIMULATED, amounts in cents")
for p in day:
print(f"payment {p.seq:2d}: {BANKS[p.sender]} pays {BANKS[p.receiver]} {p.amount_cents:>9d} cents")

print()
print("B. Each bank under the two designs, in cents")
checks: list[bool] = []
for bank in range(len(BANKS)):
need = rtgs_peak_need(day, bank)
net = net_position(day, bank)
debit = dns_net_debit(day, bank)
checks.append(need >= debit)
print(
f"{BANKS[bank]}: RTGS peak need {need:>8d}; net position {net:>+9d}; "
f"DNS net debit {debit:>8d}; RTGS need at least DNS net debit: {need >= debit}"
)

print()
print("C. Checks and totals")
nets = sum(net_position(day, b) for b in range(len(BANKS)))
print("net positions sum to zero:", nets == 0)
print("RTGS need is at least the DNS net debit for every bank:", all(checks))
gross = sum(p.amount_cents for p in day)
moved = sum(dns_net_debit(day, b) for b in range(len(BANKS)))
print(f"gross value of the day's payments: {gross} cents")
print(f"amount moved at settlement under DNS (sum of net debits): {moved} cents")
funded = sum(1 for b in range(len(BANKS)) if dns_net_debit(day, b) > 0)
print(f"banks with a positive DNS net debit: {funded} of {len(BANKS)}")
beyond = sum(1 for b in range(len(BANKS)) if rtgs_peak_need(day, b) > dns_net_debit(day, b))
print(f"banks whose RTGS need exceeds their DNS net debit: {beyond} of {len(BANKS)}")

print()
print("D. Settlement lag, a lab parameter in business days (not a market rule)")
print(f"lag: {SETTLEMENT_LAG_DAYS} business day")
for trade_day in range(3):
settles = settlement_day(trade_day, SETTLEMENT_LAG_DAYS)
print(f"trade on business day {trade_day} settles on business day {settles}")

main()

3Conditions on the proofs

Each proof above uses only Assumption 1.3. Proposition 2.2 uses A2 to fix the constraint that the balance stays nonnegative, and Proposition 2.4 uses only the fact that the whole day is one of the prefixes. Proposition 2.3 does not need the rule that a payment's sender and receiver are different banks: a payment from a bank to itself adds and subtracts the same amount. The distinctness rule keeps every payment between two accounts, which is the intended model.

Remark 3.1 (where the order matters)

For a bank \(i\) whose inflows all come before its outflows, the running sums rise to the total inflow and then fall monotonically to \(\mathrm{Net}_i\). The running minimum over \(k\) is then \(\min(0, \mathrm{Net}_i)\), and Proposition 2.2 gives \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max(0, -\mathrm{Net}_i) = \mathrm{Debit}_i\). The order of the other banks' payments does not change \(S_{i,k}\), because only the payments to or from \(i\) enter it. Problem R1 shows that no order gives bank \(i\) a lower need than this one.

4Settlement cycle as a parameter

Definition 1.2 leaves \(d\) as a parameter. Lab 1, Part D, prints a settlement lag of one business day, and it gives the settlement day as the trade day plus that lag. The statements of this lesson hold for every \(d \ge 0\), because none of Propositions 2.2 to 2.4 involves \(d\). A statement about the value of \(d\) in an actual market needs an admitted source, and this lesson has none.

The two designs are exact inside Assumption 1.3 and simplified outside it. Four places where the analyst must act:

The model has no queues, credit lines or liquidity-saving features. A2 allows a bank to spend only its opening balance. Any design that queues payments or lends intraday is outside the two designs defined here. The lesson states no finding about any such design.

The day is given. A1 takes the payments as known. The model does not describe when payments are created, or what happens when one fails.

The settlement cycle is a parameter. Section 4 holds for every value of \(d\). The lesson does not state a value of \(d\) for any market, and the T+1 rule is named, not stated.

The lesson does not say which real system uses which design. No admitted source covers that question, so Definition 1.1 is the only description of the two designs.

5Misconceptions

“Net settlement removes the need for liquidity.”

It cannot raise the need of a bank that is a net receiver, and it can lower that need: Bank A in Lab 1 has an RTGS peak need of 112500 cents and a DNS net debit of 0. It does not remove the need for a bank that is a net payer. Bank B in Lab 1 has a net position of \(-1533900\) cents, so its DNS net debit is 1533900 cents. Its RTGS peak need is the same 1533900 cents. Under DNS it must fund the whole net debit at settlement, by Definition 2.1 and A2. The printed day is the evidence.

“A bank with a net credit needs no funds, whichever design applies.”

Under DNS, a net receiver has a net debit of zero. Under RTGS it can still need funds, because its payments out may come before its payments in. Bank A in Lab 1 has a net position of \(+379800\) cents and a DNS net debit of 0, while its RTGS peak need is 112500 cents. Proposition 2.4 is strict in that case.

“The order in which payments are sent does not change how much a bank must hold.”

Remark 2.5 shows that only the peak need depends on order. Remark 2.7 sends the same three payments in another order, and A's peak need falls from 100 dollars to 50 dollars. The net debit of 50 dollars does not change, which shows that the order affects the peak need and not the settlement amount.

6Problem set

Attempt each problem before opening its solution. The problems in Mechanics use only the definitions and propositions of this lesson. Problems marked “Lab 1” use the printed output above.

Mechanics

M1 · A bank that receives, then pays

Bank D opens with a balance of 0 dollars. Its only payments are an incoming 300 dollars, followed by an outgoing 500 dollars. Find its peak intraday need and its net debit, in dollars.

Solution

The running sums are \(0, 300, -200\). The minimum is \(-200\), so \(\mathrm{Need}_D = 200\) dollars by Proposition 2.2. The net position is \(-200\), so \(\mathrm{Debit}_D = 200\) dollars. The two are equal, as Proposition 2.4 allows: the lowest point is the end of the day.

M2 · Doubling every payment

Double every amount in Example 2.6, keeping the order. Find \(\mathrm{Need}_A\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_A\) in dollars.

Solution

Every \(S_{A,k}\) doubles, so the minimum doubles to \(-200\) and \(\mathrm{Need}_A = 200\) dollars. \(\mathrm{Net}_A\) doubles to \(-100\), so \(\mathrm{Debit}_A = 100\) dollars. Both values scale with the amounts, as the running-minimum formula of Proposition 2.2 shows.

M3 · Three payments with a dip

A bank opens with 0 dollars and then receives 40 dollars, pays 70 dollars and receives 10 dollars, in that order. Find its peak need and its net debit, in dollars.

Solution

The running sums are \(0, 40, -30, -20\). The minimum is \(-30\), so the peak need is 30 dollars. The net position is \(-20\), so the net debit is 20 dollars. The need exceeds the debit by 10 dollars, which is the dip below the final position.

Proof

P1 · The net positions sum to zero

Prove Proposition 2.3 without using the sending order, and state which part of Assumption 1.3 it uses.

Solution

Summing \(\mathrm{Net}_i\) over the banks counts each payment twice, once as an inflow to its receiver and once as an outflow from its sender, with opposite signs. The total is therefore the sum of \(a - a\) over all payments, which is zero. The proof does not use the sending order, and it does not need the sender and the receiver to differ, because a payment from a bank to itself adds and subtracts the same amount.

P2 · When the two need measures coincide

Under Assumption 1.3, show that \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \mathrm{Debit}_i\) if and only if either \(\min_k S_{i,k} \ge 0\), or \(S_{i,m} = \min_k S_{i,k}\). State every hypothesis the argument uses.

Solution

Write \(\mu = \min_k S_{i,k}\), so that \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max(0,-\mu)\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = \max(0,-S_{i,m})\), with \(\mu \le S_{i,m}\). If \(\mu \ge 0\), then \(S_{i,m} \ge 0\), and both are zero. If \(\mu < 0\) and \(S_{i,m} = \mu\), both equal \(-\mu > 0\). If \(\mu < 0\) and \(S_{i,m} > \mu\), then \(\mathrm{Need}_i = -\mu\), while \(\mathrm{Debit}_i\) is either zero (when \(S_{i,m} \ge 0\)) or \(-S_{i,m} < -\mu\), so they differ. The hypotheses used are only \(\mu \le S_{i,m}\) and the definitions in Section 2.

P3 · Scaling the amounts

Show that if every payment amount is multiplied by \(c > 0\), then \(\mathrm{Need}_i\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_i\) are multiplied by \(c\).

Solution

Each \(S_{i,k}\) is a signed sum of amounts, so it is multiplied by \(c\) when every amount is. Then \(\min_k S_{i,k}\) is multiplied by \(c\), because \(c > 0\) preserves order, and \(\max(0, \cdot)\) commutes with multiplication by \(c > 0\). Proposition 2.2 then gives \(\mathrm{Need}_i\) multiplied by \(c\). The same argument applies to \(\mathrm{Net}_i\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_i\).

Empirical

E1 · The bank with the largest net debit

Using only Lab 1, Part B, state which bank has the largest DNS net debit, and its RTGS peak need, in cents.

Solution

Bank B has the largest net debit, 1533900 cents, and its RTGS peak need is also 1533900 cents. Bank A and Bank C have net debits of 0 cents. The two measures are equal for Bank B, which is the case in Problem P2 where the lowest running position is the final one.

E2 · Banks whose need exceeds their net debit

Using only Lab 1, Part C, how many banks have an RTGS peak need above their DNS net debit? Name them using Part B.

Solution

Part C prints 1 of 3 banks. Part B shows that the bank is Bank A, with a peak need of 112500 cents and a net debit of 0 cents. Bank B and Bank C have need equal to net debit, both 1533900 cents for Bank B and 0 cents for Bank C.

E3 · The settled share of gross value

Using only Lab 1, Part C, express the amount moved at settlement under DNS as a share of the gross value of the day's payments. State the base and the rounding.

Solution

The base is the gross value, 2841600 cents. The amount moved is 1533900 cents. The ratio is \(1533900 / 2841600 = 0.5398\) to four places, rounded half up from \(0.539801\ldots\). As a share of gross value that is 53.98 percent. Under DNS, the rest of the gross value does not have to move at settlement.

Research

R1 · The best order for one bank

Fix bank \(i\) and the payments that involve it. Show that for any sending order, \(\mathrm{Need}_i \ge \mathrm{Debit}_i\), and that equality is attained by an order in which all of \(i\)'s inflows precede all of its outflows.

Solution

The inequality is Proposition 2.4. For the equality, Remark 3.1 shows that if all inflows of \(i\) come first, the running sums rise and then fall monotonically to \(\mathrm{Net}_i\). The running minimum is then \(\min(0, \mathrm{Net}_i)\), so \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max(0, -\mathrm{Net}_i) = \mathrm{Debit}_i\). Such an order exists, because the order of the other payments does not affect \(S_{i,k}\), and the payments involving \(i\) can be placed in that order. The hypothesis used is that the net position does not depend on order.

R2 · The amount moved at settlement

Show that \(\sum_i \mathrm{Debit}_i\) equals \(\sum_i \max(0, \mathrm{Net}_i)\), and that this sum is at most the gross value of the day's payments.

Solution

Since \(\max(0,x) - \max(0,-x) = x\), summing gives \(\sum_i \max(0,\mathrm{Net}_i) - \sum_i \max(0,-\mathrm{Net}_i) = \sum_i \mathrm{Net}_i = 0\) by Proposition 2.3. So the two sums are equal. Each \(\max(0,\mathrm{Net}_i)\) is at most the total inflow of bank \(i\), because the total inflow is nonnegative and at least \(\mathrm{Net}_i\). Summing the inflows over all banks gives the gross value, since every payment is an inflow to exactly one bank.

R3 · A cycle with no net debit

Take three payments in order: bank A pays bank B 100 dollars, bank B pays bank C 100 dollars, and bank C pays bank A 100 dollars. Show that every DNS net debit is zero while A's RTGS peak need is positive, and state which definition makes each DNS net debit zero.

Solution

Each bank receives 100 and sends 100, so every \(\mathrm{Net}_i = 0\) and every \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = 0\). For bank A the running sums are \(0, -100, -100, 0\), so \(\mathrm{Need}_A = 100\) dollars. Each zero debit follows from the definition \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = \max(0,-\mathrm{Net}_i)\), since every net position is zero. The example shows that net settlement can leave no funding requirement at settlement while intraday funding is still needed.
