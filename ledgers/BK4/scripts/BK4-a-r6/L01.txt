51	In the model used here, a trade is paid for when the payment behind it settles, and a bank that sends a payment needs money in its account to make it. This lesson defines a payment system and the settlement cycle, two terms the unit uses throughout. It compares two designs on one day of payments: gross real-time settlement, where each payment settles when it is sent, and deferred net settlement, where each bank settles only its net position at the end of the day. The liquidity arithmetic is exact, and Lab 1 checks it on a seeded SIMULATED day. The lesson describes no real payment system.
53	1Payments, settlement and the settlement cycle
55	A payment moves money from one bank's account to another's. In this model, a trade recorded at one date settles when the money for it has moved between accounts. FC1 Lesson 5 followed one trade through that sequence. This lesson looks at what the banks must hold while the payments are made. Lab amounts are in cents, and the worked examples state whole dollars. Every balance is an integer number of cents, so no rounding is needed.
58	Definition 1.1 (payment system)
59	In this lesson a payment system is an arrangement that moves money between the accounts of banks. It has two designs, which the rest of this definition defines and Section 2 measures. In real-time gross settlement (RTGS) each payment is applied to the sender's and the receiver's accounts when it is sent. In deferred net settlement (DNS) payments are held until the end of the day, and each bank settles only its net position, which is the sum of what it received minus the sum of what it sent.
63	Definition 1.2 (settlement cycle)
64	The settlement cycle of a trade is the number d of business days from its trade date to its settlement date, where \(d\) is a whole number and \(d \ge 0\). The unit treats \(d\) as a parameter. The curriculum names a T+1 rule for the settlement cycle. This lesson does not state that rule, because no source for it is on this program's reference list yet. The results of Section 2 do not involve \(d\), so they hold for any value of \(d\), and the lab prints the value it uses.
68	Assumption 1.3 (the model of one day)
69	A day consists of \(m\) payments, numbered \(1, \dots, m\) in sending order. The banks are a finite set, and each payment has one sender and one receiver, which are different banks in that set. Two further assumptions apply to the designs:
70	A1 (fixed payments). The payments of the day are given. None is added, cancelled or changed during the day.
71	A2 (no intraday credit). A bank's only funds are its opening balance \(o_i \ge 0\). Under RTGS its balance after each payment must stay nonnegative, and under DNS the net debit, defined in Definition 2.1, must be covered in full from the opening balance at settlement.
74	2Liquidity under the two designs
76	For a bank \(i\) and a count \(k\) from 0 to \(m\), write \(S_{i,k}\) for the sum of payments received by \(i\) minus the sum sent by \(i\) among the first \(k\) payments in sending order, with \(S_{i,0} = 0\). Write \(\mathrm{Net}_i = S_{i,m}\) for the net position of \(i\) over the whole day.
79	Definition 2.1 (peak intraday need and net debit)
80	The peak intraday need of bank \(i\) under RTGS is the least \(o_i \ge 0\) such that \(o_i + S_{i,k} \ge 0\) for every \(k = 0, \dots, m\). It is the least opening balance that keeps the running balance nonnegative after every payment. The net debit of bank \(i\) under DNS is \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = \max(0, -\mathrm{Net}_i)\), the amount it must fund at settlement.
84	Proposition 2.2 (the peak need is a running minimum)
85	Under Assumption 1.3 (A1 and A2), the peak intraday need of bank \(i\) is \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max\bigl(0,\, -\min_{0 \le k \le m} S_{i,k}\bigr)\).
89	Proof
90	Let \(\mu = \min_{0 \le k \le m} S_{i,k}\). The constraint \(o_i + S_{i,k} \ge 0\) for all \(k\) is equivalent to \(o_i \ge -S_{i,k}\) for all \(k\), which is equivalent to \(o_i \ge \max_k(-S_{i,k}) = -\mu\). Together with \(o_i \ge 0\), the feasible opening balances are the \(o_i \ge \max(0, -\mu)\). Their least element is \(\max(0,-\mu)\), which is the stated need. \(\blacksquare\)
94	Proposition 2.3 (net positions sum to zero)
95	Under Assumption 1.3, the net positions of the banks sum to zero: \(\sum_i \mathrm{Net}_i = 0\).
99	Proof
100	Take a payment of \(a\) cents from bank \(s\) to bank \(r\), with \(s \ne r\) by Assumption 1.3. It adds \(+a\) to the net position of \(r\) and \(-a\) to that of \(s\). No other bank changes. Summing over all payments, each payment contributes \(+a - a = 0\) to the total, so the sum of the net positions is zero. \(\blacksquare\)
104	Proposition 2.4 (the peak need is never below the net debit)
105	Under Assumption 1.3, for every bank \(i\), \(\mathrm{Need}_i \ge \mathrm{Debit}_i\). The inequality can be strict.
109	Proof
110	The index \(k = m\) is one of the indices in the minimum of Proposition 2.2, so \(\mu \le S_{i,m} = \mathrm{Net}_i\), and therefore \(-\mu \ge -\mathrm{Net}_i\). Since \(\max(0,\cdot)\) is nondecreasing, \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max(0,-\mu) \ge \max(0,-\mathrm{Net}_i) = \mathrm{Debit}_i\). For strictness, Example 2.6 has a bank with \(\mathrm{Need}_i = 100\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = 50\). \(\blacksquare\)
114	Remark 2.5 (what the order changes)
115	The net position \(\mathrm{Net}_i\) and the net debit \(\mathrm{Debit}_i\) do not depend on the sending order. The peak need can, because it depends on the running minimum of \(S_{i,k}\), and that minimum can change with the order. Proposition 2.4 holds for every order. The order of payments can change the peak need, and so it can change the gap between the peak need and the net debit. Lab 1 prints one order, and nothing it prints shows a different order.
119	Example 2.6 (three payments, worked by hand)
120	Three banks, in sending order: payment 1, A pays B 100 dollars; payment 2, B pays C 80 dollars; payment 3, C pays A 50 dollars. The running sums are: for A, \(S_{A,k} = 0, -100, -100, -50\); for B, \(0, 100, 20, 20\); for C, \(0, 0, 80, 30\). The minima are \(-100\), \(0\) and \(0\). So \(\mathrm{Need}_A = 100\), \(\mathrm{Need}_B = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Need}_C = 0\) dollars. The net positions are \(\mathrm{Net}_A = -50\), \(\mathrm{Net}_B = 20\) and \(\mathrm{Net}_C = 30\), so \(\mathrm{Debit}_A = 50\) and the other two debits are 0. The net positions sum to \(-50 + 20 + 30 = 0\), as Proposition 2.3 requires. Under DNS the banks settle 50 dollars in total. The day's gross value is 230 dollars.
124	Remark 2.7 (the same three payments, sent in another order)
125	Send payment 3 first, so that C pays A 50 dollars, and keep the other two in place. Then the running sums for A are \(0, 50, -50, -50\). The minimum is \(-50\), so \(\mathrm{Need}_A = 50\) dollars, half the 100 dollars of Example 2.6. The net debit of A is still 50 dollars, because the net position did not change. Remark 2.5 is the general statement of this.
129	Lab 1 · One seeded day under the two designs (SIMULATED)
130	The day is SIMULATED: twelve payments among Bank A, Bank B and Bank C, drawn from seed 20260, in whole dollars. Expected from Propositions 2.3 and 2.4, before running: for every bank the RTGS peak need is at least its DNS net debit, and the net positions sum to zero. Part C also counts the banks with a positive DNS net debit and the banks whose need exceeds their debit. Part D prints the settlement lag, the value of \(d\), as a lab parameter in business days. No network and no real data are used.
131	"""BK4 lab L01: two settlement designs for one seeded day of payments among three banks.
133	SIMULATED. The twelve payments come from a fixed seed. They describe no real bank and no real payment system. Every
134	amount and every balance is an integer number of cents, so no rounding is used. The lab compares two designs:
136	* gross real-time settlement (RTGS): each payment settles when it is sent. A bank's peak intraday liquidity need is the
137	least opening balance that keeps its running balance nonnegative after every payment, taken in sending order.
138	* deferred net settlement (DNS): payments wait until the end of the day, and each bank settles only its net position.
139	Its net debit is the larger of zero and minus its net position. It is the amount the bank must fund at settlement.
141	The settlement lag in Part D is a lab parameter in business days. It is not a rule of any market. Standard library only;
142	no network; the only random numbers come from the seeded generator.
143	"""
145	from __future__ import annotations
147	import random
148	from dataclasses import dataclass
150	SEED = 20260
151	BANKS = ("Bank A", "Bank B", "Bank C")
152	PAYMENTS = 12
153	MIN_DOLLARS = 100
154	MAX_DOLLARS = 5000
155	SETTLEMENT_LAG_DAYS = 1
158	@dataclass(frozen=True)
159	class Payment:
160	"""One payment: its place in the sending order (from 1), the sending and receiving bank, and its amount in cents."""
162	seq: int
163	sender: int
164	receiver: int
165	amount_cents: int
168	def make_day(seed: int) -> list[Payment]:
169	"""The seeded day: PAYMENTS payments between two distinct banks, in whole dollars within the bounds above."""
170	rng = random.Random(seed)
171	day: list[Payment] = []
172	for seq in range(1, PAYMENTS + 1):
173	sender, receiver = rng.sample(range(len(BANKS)), 2)
174	dollars = rng.randint(MIN_DOLLARS, MAX_DOLLARS)
175	day.append(Payment(seq, sender, receiver, 100 * dollars))
176	return day
179	def net_position(day: list[Payment], bank: int) -> int:
180	"""Cents received minus cents sent by the bank over the whole day. A positive value is a net credit."""
181	received = sum(p.amount_cents for p in day if p.receiver == bank)
182	sent = sum(p.amount_cents for p in day if p.sender == bank)
183	return received - sent
186	def rtgs_peak_need(day: list[Payment], bank: int) -> int:
187	"""Least opening balance in cents that keeps the running balance nonnegative after each payment, in sending order.
189	The list is in sending order, so each payment is applied after the ones before it.
190	"""
191	balance = 0
192	lowest = 0
193	for p in day:
194	if p.sender == bank:
195	balance -= p.amount_cents
196	elif p.receiver == bank:
197	balance += p.amount_cents
198	lowest = min(lowest, balance)
199	return -lowest
202	def dns_net_debit(day: list[Payment], bank: int) -> int:
203	"""Cents the bank must fund at settlement under deferred net settlement: max(0, minus its net position)."""
204	return max(0, -net_position(day, bank))
207	def settlement_day(trade_day: int, lag: int) -> int:
208	"""Business day on which a trade made on trade_day settles, given a lag in business days."""
209	return trade_day + lag
212	def main() -> None:
213	day = make_day(SEED)
214	print("A. The day: payments in sending order, SIMULATED, amounts in cents")
215	for p in day:
216	print(f"payment {p.seq:2d}: {BANKS[p.sender]} pays {BANKS[p.receiver]} {p.amount_cents:>9d} cents")
218	print()
219	print("B. Each bank under the two designs, in cents")
220	checks: list[bool] = []
221	for bank in range(len(BANKS)):
222	need = rtgs_peak_need(day, bank)
223	net = net_position(day, bank)
224	debit = dns_net_debit(day, bank)
225	checks.append(need >= debit)
226	print(
227	f"{BANKS[bank]}: RTGS peak need {need:>8d}; net position {net:>+9d}; "
228	f"DNS net debit {debit:>8d}; RTGS need at least DNS net debit: {need >= debit}"
229	)
231	print()
232	print("C. Checks and totals")
233	nets = sum(net_position(day, b) for b in range(len(BANKS)))
234	print("net positions sum to zero:", nets == 0)
235	print("RTGS need is at least the DNS net debit for every bank:", all(checks))
236	gross = sum(p.amount_cents for p in day)
237	moved = sum(dns_net_debit(day, b) for b in range(len(BANKS)))
238	print(f"gross value of the day's payments: {gross} cents")
239	print(f"amount moved at settlement under DNS (sum of net debits): {moved} cents")
240	funded = sum(1 for b in range(len(BANKS)) if dns_net_debit(day, b) > 0)
241	print(f"banks with a positive DNS net debit: {funded} of {len(BANKS)}")
242	beyond = sum(1 for b in range(len(BANKS)) if rtgs_peak_need(day, b) > dns_net_debit(day, b))
243	print(f"banks whose RTGS need exceeds their DNS net debit: {beyond} of {len(BANKS)}")
245	print()
246	print("D. Settlement lag, a lab parameter in business days (not a market rule)")
247	print(f"lag: {SETTLEMENT_LAG_DAYS} business day")
248	for trade_day in range(3):
249	settles = settlement_day(trade_day, SETTLEMENT_LAG_DAYS)
250	print(f"trade on business day {trade_day} settles on business day {settles}")
253	main()

294	3Conditions on the proofs
296	Each proof above uses only Assumption 1.3. Proposition 2.2 uses A2 to fix the constraint that the balance stays nonnegative, and Proposition 2.4 uses the formula of Proposition 2.2, the fact that the index \(k = m\) is one of the indices in its minimum, and the fact that \(\max(0,\cdot)\) is nondecreasing. Proposition 2.3 does not need the rule that a payment's sender and receiver are different banks: a payment from a bank to itself adds and subtracts the same amount. The distinctness rule keeps every payment between two accounts, which is the intended model.
299	Remark 3.1 (where the order matters)
300	For a bank \(i\) whose inflows all come before its outflows, the running sums rise to the total inflow and then fall monotonically to \(\mathrm{Net}_i\). The running minimum over \(k\) is then \(\min(0, \mathrm{Net}_i)\), and Proposition 2.2 gives \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max(0, -\mathrm{Net}_i) = \mathrm{Debit}_i\). The running minimum of \(S_{i,k}\) depends only on the order of the payments to or from \(i\), because \(S_{i,k}\) changes only at those payments. Problem R1 shows that no order gives bank \(i\) a lower need than this one.
303	4Settlement cycle as a parameter
305	Definition 1.2 leaves \(d\) as a parameter. Lab 1, Part D, prints a settlement lag of one business day, and it gives the settlement day as the trade day plus that lag. The statements of this lesson hold for every \(d \ge 0\), because none of Propositions 2.2 to 2.4 involves \(d\). A statement about the value of \(d\) in an actual market needs an admitted source, and this lesson has none.
308	The two designs are exact inside Assumption 1.3 and simplified outside it. Four places where the model stops:
310	The model has no queues, credit lines or liquidity-saving features. A2 allows a bank to spend only its opening balance. Any design that queues payments or lends intraday is outside the two designs defined here. The lesson states no finding about any such design.
311	The day is given. A1 takes the payments as known. The model does not describe when payments are created, or what happens when one fails.
312	The settlement cycle is a parameter. Section 4 holds for every value of \(d\). The lesson does not state a value of \(d\) for any market, and the T+1 rule is named, not stated.
313	The lesson does not say which real system uses which design. No admitted source covers that question, so the lesson states no fact about which design any real system uses; the two designs are defined in Definitions 1.1 and 2.1.
318	5Misconceptions
321	“Net settlement removes the need for liquidity.”
322	It cannot raise the need of a bank that is a net receiver, and it can lower that need: Bank A in Lab 1 has an RTGS peak need of 112500 cents and a DNS net debit of 0. It does not remove the need for a bank that is a net payer. Bank B in Lab 1 has a net position of \(-1533900\) cents, so its DNS net debit is 1533900 cents. Its RTGS peak need is the same 1533900 cents. Under DNS it must fund the whole net debit at settlement, by Definition 2.1 and A2. The printed day is the evidence.
325	“A bank with a net credit needs no funds, whichever design applies.”
326	Under DNS, a net receiver has a net debit of zero. Under RTGS it can still need funds, because its payments out may come before its payments in. Bank A in Lab 1 has a net position of \(+379800\) cents and a DNS net debit of 0, while its RTGS peak need is 112500 cents. Proposition 2.4 is strict in that case.
329	“The order in which payments are sent does not change how much a bank must hold.”
330	Remark 2.5 shows that the net debit does not depend on order, and that the peak need can. Remark 2.7 sends the same three payments in another order, and A's peak need falls from 100 dollars to 50 dollars. The net debit of 50 dollars does not change, which shows that the order can change the peak need and does not change the settlement amount.

340	Mechanics
342	M1 · A bank that receives, then pays
343	Bank D opens with a balance of 0 dollars. Its only payments are an incoming 300 dollars, followed by an outgoing 500 dollars. Find its peak intraday need and its net debit, in dollars.
344	Solution
345	The running sums are \(0, 300, -200\). The minimum is \(-200\), so \(\mathrm{Need}_D = 200\) dollars by Proposition 2.2. The net position is \(-200\), so \(\mathrm{Debit}_D = 200\) dollars. The two are equal, as Proposition 2.4 allows: the lowest point is the end of the day.
349	M2 · Doubling every payment
350	Double every amount in Example 2.6, keeping the order. Find \(\mathrm{Need}_A\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_A\) in dollars.
351	Solution
352	Every \(S_{A,k}\) doubles, so the minimum doubles to \(-200\) and \(\mathrm{Need}_A = 200\) dollars. \(\mathrm{Net}_A\) doubles to \(-100\), so \(\mathrm{Debit}_A = 100\) dollars. Both values scale with the amounts, as the running-minimum formula of Proposition 2.2 shows.
356	M3 · Three payments with a dip
357	A bank opens with 0 dollars and then receives 40 dollars, pays 70 dollars and receives 10 dollars, in that order. Find its peak need and its net debit, in dollars.
358	Solution
359	The running sums are \(0, 40, -30, -20\). The minimum is \(-30\), so the peak need is 30 dollars. The net position is \(-20\), so the net debit is 20 dollars. The need exceeds the debit by 10 dollars, which is the dip below the final position.
365	Proof
367	P1 · The net positions sum to zero
368	Prove Proposition 2.3 without using the sending order, and state which part of Assumption 1.3 it uses.
369	Solution
370	Summing \(\mathrm{Net}_i\) over the banks counts each payment twice, once as an inflow to its receiver and once as an outflow from its sender, with opposite signs. The total is therefore the sum of \(a - a\) over all payments, which is zero. The proof uses A1 in the form that the day's payments form a fixed finite list, so the sum is over a finite set. It also uses the clause of Assumption 1.3 that each payment has one sender and one receiver, so that each payment adds to one net position and subtracts from one. It does not use the sending order, and it does not need the sender and the receiver to differ, because a payment from a bank to itself adds and subtracts the same amount.
374	P2 · When the two need measures coincide
375	Under Assumption 1.3, show that \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \mathrm{Debit}_i\) if and only if either \(\min_k S_{i,k} \ge 0\), or \(S_{i,m} = \min_k S_{i,k}\). State every hypothesis the argument uses.
376	Solution
377	Write \(\mu = \min_k S_{i,k}\), so that \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max(0,-\mu)\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = \max(0,-S_{i,m})\), with \(\mu \le S_{i,m}\). If \(\mu \ge 0\), then \(S_{i,m} \ge 0\), and both are zero. If \(\mu < 0\) and \(S_{i,m} = \mu\), both equal \(-\mu > 0\). If \(\mu < 0\) and \(S_{i,m} > \mu\), then \(\mathrm{Need}_i = -\mu\), while \(\mathrm{Debit}_i\) is either zero (when \(S_{i,m} \ge 0\)) or \(-S_{i,m} < -\mu\), so they differ. The hypotheses used are only \(\mu \le S_{i,m}\), the formula of Proposition 2.2 under Assumption 1.3, and the definitions in Section 2.
381	P3 · Scaling the amounts
382	Show that if every payment amount is multiplied by \(c > 0\), then \(\mathrm{Need}_i\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_i\) are multiplied by \(c\).
383	Solution
384	Each \(S_{i,k}\) is a signed sum of amounts, so it is multiplied by \(c\) when every amount is. Then \(\min_k S_{i,k}\) is multiplied by \(c\), because \(c > 0\) preserves order, and \(\max(0, \cdot)\) commutes with multiplication by \(c > 0\). Proposition 2.2 then gives \(\mathrm{Need}_i\) multiplied by \(c\). The same argument applies to \(\mathrm{Net}_i\) and \(\mathrm{Debit}_i\).

415	Research
417	R1 · The best order for one bank
418	Fix bank \(i\) and the payments that involve it. Show that for any sending order, \(\mathrm{Need}_i \ge \mathrm{Debit}_i\), and that equality is attained by an order in which all of \(i\)'s inflows precede all of its outflows.
419	Solution
420	The inequality is Proposition 2.4. For the equality, Remark 3.1 shows that if all inflows of \(i\) come first, the running sums rise and then fall monotonically to \(\mathrm{Net}_i\). The running minimum is then \(\min(0, \mathrm{Net}_i)\), so \(\mathrm{Need}_i = \max(0, -\mathrm{Net}_i) = \mathrm{Debit}_i\). Such an order exists, because the running minimum of \(S_{i,k}\) depends only on the order of the payments involving \(i\). The other payments can be placed anywhere, and the payments involving \(i\) can be placed in the inflows-first order. The hypothesis used is that the net position does not depend on order.
424	R2 · The amount moved at settlement
425	Show that \(\sum_i \mathrm{Debit}_i\) equals \(\sum_i \max(0, \mathrm{Net}_i)\), and that this sum is at most the gross value of the day's payments.
426	Solution
427	Since \(\max(0,x) - \max(0,-x) = x\), summing gives \(\sum_i \max(0,\mathrm{Net}_i) - \sum_i \max(0,-\mathrm{Net}_i) = \sum_i \mathrm{Net}_i = 0\) by Proposition 2.3. So the two sums are equal. Each \(\max(0,\mathrm{Net}_i)\) is at most the total inflow of bank \(i\), because the total inflow is nonnegative and at least \(\mathrm{Net}_i\). Summing the inflows over all banks gives the gross value, since every payment is an inflow to exactly one bank.
431	R3 · A cycle with no net debit
432	Take three payments in order: bank A pays bank B 100 dollars, bank B pays bank C 100 dollars, and bank C pays bank A 100 dollars. Show that every DNS net debit is zero while A's RTGS peak need is positive, and state which definition makes each DNS net debit zero.
433	Solution
434	Each bank receives 100 and sends 100, so every \(\mathrm{Net}_i = 0\) and every \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = 0\). For bank A the running sums are \(0, -100, -100, 0\), so \(\mathrm{Need}_A = 100\) dollars. Each zero debit follows from the definition \(\mathrm{Debit}_i = \max(0,-\mathrm{Net}_i)\), since every net position is zero. The example shows that net settlement can leave no funding requirement at settlement while intraday funding is still needed.
