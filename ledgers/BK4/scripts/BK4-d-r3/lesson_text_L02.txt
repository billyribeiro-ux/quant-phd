Members that both owe and are owed amounts can offset them, so the amount that must be paid after netting can be smaller than the amount traded, as Example 2.4 shows. A central counterparty (defined in Definition 1.2) becomes the counterparty to each side of a trade, which turns the bilateral claims into one net obligation per member (Definition 2.1). This lesson defines novation and the CCP, proves the netting identities, and checks them in Lab 1 on a seeded SIMULATED set of trades. The lesson states no finding about whether a CCP reduces counterparty risk.

1Bilateral claims and novation

Take a trade in which one member owes another a fixed amount of cents. Before anything else happens, the trade is a claim of the receiver on the payer. The definitions below describe what changes when a counterparty is inserted between the two parties.

Definition 1.1 (novation)

Novation replaces a contract between two parties by two contracts: one between the first party and a new party, and one between the new party and the second party. The original contract is extinguished.

Definition 1.2 (central counterparty)

A central counterparty (CCP) is a firm that takes the position of the counterparty to both sides of each trade it clears, by novation. Each member then has its obligations to the CCP, not to the member on the other side.

Assumption 1.3 (the trade set)

A finite set of members trades in a fixed set of trades, numbered \(1, \dots, q\). Each trade \(t\) has a payer \(p_t\) that owes a receiver \(r_t\) the amount \(a_t\) in cents, with \(a_t > 0\). Three further assumptions apply:

A1 (fixed obligations). The trades and their amounts are fixed before netting. No trade is added, cancelled or repriced in the netting.

A2 (two parties). Each trade has one payer and one receiver, and they are different members of the set.

A3 (flat counterparty). After novation the CCP takes no position of its own. Each trade's payer owes the amount to the CCP, and the CCP owes the same amount to the receiver.

Write \(G = \sum_t a_t\) for the gross notional, in cents. For each member \(i\), write \(\mathrm{Rec}_i\) for the total of the trades it receives and \(\mathrm{Pay}_i\) for the total of the trades it pays, both in cents.

2The netting identities

Definition 2.1 (multilateral netting, net obligation, netted amount, reduction)

Multilateral netting replaces the trades of a set by one net obligation per member. The net obligation of member \(i\) is \(n_i = \mathrm{Rec}_i - \mathrm{Pay}_i\). A positive \(n_i\) is owed by the counterparty to the member, and a negative \(n_i\) is owed by the member to the counterparty. The netted amount is \(A = \sum_i \max(0, n_i)\), the total that must be paid to members after netting. The reduction from gross notional is \(G - A\), and its size in basis points of gross notional is \(\lfloor 10000\,(G-A)/G \rfloor\), with \(G > 0\). The floor is an integer division of nonnegative integers.

Proposition 2.2 (netting identities)

Under Assumption 1.3 (A1 to A3):

(a) \(\sum_i \mathrm{Rec}_i = G = \sum_i \mathrm{Pay}_i\);

(b) \(\sum_i n_i = 0\);

(c) \(A = \sum_i \max(0, -n_i)\), so the amount paid to net receivers equals the amount paid by net payers;

(d) \(0 \le A \le G\).

Proof

(a) By A2 each trade has one receiver and one payer, so it enters exactly one of the sums \(\sum_i \mathrm{Rec}_i\) and exactly one of \(\sum_i \mathrm{Pay}_i\), in both cases with the amount \(a_t\). Each sum therefore equals \(\sum_t a_t = G\).

(b) By (a), \(\sum_i n_i = \sum_i \mathrm{Rec}_i - \sum_i \mathrm{Pay}_i = G - G = 0\).

(c) For any real \(x\), \(\max(0,x) - \max(0,-x) = x\). Summing over the members and using (b) gives \(\sum_i \max(0,n_i) - \sum_i \max(0,-n_i) = 0\), which is the stated identity.

(d) The amounts are nonnegative, so \(A \ge 0\). Since \(\mathrm{Pay}_i \ge 0\), we have \(n_i \le \mathrm{Rec}_i\), and since \(\mathrm{Rec}_i \ge 0\), we have \(\max(0,n_i) \le \mathrm{Rec}_i\). Summing over \(i\) and using (a) gives \(A \le G\). \(\blacksquare\)

Proposition 2.3 (no netting reduction is achieved exactly when no member both receives and pays)

Under Assumption 1.3, \(A = G\) if and only if, for every member \(i\), \(\mathrm{Rec}_i = 0\) or \(\mathrm{Pay}_i = 0\).

Proof

By Proposition 2.2(a), \(G = \sum_i \mathrm{Rec}_i\), and \(A = \sum_i \max(0, \mathrm{Rec}_i - \mathrm{Pay}_i)\). Each term satisfies \(\max(0, \mathrm{Rec}_i - \mathrm{Pay}_i) \le \mathrm{Rec}_i\), by the argument of Proposition 2.2(d). So \(A = G\) holds if and only if each term is equal to its bound. Three cases cover the terms. If \(\mathrm{Rec}_i = 0\), the term is \(\max(0,-\mathrm{Pay}_i) = 0 = \mathrm{Rec}_i\). If \(\mathrm{Rec}_i > 0\) and \(\mathrm{Pay}_i = 0\), the term is \(\mathrm{Rec}_i\). If \(\mathrm{Rec}_i > 0\) and \(\mathrm{Pay}_i > 0\), the term is either \(\mathrm{Rec}_i - \mathrm{Pay}_i < \mathrm{Rec}_i\) or \(0 < \mathrm{Rec}_i\), so it is strictly smaller. \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.4 (three trades, worked by hand)

Members 1, 2 and 3 trade in this order: member 1 owes member 2 100 dollars; member 2 owes member 3 80 dollars; member 3 owes member 1 50 dollars. Then \(G = 230\) dollars. The receivables and payables are: member 1, \(\mathrm{Rec}_1 = 50\) and \(\mathrm{Pay}_1 = 100\); member 2, \(\mathrm{Rec}_2 = 100\) and \(\mathrm{Pay}_2 = 80\); member 3, \(\mathrm{Rec}_3 = 80\) and \(\mathrm{Pay}_3 = 50\). The net obligations are \(n_1 = -50\), \(n_2 = 20\) and \(n_3 = 30\) dollars. They sum to zero, as Proposition 2.2(b) requires. The netted amount is \(A = 20 + 30 = 50\) dollars, which equals \(\sum_i \max(0,-n_i) = 50\) dollars, as Proposition 2.2(c) requires. The reduction is \(230 - 50 = 180\) dollars, which is \(\lfloor 10000 \cdot 180/230 \rfloor = \lfloor 7826.08\ldots \rfloor = 7826\) basis points of gross notional. Member 1 both receives and pays, so Proposition 2.3 says \(A < G\), and the example confirms it.

Remark 2.5 (what the identities do not use)

Proposition 2.2 uses no sending order, no timing and no price. It uses only the amounts and who owes whom. The identities hold for any set of trades that satisfies Assumption 1.3, however many members there are, and they say nothing about what happens if a member cannot pay.

3Lab 1: netting for one seeded set of trades

Lab 1 generates six trades among four members from a fixed seed. The lab checks the identities of Proposition 2.2 on that set.

Lab 1 · Exposure before and after a central counterparty (SIMULATED)

The trade set is SIMULATED: six trades among four members, drawn from seed 2026 in whole dollars and printed in cents. Expected from Proposition 2.2, before running: the receivables sum to the gross notional; the net obligations sum to zero; the amount paid after netting equals the amount paid by net payers and is at most the gross notional. No network and no real data are used.

"""BK4 lab L02: multilateral netting before and after a central counterparty, for one seeded SIMULATED set of trades.

SIMULATED. Four members and six trades come from a fixed seed. They describe no real market, member or clearing house.
Each trade has one payer, who owes one receiver the trade amount, and the two parties are distinct members. Every amount
is an integer number of cents. The lab uses no rounding except the floor in the basis-point reduction, which is an
integer division of nonnegative integers.

Before novation, each trade is a bilateral claim of its receiver on its payer. After novation, the central counterparty
is the counterparty to each side, so each member has one net obligation to the counterparty: the sum of what it is owed
minus the sum of what it owes. A positive net obligation is owed to the member by the counterparty. A negative one is
owed by the member to the counterparty.

Standard library only; no network; the only random numbers come from the seeded generator.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass

SEED = 2026
MEMBERS = 4
TRADES = 6
MIN_DOLLARS = 500
MAX_DOLLARS = 20000

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Trade:
"""One trade: its number from 1, the payer that owes the amount, the receiver, and the amount in cents."""

seq: int
payer: int
receiver: int
amount_cents: int

def make_trades(seed: int) -> list[Trade]:
"""The seeded trade set: TRADES trades, each between two distinct members, in whole dollars."""
rng = random.Random(seed)
trades: list[Trade] = []
for seq in range(1, TRADES + 1):
payer, receiver = rng.sample(range(MEMBERS), 2)
dollars = rng.randint(MIN_DOLLARS, MAX_DOLLARS)
trades.append(Trade(seq, payer, receiver, 100 * dollars))
return trades

def gross_notional(trades: list[Trade]) -> int:
"""Sum of all trade amounts in cents."""
return sum(t.amount_cents for t in trades)

def receivable(trades: list[Trade], member: int) -> int:
"""Cents the member is owed, summed over the trades it receives, before any netting."""
return sum(t.amount_cents for t in trades if t.receiver == member)

def payable(trades: list[Trade], member: int) -> int:
"""Cents the member owes, summed over the trades it pays, before any netting."""
return sum(t.amount_cents for t in trades if t.payer == member)

def net_obligation(trades: list[Trade], member: int) -> int:
"""Receivable minus payable in cents. Positive: the counterparty owes the member. Negative: the member owes it."""
return receivable(trades, member) - payable(trades, member)

def netted_amount(trades: list[Trade]) -> int:
"""Cents that must be paid after netting: the sum of the positive net obligations."""
return sum(max(0, net_obligation(trades, m)) for m in range(MEMBERS))

def reduction_basis_points(gross: int, netted: int) -> int:
"""Reduction from gross notional to the netted amount, in whole basis points of gross, floored."""
return (10000 * (gross - netted)) // gross

def main() -> None:
trades = make_trades(SEED)
gross = gross_notional(trades)

print("A. The trades, SIMULATED, amounts in cents")
for t in trades:
print(f"trade {t.seq}: member {t.payer + 1} owes member {t.receiver + 1} {t.amount_cents:>8d} cents")

print()
print("B. Before novation: bilateral claims, in cents")
for m in range(MEMBERS):
print(f"member {m + 1}: receivable {receivable(trades, m):>8d}; payable {payable(trades, m):>8d}")
exposure_before = sum(receivable(trades, m) for m in range(MEMBERS))
print(f"gross notional: {gross} cents")
print("gross exposure before novation (sum of receivables) equals gross notional:", exposure_before == gross)

print()
print("C. After novation: one net obligation per member to the counterparty, in cents")
nets = [net_obligation(trades, m) for m in range(MEMBERS)]
for m, net in enumerate(nets):
print(f"member {m + 1}: net obligation {net:>+9d}")
print("net obligations sum to zero:", sum(nets) == 0)

print()
print("D. Amount to be paid after netting, and the reduction from gross notional")
netted = netted_amount(trades)
owed_by_members = sum(max(0, -n) for n in nets)
print(f"amount paid after netting (sum of positive net obligations): {netted} cents")
print("payments in equal the payments out after netting:", netted == owed_by_members)
print("after netting is at most gross notional:", netted <= gross)
print(f"reduction from gross notional: {gross - netted} cents")
print(f"reduction in basis points of gross notional, floored: {reduction_basis_points(gross, netted)}")

main()

4The CCP sets the amounts, a payment system moves them

The identities of Section 2 are bookkeeping results. They say what each member owes or is owed after novation, and they say that the total owed to members equals the total owed by members. They do not move money. Proposition 2.2(c) gives the amount \(A\) that must be paid after netting, and Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1 defines the payment system that settles it.

The question of whether a CCP reduces counterparty risk is asked by Duffie and Zhu (2011) . The question is their title. This lesson states no answer to it. Counterparty risk needs a model of default, and the netting identities contain none. The default waterfall, which is the first place a CCP's own resources enter the model, is the subject of Lesson 3.

The netting identities are exact under Assumption 1.3 and simplified outside it. Four places where the analyst must act:

The obligations are fixed. A1 takes the trades as given. The model does not describe how the obligations arise, or whether they change before netting.

The CCP is flat. A3 says the CCP takes no position of its own. A CCP that holds positions, or that is itself exposed, is outside the model.

Default is not modelled. Proposition 2.2 holds whether or not a member can pay its obligation. Default is absent from the identities. Lesson 3 introduces it.

The lesson states no finding about real clearing houses. Duffie and Zhu (2011) asks the question at paper level, and no source for the answer is on this program's reference list yet.

5Misconceptions

“Netting removes the money that must move.”

Netting reduces the amount that must move, and in general it does not remove it. In Example 2.4 the amount paid after netting is 50 dollars, against a gross notional of 230 dollars. The amount is zero only when every net obligation is zero, because the netted amount \(A\) is zero exactly when all \(n_i \le 0\), and Proposition 2.2(b) then forces every \(n_i\) to be zero. Lab 1, Part D, prints a positive netted amount for its set.

“A central counterparty is a payment system.”

In this model, novation fixes who owes what to whom, and the netting identities say nothing about the transfer itself. Proposition 2.2(c) gives the amount \(A\) that must be paid after netting, and that payment is made through a payment system, as Lesson 1 defines it. Lab 1 prints the amount and moves no money.

“The zero sum of net obligations shows that the CCP cannot fail.”

Proposition 2.2(b) is a bookkeeping identity that follows from A1 to A3. It holds whether or not any member defaults, so it says nothing about default. Lab 1, Part C, sums to zero for a set in which no member is assumed to fail. Default is modelled in Lesson 3.

Mechanics

M1 · Four members in a chain

Member 1 owes member 2 50 dollars, member 2 owes member 3 70 dollars, member 3 owes member 4 20 dollars, and member 4 owes member 1 40 dollars. Find each net obligation, the netted amount, and the reduction in basis points of gross notional, floored.

Solution

The receivables and payables are: member 1, 40 and 50; member 2, 50 and 70; member 3, 70 and 20; member 4, 20 and 40. The net obligations are \(n_1 = -10\), \(n_2 = -20\), \(n_3 = 50\) and \(n_4 = -20\) dollars, which sum to zero. The netted amount is \(A = 50\) dollars, and the gross notional is \(G = 180\) dollars. The reduction is 130 dollars, which is \(\lfloor 10000 \cdot 130/180 \rfloor = \lfloor 7222.2\ldots \rfloor = 7222\) basis points.

M2 · Doubling the trades

Double every amount in Example 2.4, keeping the trades. Find the netted amount, the reduction in basis points, and say why the basis-point figure does not change.

Solution

The gross notional becomes 460 dollars and the netted amount 100 dollars, so the reduction is 360 dollars. The basis points are \(\lfloor 10000 \cdot 360/460 \rfloor = \lfloor 7826.08\ldots \rfloor = 7826\), the same as before. Both the reduction and the gross notional double, so their ratio is unchanged, and the basis-point figure is unchanged when every amount is multiplied by the same factor.

M3 · A set with no reduction

Member 1 owes member 2 100 dollars, and member 1 owes member 3 100 dollars. Find the net obligations, check that they sum to zero, and say what the reduction is.

Solution

Member 1 has \(\mathrm{Rec}_1 = 0\) and \(\mathrm{Pay}_1 = 200\), so \(n_1 = -200\). Members 2 and 3 each receive 100 and pay nothing, so \(n_2 = n_3 = 100\). The net obligations sum to zero. The netted amount is \(A = 200\) dollars, equal to the gross notional \(G = 200\) dollars. The reduction is zero basis points. This is the equality case of Proposition 2.3, since no member both receives and pays.

Proof

P1 · The identities of Proposition 2.2

Prove parts (b) and (c) of Proposition 2.2, and state which assumption each uses.

Solution

Part (a) uses A2, which puts each trade once in the receivables and once in the payables, and A1, which keeps each amount the same in both sums. Then \(\sum_i n_i = G - G = 0\), which is part (b). Part (c) uses only the identity \(\max(0,x) - \max(0,-x) = x\), applied to each \(x = n_i\), and the zero sum of part (b). Summing gives \(\sum_i \max(0,n_i) = \sum_i \max(0,-n_i)\).

P2 · When the netted amount equals the gross notional

Prove Proposition 2.3 in both directions, and state each hypothesis the argument uses.

Solution

Under Assumption 1.3, \(G = \sum_i \mathrm{Rec}_i\) and \(A = \sum_i \max(0,\mathrm{Rec}_i - \mathrm{Pay}_i)\). Each term is at most \(\mathrm{Rec}_i\), since \(\mathrm{Pay}_i \ge 0\). If every member has \(\mathrm{Rec}_i = 0\) or \(\mathrm{Pay}_i = 0\), each term equals \(\mathrm{Rec}_i\), so \(A = G\). Conversely, if some member has \(\mathrm{Rec}_i > 0\) and \(\mathrm{Pay}_i > 0\), its term is strictly less than \(\mathrm{Rec}_i\), because \(\mathrm{Rec}_i - \mathrm{Pay}_i < \mathrm{Rec}_i\) and \(0 < \mathrm{Rec}_i\). Then \(A < G\), since all other terms are at most their bounds. The hypotheses used are \(\mathrm{Pay}_i \ge 0\), \(\mathrm{Rec}_i \ge 0\), and the identity \(G = \sum_i \mathrm{Rec}_i\) of Proposition 2.2(a).

P3 · A complete cycle

Members \(1, \dots, k\), with \(k \ge 2\), trade in a cycle: member \(t\) owes member \(t+1\) the amount \(a > 0\) for \(t < k\), and member \(k\) owes member 1 the amount \(a\). Show that every net obligation is zero, that \(A = 0\), and that the reduction is 10000 basis points.

Solution

Each member receives \(a\) from its predecessor and pays \(a\) to its successor, so \(\mathrm{Rec}_i = \mathrm{Pay}_i = a\) and \(n_i = 0\) for every \(i\). Then \(A = \sum_i \max(0,0) = 0\). The gross notional is \(G = ka > 0\), so the reduction is \(ka\) cents and \(\lfloor 10000 \cdot ka/(ka) \rfloor = 10000\) basis points. For \(k = 2\) the cycle is two trades, one in each direction, which cancel completely.

Research

R1 · The range of the reduction

Show that under Assumption 1.3 the reduction in basis points lies in the range from 0 to 10000, and equals 10000 exactly when \(A = 0\).

Solution

By Proposition 2.2(d), \(0 \le A \le G\), so \(0 \le G - A \le G\) and \(0 \le (G-A)/G \le 1\). Multiplying by 10000 and flooring gives an integer between 0 and 10000. The floor equals 10000 only if \(10000\,(G-A)/G \ge 10000\), which with the upper bound forces \((G-A)/G = 1\), that is \(A = 0\). Conversely, if \(A = 0\), the quotient is 1 and the floor is 10000.

R2 · Same netted amount, different gross notional

Show that the netted amount depends on the trades only through the net obligations, and give two trade sets with the same netted amount and different gross notionals.

Solution

\(A = \sum_i \max(0,n_i)\) is a function of the \(n_i\) alone, so two trade sets with the same net obligations have the same \(A\). Set X is one trade in which member 1 owes member 2 100 dollars. Set Y adds a trade in which member 3 owes member 4 50 dollars and a trade in which member 4 owes member 3 50 dollars, and keeps the first trade. In Y, \(n_1 = -100\), \(n_2 = 100\), and \(n_3 = n_4 = 0\), so \(A = 100\) dollars, as in X. The gross notional is 100 dollars in X and 200 dollars in Y.

R3 · One more trade moves the netted amount by at most its size

Add a trade of \(a > 0\) cents from member \(i\) to member \(j\), with \(i \ne j\), to a set that satisfies Assumption 1.3. Show that the netted amount changes by at most \(a\) in absolute value.

Solution

The new trade lowers \(n_i\) by \(a\) and raises \(n_j\) by \(a\), and leaves the other net obligations unchanged. The function \(x \mapsto \max(0,x)\) changes by at most the size of its argument's change, so \(\max(0,n_i)\) falls by between 0 and \(a\), and \(\max(0,n_j)\) rises by between 0 and \(a\). Therefore the total \(A\) changes by at least \(-a\) and at most \(a\). The gross notional rises by exactly \(a\).

There are 100 cents in a dollar, so 5093600 cents is 50936 dollars (Lab 1, Part D).

A cycle of trades among \(k\) members, each trade of the same amount, runs around the cycle. What is the netted amount?

Options
The gross notional
Zero
The amount of one trade

Each member receives one trade and pays one trade of the same size, so every net obligation is zero (Problem P3).
