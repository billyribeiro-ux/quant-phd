## The Markov Property
        
          
- Uses: Brownian motion, sigma-algebra, filtration, stopping time, normal distribution
          
- Introduces: Markov property
        
      
      
        
- Ttheorem
        
- Areplicated
        
- Bpublished, not settled
        
- Csingle study or lab result
        
- Dlore
        
- Xrefuted
      
      
Brownian motion forgets its past in a precise way. Given the path up to time \(s\), the future of \(B\) depends on that path only through \(B_s\), and the conditional law is a Gaussian transition density (Definition 1.2 below). This is the Markov property (Definition 1.1 below). The lesson proves it at fixed times from independent increments, proves the Chapman-Kolmogorov identity for the Gaussian transition density by completing the square, and states the strong Markov property at stopping times (Section 3) as a numbered assumption with a sketch. Lesson 6 needs that assumption to reflect the path at the first time it reaches a level.
      
## 1The filtration and the Markov property at fixed times
      
Let \(B\) be a standard Brownian motion in the sense of Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1: (D1) \(B_0=0\), (D2) the increment \(B_t-B_s\) is \(\Normal(0,t-s)\) for \(0\le s<t\), and (D3) increments over disjoint intervals are independent. Write \(\mathcal F_t=\sigma(B_r : 0\le r\le t)\) for the \(\sigma\)-field generated by the path up to time \(t\). This is the information the path has revealed by \(t\). For \(s\le t\), \(\mathcal F_s\subseteq\mathcal F_t\), so \((\mathcal F_t)_{t\ge 0}\) is a filtration in the sense of M6, here indexed by a continuous time. Sections 1 and 2 use (D1) to (D3), the standard facts A4(a), A4(b), A4(e) and A4(f) stated below, and not continuity of the paths.
      
        Assumption A4 (standard facts used without proof)
          
- (a) Extension of independence. Let \(\mathcal P\) be a \(\pi\)-system (a family of sets closed under intersection) generating \(\mathcal G\), and let \(Y\) be a random variable with \(\Prob(A\cap\{Y\in C\})=\Prob(A)\,\Prob(Y\in C)\) for every \(A\in\mathcal P\) and every Borel set \(C\). Then \(\mathcal G\) and \(\sigma(Y)\) are independent. This is the \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem.
          
- (b) Freezing. If \(Z\) is \(\mathcal G\)-measurable, \(Y\) is independent of \(\mathcal G\), and \(f\) is bounded and jointly measurable, then \(\E[f(Z,Y)\mid\mathcal G]=\bar f(Z)\) almost surely (that is, with probability one), where \(\bar f(z)=\E[f(z,Y)]\). The variable \(Y\) may be a random variable or a random continuous path.
          
- (c) Hitting times. For a continuous adapted process and a closed set, the first time the process is in the set is a stopping time.
          
- (d) Path determination. Every real random variable that is \(\sigma(B_r : r\le s)\)-measurable is a function of the path \((B_r)_{r\le s}\).
          
- (e) Gaussian integral. For \(c>0\), \(\int_{\R}e^{-c w^2/2}\,dw=(2\pi/c)^{1/2}\).
          
- (f) Grouping. Functions of disjoint groups of mutually independent random variables are independent.
          
- (g) Joint measurability. If \(X\) has continuous paths on \([0,t]\), then \((u,\omega)\mapsto X_u(\omega)\) is jointly measurable on \([0,t]\times\Omega\), so \(X_U\) is a random variable for every random variable \(U\) with values in \([0,t]\).
          
- (h) Measurability at a stopping time. If \(X\) is a continuous adapted process and \(\rho\) is a stopping time with \(\rho\le c\) for a constant \(c<\infty\), then \(X_\rho\) and \(\rho\) are measurable with respect to \(\mathcal F_\rho\), the \(\sigma\)-field of events known at \(\rho\) defined in Section 3.
        
        
Each part is a standard fact of measure theory or calculus. This page states them and does not prove them. A result that uses a part says "Assuming A4" and names the part.
      
      
        Definition 1.1 (Markov property)
The process \(B\) has the Markov property with respect to \((\mathcal F_t)\) when, for all \(0\le s\le t\) and every bounded Borel function \(g\colon\R\to\R\), there is a Borel function \(\psi\) with \(\E[g(B_t)\mid\mathcal F_s]=\psi(B_s)\) almost surely. The conditional law of the future, given the past, is then a function of the present value alone.
      
      
        Definition 1.2 (Gaussian transition density)
For \(u>0\) and \(x,y\in\R\), the Gaussian transition density is
        \[
          p_u(x,y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi u}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(y-x)^2}{2u}\Big).
        \]
It is the density in \(y\) of the normal law \(\Normal(x,u)\). For fixed \(x\) and \(u\), it integrates to 1 over \(y\), because the substitution \(y=x+\sqrt u\,w\) reduces the integral to the integral of the standard normal density.
      
      
        Lemma 1.3 (the future increment is independent of the past)
Assumptions.
        
          
- \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion: (D1), (D2) and (D3) of Lesson 1 hold.
          
- Assumptions A4(a) (extension of independence) and A4(f) (grouping).
        
        
Statement. For \(0\le s\le t\), the random variable \(B_t-B_s\) is independent of \(\mathcal F_s\).
      
      
        Proof
If \(s=t\), then \(B_t-B_s=0\) is a constant, which is independent of every \(\sigma\)-field, so the claim holds. Let \(s<t\). If \(s=0\), then \(\mathcal F_0=\sigma(B_0)\) is generated by the constant \(B_0=0\), so independence is immediate. Let \(s>0\). Take any finite set of times \(0=r_0<r_1<\dots<r_k=s\) and write \(\Delta_j=B_{r_j}-B_{r_{j-1}}\) for \(1\le j\le k\), and \(\Delta_{k+1}=B_t-B_s\). The points \(0=r_0<r_1<\dots<r_k=s<t\) are admissible in (D3), so \(\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_{k+1}\) are mutually independent. Because \(B_0=0\), each \(B_{r_j}\) is the sum \(\Delta_1+\dots+\Delta_j\), a function of \(\Delta_1,\dots,\Delta_k\). By A4(f), functions of disjoint groups of mutually independent variables are independent, so \((B_{r_1},\dots,B_{r_k})\) is independent of \(\Delta_{k+1}=B_t-B_s\).
        
Let \(\mathcal P\) be the family of events \(\{B_{r_1}\in C_1,\dots,B_{r_k}\in C_k\}\), for finite sets of times \(r_j\in[0,s]\) and Borel sets \(C_j\). Any such finite set of times is contained in a set that contains \(0\) and \(s\), and enlarging the set does not change the events of \(\mathcal P\) that it defines, so the argument above applies to every member of \(\mathcal P\). The family \(\mathcal P\) is a \(\pi\)-system, because two such events are again events of the same form, and it generates \(\mathcal F_s\). Each member of \(\mathcal P\) is independent of \(\{B_t-B_s\in C\}\), so Assumption A4(a) gives the claim.
      
      
        Theorem 1.4 (the Markov property at fixed times)
Assumptions.
        
          
- \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion, and Lemma 1.3 holds.
          
- Assumption A4(b) (freezing).
        
        
Statement. For \(0\le s<t\) and every bounded Borel \(g\), \(\E[g(B_t)\mid\mathcal F_s]=\psi(B_s)\) almost surely, with
        \[
          \psi(x)=\int_{\R} g(y)\,p_{t-s}(x,y)\,dy.
        \]
So \(B\) has the Markov property of Definition 1.1, and the transition function is \(p_{t-s}\).
      
      
        Proof
Write \(B_t=B_s+(B_t-B_s)\). The variable \(B_s\) is \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable, and \(B_t-B_s\) is independent of \(\mathcal F_s\) by Lemma 1.3. Apply Assumption A4(b) with \(Z=B_s\), \(Y=B_t-B_s\), \(\mathcal G=\mathcal F_s\), and \(f(z,y)=g(z+y)\), which is bounded and jointly measurable. It gives \(\E[g(B_t)\mid\mathcal F_s]=\bar f(B_s)\) with \(\bar f(z)=\E[g(z+B_t-B_s)]\).
        
By (D2), \(B_t-B_s\) has the law \(\Normal(0,u)\) with \(u=t-s\). Its density is \(\phi_u(w)=(2\pi u)^{-1/2}e^{-w^2/(2u)}\). Therefore \(\bar f(z)=\int_{\R} g(z+w)\,\phi_u(w)\,dw\). Substitute \(y=z+w\). Then \(\phi_u(w)=\phi_u(y-z)=p_u(z,y)\), and \(\bar f(z)=\int_{\R} g(y)\,p_u(z,y)\,dy=\psi(z)\). Setting \(z=B_s\) gives the claim. \(\blacksquare\)
        
The case \(s=t\), which the statement above excludes, is immediate. Take \(\psi=g\). The variable \(g(B_t)\) is bounded and \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable, because \(B_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable by the definition of \(\mathcal F_t\). It therefore satisfies the defining property of \(\E[\,\cdot\mid\mathcal F_t]\) with itself as the candidate, so \(\E[g(B_t)\mid\mathcal F_t]=g(B_t)=\psi(B_t)\) almost surely. With the case \(s<t\) above, this gives the Markov property of Definition 1.1 for all \(0\le s\le t\).
      
      
        Remark 1.5 (what the proof uses)
The proof of Theorem 1.4 uses (D2) for the law of the future increment, (D3) through Lemma 1.3, and Assumption A4(b). It uses no continuity of paths, so Sections 1 and 2 hold for any standard Brownian motion in the sense of Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1, including the discontinuous process of Lesson 1, Section 4.
      
      
## 2Chapman-Kolmogorov for the transition density
      
Theorem 1.4 writes the conditional law of \(B_t\) given the past as \(p_{t-s}\) applied at \(B_s\). The next identity says that transitions compose: moving from \(x\) to \(z\) in time \(u\), then from \(z\) to \(y\) in time \(v\), has the same effect as moving from \(x\) to \(y\) in time \(u+v\). It is a fact about the functions \(p_u\), and it uses no path property of \(B\).
      
        Lemma 2.2 (completing the square)
Assumptions. \(u,v>0\) and \(x,y,z\in\R\). Put \(m=(vx+uy)/(u+v)\).
        
Statement. \(\dfrac{(z-x)^2}{u}+\dfrac{(y-z)^2}{v}=\dfrac{(u+v)}{uv}\,(z-m)^2+\dfrac{(y-x)^2}{u+v}\).
      
      
        Proof
Multiply both sides by \(uv\). The left side becomes \(v(z-x)^2+u(y-z)^2\). Expanding gives \((u+v)z^2-2(vx+uy)z+vx^2+uy^2\). Since \((u+v)m=vx+uy\), the right side becomes \((u+v)z^2-2(vx+uy)z+(u+v)m^2+uv(y-x)^2/(u+v)\). The two sides therefore differ only in the constant terms. We need \(vx^2+uy^2-(vx+uy)^2/(u+v)=uv(y-x)^2/(u+v)\). Multiply by \(u+v\): the left side is \((u+v)(vx^2+uy^2)-(vx+uy)^2=uvx^2+uvy^2-2uvxy\), using \(v^2x^2+u^2y^2\) on both terms, which cancel. The right side is \(uv(y-x)^2=uvx^2+uvy^2-2uvxy\). The two agree. \(\blacksquare\)
      
      
        Theorem 2.1 (Chapman-Kolmogorov)
Assumptions. \(u,v>0\), \(x,y\in\R\); Lemma 2.2 and Assumption A4(e) (the Gaussian integral).
        
Statement.
        \[
          \int_{\R} p_u(x,z)\,p_v(z,y)\,dz=p_{u+v}(x,y).
        \]
      
        Proof
By Definition 1.2, the product is \(p_u(x,z)\,p_v(z,y)=(2\pi)^{-1}(uv)^{-1/2}\exp\big(-\tfrac12[(z-x)^2/u+(y-z)^2/v]\big)\). Lemma 2.2 replaces the bracket. With \(c=(u+v)/(uv)\), the product equals
        \[
          (2\pi)^{-1}(uv)^{-1/2}\,e^{-(y-x)^2/(2(u+v))}\,e^{-c(z-m)^2/2}.
        \]
Only the last factor depends on \(z\). By Assumption A4(e), \(\int_{\R}e^{-c(z-m)^2/2}\,dz=(2\pi/c)^{1/2}=(2\pi uv/(u+v))^{1/2}\). The integral is therefore
        \[
          (2\pi)^{-1}(uv)^{-1/2}\Big(\frac{2\pi uv}{u+v}\Big)^{1/2}e^{-(y-x)^2/(2(u+v))}
          =\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(u+v)}}\,e^{-(y-x)^2/(2(u+v))}=p_{u+v}(x,y).
        \]
The powers of \(2\pi\) give \((2\pi)^{-1}(2\pi)^{1/2}=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\), and the powers of \(uv\) cancel. \(\blacksquare\)
      
      
        
## Lab 1 · Chapman-Kolmogorov by quadrature
        
The lab approximates \(\int p_s(x,z)\,p_t(z,y)\,dz\) by a composite Simpson rule on \([-12,12]\) for \(225\) cases, \(s,t\in\{0.5,1,2\}\) and \(x,y\in\{-1,-0.25,0,0.5,1\}\), and compares it with \(p_{s+t}(x,y)\). Prediction: every gap is at the level of floating-point rounding, far below the stated tolerance \(10^{-6}\). The mass of the integrand for \(12\le|z|\le 40\) is far below the largest gap. The integral of \(p_t(0,\cdot)\) over \([-12,12]\) is \(1\) to ten printed digits. The lab uses no randomness and reads no data.
        
"""SC1 lab L05: the Chapman-Kolmogorov identity for the Gaussian transition density, by quadrature.
Standard library only. Nothing is random and nothing is read from a file, so every run prints the same lines.
The transition density is p_u(x, y) = (2 pi u)^(-1/2) exp(-(y - x)^2 / (2 u)) for u > 0. For each case the lab
approximates the integral over the intermediate point z of p_s(x, z) p_t(z, y) by a composite Simpson rule on
[-12, 12] and compares it with p_(s+t)(x, y). The mass of the integrand for 12 <= |z| <= 40 is computed and printed,
so the truncation can be compared with the gaps. The gaps are at the scale of double-precision rounding, not exact
values; the exact identity is proved on the page.
"""
from __future__ import annotations
import math
from collections.abc import Callable
LOWER = -12.0
UPPER = 12.0
INTERVALS = 2000
TIMES = (0.5, 1.0, 2.0)
POINTS = (-1.0, -0.25, 0.0, 0.5, 1.0)
TOLERANCE = 1e-6
def density(u: float, x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """The Gaussian transition density p_u(x, y) for u > 0: the N(x, u) density evaluated at y, in 1/length."""
    return math.exp(-((y - x) ** 2) / (2.0 * u)) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi * u)
def simpson(g: Callable[[float], float], a: float, b: float, n: int) -> float:
    """Composite Simpson rule on [a, b] with n subintervals; n must be even."""
    h = (b - a) / n
    total = g(a) + g(b)
    for i in range(1, n):
        total += (4.0 if i % 2 == 1 else 2.0) * g(a + i * h)
    return total * h / 3.0
def chapman_kolmogorov_gap(s: float, t: float, x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """Quadrature of the integral of p_s(x, z) p_t(z, y) over z, minus p_(s+t)(x, y)."""
    integral = simpson(lambda z: density(s, x, z) * density(t, z, y), LOWER, UPPER, INTERVALS)
    return integral - density(s + t, x, y)
def tail_mass(s: float, t: float, x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """Integral of p_s(x, z) p_t(z, y) over 12 <= |z| <= 40, by Simpson on [12, 40] and [-40, -12]."""
    def integrand(z: float) -> float:
        return density(s, x, z) * density(t, z, y)
    return simpson(integrand, UPPER, 40.0, INTERVALS) + simpson(integrand, -40.0, LOWER, INTERVALS)
def mass_over_window(t: float) -> float:
    """Integral of the transition density p_t(0, y) over y in [-12, 12]; it is 1 up to quadrature error."""
    def integrand(y: float) -> float:
        return density(t, 0.0, y)
    return simpson(integrand, LOWER, UPPER, INTERVALS)
def main() -> None:
    cases = 0
    worst = 0.0
    worst_tail = 0.0
    for s in TIMES:
        for t in TIMES:
            for x in POINTS:
                for y in POINTS:
                    worst = max(worst, abs(chapman_kolmogorov_gap(s, t, x, y)))
                    worst_tail = max(worst_tail, tail_mass(s, t, x, y))
                    cases += 1
    print(f"Chapman-Kolmogorov cases checked: {cases}")
    print(f"largest absolute quadrature gap: {worst:.2e}")
    print(f"largest gap below the stated tolerance {TOLERANCE:g}: {worst < TOLERANCE}")
    print(f"largest mass of the integrand for 12 <= |z| <= 40: {worst_tail:.2e}")
    example = chapman_kolmogorov_gap(1.0, 2.0, 0.5, -0.25)
    print(f"example s = 1, t = 2, x = 0.5, y = -0.25: quadrature gap {example:+.2e}")
    for t in TIMES:
        mass = mass_over_window(t)
        print(f"integral of p_{t:g}(0, y) over y in [-12, 12]: {mass:.10f}")
main()
        
Chapman-Kolmogorov cases checked: 225
largest absolute quadrature gap: 5.55e-16
largest gap below the stated tolerance 1e-06: True
largest mass of the integrand for 12 <= |z| <= 40: 3.81e-29
example s = 1, t = 2, x = 0.5, y = -0.25: quadrature gap -3.05e-16
integral of p_0.5(0, y) over y in [-12, 12]: 1.0000000000
integral of p_1(0, y) over y in [-12, 12]: 1.0000000000
integral of p_2(0, y) over y in [-12, 12]: 1.0000000000
        
The 225 gaps are at most \(5.55\times10^{-16}\), about 2.5 times the double-precision machine epsilon \(2.2\times10^{-16}\), so the floating-point evaluation reproduces \(p_{s+t}\) to that scale on every case the lab checks. The largest mass of the integrand for \(12\le|z|\le 40\) is \(3.81\times10^{-29}\), far below the gaps; the printed values do not include the mass for \(|z|>40\). The integrals equal \(1\) to ten printed digits. The printed digits show no error larger than \(5\times10^{-11}\) and do not show the sign of the rounding error. The lab checks 225 grid points. It does not show the identity for other values of \(x\), \(y\), \(s\) or \(t\). Theorem 2.1 proves the identity for all of them.
      
      
## 3The strong Markov property, as an assumption
      
A stopping time \(\rho\), with values in \([0,\infty]\), is a random time with \(\{\rho\le r\}\in\mathcal F_r\) for every \(r\ge 0\). This is the definition of M6's stopping time with the index now continuous. The \(\sigma\)-field of events known at time \(\rho\) is \(\mathcal F_\rho=\{A\in\mathcal F : A\cap\{\rho\le r\}\in\mathcal F_r\text{ for all }r\ge 0\}\). Deterministic times are stopping times. The Markov property at a deterministic time is Theorem 1.4. The strong Markov property extends it to a random time.
      
        Assumption A3 (strong Markov property; not proved here)
          
- \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion whose paths are continuous.
          
- \(\rho\) is a stopping time with \(\rho\le c\) for some constant \(c<\infty\).
        
        
Conclusion. The process \(X_r=B_{\rho+r}-B_\rho\), for \(r\ge 0\), is a standard Brownian motion, and it is independent of \(\mathcal F_\rho\).
      
      
Assumption A3 is stated for bounded stopping times. Every result that uses it says "Assuming A3" in its statement. Lesson 6 applies it to \(\rho=\tau_a\wedge t\), where \(\tau_a\) is the first hitting time of \(a\), defined in Lesson 6, Section 2; this \(\rho\) is bounded by \(t\). For an unbounded \(\tau_a\), the finiteness that A3 would need is not established on this page.
      
Sketch, not a proof. Let \(\rho_n=\lceil 2^n\rho\rceil/2^n\) and \(X^{(n)}_r=B_{\rho_n+r}-B_{\rho_n}\). Problem R3 shows that \(\rho\le\rho_n\le\rho+2^{-n}\) and that \(\rho_n\) is a stopping time. The variable \(\rho_n\) takes countably many values. For a fixed value \(q\), the conclusion of A3 holds at the fixed time \(q\): the finite-dimensional increments of \(B_{q+\cdot}-B_q\) have the laws given by (D1) to (D3), and the increments after \(q\) are independent of \(\mathcal F_q\) by the argument of Lemma 1.3, applied to finitely many increments after \(q\). Summing over the countably many values \(q\) of \(\rho_n\) gives the conclusion for \(X^{(n)}\), because \(X^{(n)}\) equals \(B_{q+\cdot}-B_q\) on each event \(\{\rho_n=q\}\). Continuity of the paths gives \(X^{(n)}_r\to B_{\rho+r}-B_\rho\) for every \(r\) and every outcome, as \(n\to\infty\). The steps skipped: (i) the extension of that independence from finitely many increments after \(q\) to the whole path \((B_{q+r}-B_q)_{r\ge 0}\), which needs Assumption A4(a) applied to the \(\pi\)-system of cylinder events; and (ii) the passage to the limit in \(\E[f(X^{(n)})\indic_A]\) for \(A\in\mathcal F_\rho\) and bounded continuous \(f\) on the path space. Step (ii) needs \(A\cap\{\rho_n=q\}\in\mathcal F_q\), which follows from \(\mathcal F_\rho\subseteq\mathcal F_{\rho_n}\) because \(\rho\le\rho_n\), and then dominated convergence. The sketch does not check that the limit identifies the law of \(X\) on the path space.
      
        
## 4Where the canon breaks
        
        
- Dependent increments. The proof of Theorem 1.4 uses (D3) only in Lemma 1.3. This page says nothing about the conditional law of \(B_t\) given \(\mathcal F_s\) when (D3) fails.
        
- Discontinuous paths. Sections 1 and 2 need no continuity. A3 is assumed for a process with continuous paths. The process \(\tilde B\) of Lesson 1, Section 4, satisfies (D1) to (D3) with discontinuous paths, and A3 is not asserted for it.
        
- Unbounded stopping times. A3 is stated for \(\rho\le c\). The strong Markov property at a stopping time that may be infinite is not covered by A3 as stated. Lesson 8 studies the first hitting time of a level, and its finiteness is a result there, not here.
        
- The sketch. A3 is not proved on this page. Every result that uses it carries the assumption, and the step that the sketch skips is named above.
      
      
      
        
## 5Misconceptions
        
          
- 
“The Markov property says that \(B_t\) is independent of the past.”
            
By Problem P2, the conditional distribution of \(B_t\) given \(\mathcal F_s\) is \(\Normal(B_s,t-s)\), whose mean is \(B_s\). The conditional law depends on \(B_s\), so \(B_t\) is not independent of the past. The Markov property says that the conditional law depends on the past only through \(B_s\).
          
          
- 
“The transition density from \(B_s=x\) is the marginal density of \(B_t\).”
            
The conditional law of \(B_t\) given \(B_s=x\) has density \(p_{t-s}(x,\cdot)\), which is centred at \(x\) with variance \(t-s\). The marginal density of \(B_t\) is \(p_t(0,\cdot)\). The two coincide only when \(x=0\) and \(s=0\), because \(\Normal(x,t-s)=\Normal(0,t)\) forces \(x=0\) and \(t-s=t\).
          
          
- 
“The 225 cases of Lab 1 prove Chapman-Kolmogorov.”
            
Lab 1 checks 225 points of a grid, with quadrature. It shows agreement at those points, with a largest gap of \(5.55\times10^{-16}\). Theorem 2.1 is the proof, and it holds for all \(u,v>0\) and all \(x,y\in\R\). The lab is a check of the algebra, not a substitute for it.

## Mechanics
          
            M1 · Chapman-Kolmogorov at the origin
Take \(u=v=1\) and \(x=y=0\). Compute \(\int_{\R}p_1(0,z)\,p_1(z,0)\,dz\) and \(p_2(0,0)\), and confirm Theorem 2.1 in this case.
            
Solution
              
Here \(p_1(0,z)\,p_1(z,0)=(2\pi)^{-1}e^{-z^2/2}e^{-z^2/2}=(2\pi)^{-1}e^{-z^2}\). The integral is \((2\pi)^{-1}\int_{\R}e^{-z^2}\,dz=(2\pi)^{-1}\sqrt{\pi}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\), using the Gaussian integral with \(c=2\). Also \(p_2(0,0)=(4\pi)^{-1/2}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}}\). The two values agree, as Theorem 2.1 states.
            
          
          
            M2 · Conditional moments of a Brownian motion
For \(s=1\) and \(t=3\), find \(\E[B_3^2\mid\mathcal F_1]\) and \(\E[B_3^3\mid\mathcal F_1]\) as functions of \(B_1\).
            
Solution
              
By Theorem 1.4, \(\E[g(B_3)\mid\mathcal F_1]=\psi(B_1)\) with \(\psi(x)=\E[(x+Z)^k]\), where \(Z\) has the law \(\Normal(0,2)\). For \(k=2\), \(\psi(x)=x^2+2\E[Z]x+\E[Z^2]=x^2+2\), since \(\E Z=0\) and \(\E Z^2=2\). For \(k=3\), \(\psi(x)=x^3+3x\E[Z^2]+\E[Z^3]=x^3+6x\), since \(\E[Z^3]=0\) by symmetry of \(\Normal(0,2)\). So \(\E[B_3^2\mid\mathcal F_1]=B_1^2+2\) and \(\E[B_3^3\mid\mathcal F_1]=B_1^3+6B_1\).
            
          
          
            M3 · Independence of the next increment
Using \(\Cov(B_s,B_t)=\min(s,t)\) from Proposition 1.2 of Lesson 1, show that \(B_2-B_1\) and \(B_1\) are uncorrelated. Explain why this gives independence here.
            
Solution
              
\(\Cov(B_2-B_1,B_1)=\Cov(B_2,B_1)-\Var(B_1)=\min(2,1)-1=1-1=0\). The pair \((B_1,B_2-B_1)\) is a linear image of the Gaussian vector \((B_1,B_2)\), so it is jointly normal. For jointly normal variables, zero covariance gives independence, which is Assumption A1(c) of Lesson 1. This is the case of Lemma 1.3 with \(s=1\) and \(t=2\).
            
          
        
        
          
## Proof
          
            P1 · Derive the square of Lemma 2.2
Verify Lemma 2.2 at \(u=1\), \(v=3\), \(x=0\), \(y=1\), for every \(z\). Then show that the general identity follows from the same expansion.
            
Solution
              
Here \(m=(3\cdot 0+1\cdot 1)/4=1/4\) and \((u+v)/(uv)=4/3\). The left side is \(z^2/1+(1-z)^2/3=(3z^2+1-2z+z^2)/3=(4z^2-2z+1)/3\). The right side is \((4/3)(z-1/4)^2+1/4=(4/3)(z^2-z/2+1/16)+1/4=(4z^2-2z+1/4)/3+1/4=(4z^2-2z+1/4+3/4)/3=(4z^2-2z+1)/3\). The two sides agree. The general case is the expansion in the proof of Lemma 2.2, which uses only \((u+v)m=vx+uy\) and the cancellation of \(v^2x^2+u^2y^2\).
            
          
          
            P2 · Conditional distribution function
For \(0\le s<t\) and \(c\in\R\), show that \(\Prob(B_t\le c\mid\mathcal F_s)=\Phi\big((c-B_s)/\sqrt{t-s}\big)\), using Theorem 1.4 with \(g=\indic_{(-\infty,c]}\).
            
Solution
              
Theorem 1.4 gives \(\psi(x)=\int g(y)\,p_{t-s}(x,y)\,dy=\Prob(x+Z\le c)\) with \(Z\sim\Normal(0,t-s)\). Write \(Z=\sqrt{t-s}\,W\) with \(W\) standard normal. Then \(\Prob(x+Z\le c)=\Prob\big(W\le (c-x)/\sqrt{t-s}\big)=\Phi\big((c-x)/\sqrt{t-s}\big)\), by the registered meaning of \(\Phi\) as the standard normal CDF. Setting \(x=B_s\) gives the formula.
            
          
          
            P3 · Uniqueness of the transition function
Show that \(\int_{\R}p_u(x,y)\,dy=1\) for every \(x\in\R\) and \(u>0\), using only the substitution \(y=x+\sqrt u\,w\) and the fact that the standard normal density integrates to 1.
            
Solution
              
Substitute \(y=x+\sqrt u\,w\), so \(dy=\sqrt u\,dw\) and \((y-x)^2/(2u)=w^2/2\). Then \(\int_{\R}p_u(x,y)\,dy=\int_{\R}(2\pi u)^{-1/2}e^{-w^2/2}\sqrt u\,dw\). Since \((2\pi u)^{-1/2}\sqrt u=(2\pi)^{-1/2}\), this equals \((2\pi)^{-1/2}\int_{\R}e^{-w^2/2}\,dw=1\), because the standard normal density integrates to 1.
            
          
        
        
          
## Empirical
          
            E1 · Is the truncation a source of the gaps?
From the output of Lab 1, compare the largest mass of the integrand for \(12\le|z|\le 40\) with the largest absolute quadrature gap. State which is larger and what that says about the truncation at \(|z|=12\).
            
Solution
              
Lab 1 prints the largest absolute quadrature gap as \(5.55\times10^{-16}\) and the largest mass for \(12\le|z|\le 40\) as \(3.81\times10^{-29}\). On that region the mass is smaller than the gap by a factor of about \(1.5\times10^{13}\). The ratio of the printed values is \(5.55\times10^{-16}/3.81\times10^{-29}\approx 1.457\times10^{13}\), which is \(1.5\times10^{13}\) to two significant figures; the basis is the printed values, each rounded to three significant figures. The mass for \(|z|>40\) is not computed on this page or in Lab 1, so this comparison does not cover it. The printed mass for \(12\le|z|\le 40\) is therefore far below the printed gaps, and it does not explain them. The gaps are at the scale of double-precision rounding.
            
          
          
            E2 · What the printed digits can show
Lab 1 prints \(\int_{-12}^{12}p_1(0,y)\,dy=1.0000000000\). What error bound do the printed digits support, and what does the printed value not show?
            
Solution
              
The printed value rounds to ten decimal places, so the true quadrature value lies in \([0.99999999995,\,1.00000000005]\). The digits support an error below \(5\times10^{-11}\) in absolute value. They do not show whether the error is \(10^{-16}\) or \(10^{-12}\), and they do not show the sign of the error. The conclusion "the integral is 1" is therefore supported only to \(5\times10^{-11}\).
            
          
          
            E3 · The sign of a rounding gap
The Lab 1 example line prints a gap of \(-3.05\times10^{-16}\) for \(s=1\), \(t=2\), \(x=0.5\), \(y=-0.25\). Does the sign carry information about the quadrature?
            
Solution
              
No. The magnitude \(3.05\times10^{-16}\) is about 1.4 times the double-precision machine epsilon \(2.2\times10^{-16}\), which is the scale on which rounding can change the last digit of a quantity near 1, so the sign of this one value is not evidence about the quadrature. The lab does not compare the sign with any other quantity and does not claim that it is meaningful.
            
          
        
        
          
## Research
          
            R1 · A time of maximum that is not a stopping time
Let \(\sigma^\ast\) be the time at which \(s\mapsto B_s\) attains its maximum over \([0,1]\). Show that \(\sigma^\ast\) is not a stopping time, by exhibiting two continuous paths that agree on \([0,\tfrac12]\) and give different answers to \(\{\sigma^\ast\le\tfrac12\}\).
            
Solution
              
Let path 1 rise linearly from 0 to 1 on \([0,\tfrac14]\), fall linearly to \(\tfrac12\) on \([\tfrac14,\tfrac12]\), and stay at \(\tfrac12\) on \([\tfrac12,1]\). Its maximum over \([0,1]\) is attained at \(\tfrac14\), so \(\sigma^\ast=\tfrac14\le\tfrac12\). Let path 2 agree with path 1 on \([0,\tfrac12]\), then rise linearly from \(\tfrac12\) to 2 on \([\tfrac12,\tfrac34]\) and fall to 0 at time 1. Its maximum is at \(\tfrac34\), so \(\sigma^\ast=\tfrac34>\tfrac12\). Both paths are continuous. Take \(\Omega\) to be the space of continuous paths with \(B\) the coordinate process, a choice made for this problem only; then both paths are outcomes. By Assumption A4(d), the indicator of an event of \(\mathcal F_{1/2}\) is a function of the path on \([0,\tfrac12]\). The two paths agree there, but the indicator of \(\{\sigma^\ast\le\tfrac12\}\) takes different values on them, so \(\{\sigma^\ast\le\tfrac12\}\notin\mathcal F_{1/2}\). Hence \(\sigma^\ast\) is not a stopping time, and A3 does not apply to it.
            
          
          
            R2 · A3 at a deterministic time
Let \(s\ge 0\) be a constant. Show that \(\rho\equiv s\) is a stopping time, and that A3 applied to \(\rho\) contains the independence of Lemma 1.3.
            
Solution
              
For \(r\ge 0\), \(\{\rho\le r\}\) is the whole space if \(s\le r\) and the empty set otherwise. Both lie in \(\mathcal F_r\), so \(\rho\) is a stopping time. Here \(\mathcal F_\rho=\mathcal F_s\), because \(A\cap\{s\le r\}\in\mathcal F_r\) for all \(r\) is equivalent to \(A\in\mathcal F_s\) when \(A\in\mathcal F\). Then \(X_r=B_{s+r}-B_s\) is a standard Brownian motion by (D1) to (D3) applied to the shifted times, and A3 asserts that it is independent of \(\mathcal F_s\). Independence of \(B_t-B_s=X_{t-s}\) from \(\mathcal F_s\) is the conclusion of Lemma 1.3.
            
          
          
            R3 · Dyadic approximation from above
Let \(\rho\) be a stopping time with \(\rho\le c\) for a constant \(c<\infty\), and let \(\rho_n=\lceil 2^n\rho\rceil/2^n\). Show that \(\rho\le\rho_n\le\rho+2^{-n}\), and that \(\rho_n\) is a stopping time.
            
Solution
              
For any real \(x\), \(x\le\lceil x\rceil<x+1\). With \(x=2^n\rho\), dividing by \(2^n\) gives \(\rho\le\rho_n<\rho+2^{-n}\). For the stopping property, \(\lceil 2^n\rho\rceil\le m\) holds exactly when \(2^n\rho\le m\) for an integer \(m\). So \(\{\rho_n\le r\}=\{\rho\le k/2^n\}\) with \(k=\lfloor 2^n r\rfloor\). Since \(k/2^n\le r\), the event \(\{\rho\le k/2^n\}\) lies in \(\mathcal F_{k/2^n}\subseteq\mathcal F_r\), so \(\rho_n\) is a stopping time.
