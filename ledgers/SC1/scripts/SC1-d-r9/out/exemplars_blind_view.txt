          <h4 id="exemplar-a-title">Exemplar A</h4>
          [VERDICT HIDDEN]
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>Summary</h5>
              <p>For seed 0 the tail is about 0.0865, and the grid estimates scatter around it. The reflection principle holds, so the running maximum follows the same law as twice the endpoint tail, and the first-passage identity follows from it. The results hold for every grid, as the run shows.</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
          </div>
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>The checks</h5>
              <p>The tail is \(\operatorname{erfc}(2.474/\sqrt{2 \cdot 2.083})\), which the run gives as 0.0865. The walk of 12 steps has 1588 walks reaching 3. The one-step estimate is 0.0595, with a standard error of 0.0107, and it lies 0.0163 above half the tail (0.0432), which is 1.52 printed standard errors (0.0163 / 0.0107).</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
          </div>
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>Appendix · Proofs</h5>
              <p><strong>P1.</strong> Let \(\tau = \tau_a\), and let \(\rho\) be \(\tau\) cut off at \(t\). The paths are continuous, so \(\{\tau \le t\}\) is the event that the path has reached \(a\) by time \(t\). By symmetry, the restarted path is as likely to be above \(a\) as below, so \(\Prob(\tau \le t, B_t > a) = \tfrac12 \Prob(\tau \le t)\). Adding the paths that end at or above \(a\) gives \(\Prob(M_t \ge a) = 2\,\Prob(B_t \ge a)\).</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
          </div>
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>Discretization</h5>
              <p>Finer grids give estimates closer to the continuous tail, so the discretization error falls as the grid refines.</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
          </div>

        <article class="exemplar" id="exemplar-b" data-level="4" data-scores="4,4,4,3,4" data-label="Exemplar B" aria-labelledby="exemplar-b-title">
          <h4 id="exemplar-b-title">Exemplar B</h4>
          [VERDICT HIDDEN]
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>Summary</h5>
              <p>For seed 0 (H = 2.083, a = 2.474, N = 487 paths), the continuous tail is \(\Prob(M_H \ge a) = 0.086497\). The closed form matches Simpson's rule to within 10 to the minus 14 in absolute terms. The one-step estimate, 0.059548 with standard error 0.010735, lies within the band for half the tail. On the same 256-step paths the coarse counts never exceed the fine count of 40.</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
          </div>
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>Setup and conventions</h5>
              <p>The horizon is \(H\), not \(T\). The level is \(a\), and \(N\) is the number of paths. <code>reflection_tail</code> computes the continuous tail, <code>discrete_max_exceedance</code> the grid estimates with their standard errors, and <code>nested_grid_counts</code> the same-path counts. Every computed decimal is the unrounded float, rounded once to six places by Python's formatting, except the Simpson difference, which is given to three significant digits. The horizon and the level are printed as generated, to three decimals, and <code>N</code> is an integer. The walk counts are exact integers from <code>srw_max_count</code> and <code>srw_terminal_count</code>.</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
          </div>
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>The checks</h5>
              <p>The continuous tail is 0.086497. Simpson's rule for \(2\,\Prob(B_H \ge a)\) differs from it by 9.13e-15 in absolute value, as Lab 1 prints. The one-step row is the grid of one step, whose maximum is \(B_H\), so its target is half the tail, 0.043248. Its estimate is 0.059548, 0.016300 above the target, which is 1.52 printed standard errors (0.016300 / 0.010735) and inside the band of five target standard errors plus one path, 0.048142. For the walk of 12 steps at level 3, Lab 3 prints 1588 walks reaching the level and 794 ending at 3 or above.</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
          </div>
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>Discretization</h5>
              <p>Lab 3 gives the grid estimates for 1, 4, 16, 64 and 256 steps. Their standard errors are 0.010735, 0.009819, 0.011239, 0.012870 and 0.012455. The tail minus each estimate is 0.026948 at 1 step, 0.037215 at 4 steps, 0.020788 at 16 steps, −0.001799 at 64 steps and 0.004361 at 256 steps. The one-step bias is tail/2 = 0.043248, so the printed figure 0.026948 estimates it with sampling error −0.016300, which is 1.52 standard errors. These rows use separate paths, so their differences mix bias with noise, and I do not order them by bias. On the 256-step paths, the nested counts are 35, 29, 27 and 22 for coarse grids of 64, 16, 4 and 1 steps, each at most the fine count of 40. The coarse maximum is at most the fine maximum on every path, so the coarse grid never has the higher estimate on these paths.</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
          </div>
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>Limitations</h5>
              <p>The check uses one seed, one instance and 487 paths, so it is a sample and not a proof. The tail is for the continuous maximum on \([0, H]\), and each grid estimate is a sample from a discrete law. The nested inequality holds on every path, but these counts come from one sample, so they say nothing about the size of the bias for other seeds or other path counts, and nothing about any statement that holds almost surely. The three results are proved for every case that meets their assumptions, in the appendix.</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
          </div>
          <div class="passage">
            <div class="memo-text">
              <h5>Appendix · Proofs</h5>
              <p><strong>Assumptions for P1.</strong></p>
              <ol class="assumptions"><li>A0: \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion with \(B_0 = 0\) and continuous paths.</li><li>A3 of Lesson 5, for the bounded stopping time \(\rho = \tau \wedge t\); A4(b), A4(c), A4(g) and A4(h) of Lesson 5, for the freezing step, the first hitting time \(\tau\), the joint measurability of the restarted path, and the \(\mathcal F_\rho\)-measurability of \(B_\rho\) and \(\rho\).</li><li>\(a > 0\) and \(t > 0\) are fixed.</li></ol>
              <p><strong>P1.</strong> Let \(\tau = \tau_a\), and let \(\rho = \tau \wedge t\). By A4(c), \(\tau\) is a stopping time. So \(\rho\) is one as well: \(\{\rho \le r\} = \{\tau \le r\}\) for \(r &lt; t\), and \(\{\rho \le r\}\) is the whole space for \(r \ge t\), so both lie in \(\mathcal F_r\), by the definition of a stopping time in Section 3 of Lesson 5. Also \(\rho \le t\), so \(\rho\) is bounded and A3 applies to it. Let \(A = \{\tau \le t\}\). If \(\tau \le t\), then \(B_\tau = a\) by continuity, so \(M_t \ge a\). If \(M_t \ge a\), the maximum is attained at some \(s \le t\), because a continuous function attains its maximum on the compact interval \([0,t]\). The intermediate value theorem on \([0,s]\), with \(B_0 = 0 &lt; a\) (A0 and \(a &gt; 0\)), gives a time in \([0,s]\) at which \(B = a\), so \(\tau \le t\). Hence \(A = \{M_t \ge a\}\). Also \(\Prob(\tau = t) \le \Prob(B_t = a) = 0\), because \(\tau = t\) implies \(B_t = a\), and \(B_t\) is normal with variance \(t &gt; 0\) (A0 and Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1), so it has no atom. Let \(\tilde B_r = B_{\rho + r} - B_\rho\). By A3, \(\tilde B\) is a standard Brownian motion independent of \(\mathcal F_\rho\). On \(A\), \(\rho = \tau\) and \(B_\rho = a\), so \(B_t - a = \tilde B_{t - \rho}\). The variable \(\rho\) is \(\mathcal F_\rho\)-measurable by A4(h), so \(t - \rho\) is too. The event \(A\) is \(\mathcal F_\rho\)-measurable: for \(r \ge t\), \(A \cap \{\rho \le r\} = A \in \mathcal F_t \subseteq \mathcal F_r\), because \(A = \{\tau \le t\} \in \mathcal F_t\) by the stopping property of \(\tau\); for \(r &lt; t\), \(A \cap \{\rho \le r\} = \{\tau \le r\} \in \mathcal F_r\). Apply A4(b) with \(\mathcal G = \mathcal F_\rho\), \(Z = (t - \rho, \indic_A)\) and \(Y = \tilde B\), which is independent of \(\mathcal F_\rho\) by A3. The function \(f(z,y) = \indic_{\{z_2 = 1\}}\indic_{\{y(z_1) &lt; 0\}}\) is bounded, and it is jointly measurable because \((u,\omega) \mapsto \tilde B_u(\omega)\) is jointly measurable by A4(g). Since \(\Prob(\tilde B_u &lt; 0) = \tfrac12\) for \(u &gt; 0\) (\(\tilde B_u\) is normal with mean 0) and \(\tilde B_0 = 0\), we get \(\bar f(z) = \tfrac12\indic_{\{z_2 = 1\}}\indic_{\{z_1 &gt; 0\}}\). So A4(b) gives \(\Prob(A, B_t &lt; a) = \Prob(A, \tilde B_{t-\rho} &lt; 0) = \tfrac12\Prob(A, \rho &lt; t) = \tfrac12\Prob(A)\), the last step because \(\Prob(\tau = t) = 0\). Also \(B_t \ge a\) implies \(M_t \ge a\), so \(\{B_t \ge a\} \subseteq A\), and \(\Prob(A) = \Prob(B_t \ge a) + \Prob(A, B_t &lt; a) = \Prob(B_t \ge a) + \tfrac12\Prob(A)\). Therefore \(\Prob(M_t \ge a) = \Prob(A) = 2\,\Prob(B_t \ge a)\). Since \(\Prob(B_t = a) = 0\), this equals \(2\,\Prob(B_t &gt; a)\).</p>
              <p><strong>Assumptions for P2.</strong></p>
              <ol class="assumptions"><li>The assumptions of P1.</li><li>\(B_t \sim \Normal(0,t)\), by the definition of Brownian motion in Lesson 1 (Definition 1.1: \(B_0 = 0\), and each increment is normal with variance equal to its length).</li></ol>
              <p><strong>P2.</strong> By P1 and \(B_t \sim \Normal(0,t)\), \(\Prob(B_t \ge a) = 1 - \Phi(a/\sqrt{t})\), so \(\Prob(M_t \ge a) = 2(1 - \Phi(a/\sqrt{t}))\). For the erfc form, substitute \(w = z/\sqrt{2}\) in \(\operatorname{erfc}(x) = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_x^\infty e^{-w^2}\,dw\): this gives \(\operatorname{erfc}(x) = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{x\sqrt2}^\infty e^{-z^2/2}\,dz = 2(1 - \Phi(x\sqrt{2}))\). With \(x = a/\sqrt{2t}\), \(x\sqrt{2} = a/\sqrt{t}\), so \(\operatorname{erfc}(a/\sqrt{2t}) = 2(1 - \Phi(a/\sqrt{t}))\).</p>
              <p><strong>Assumptions for P3.</strong></p>
              <ol class="assumptions"><li>A0 with continuous paths, and \(a > 0\), for the equality of the events.</li><li>The assumptions of P2, for the second part.</li></ol>
              <p><strong>P3.</strong> The equality \(\{\tau_a \le t\} = \{M_t \ge a\}\) for every \(t \ge 0\) is the first part of the P1 argument, and it uses continuity, \(B_0 = 0\) and \(a > 0\). For the second part, \(\{\tau_a &lt; \infty\} = \bigcup_{n} \{\tau_a \le n\}\), an increasing union. By continuity of probability from below and P2, \(\Prob(\tau_a &lt; \infty) = \lim_n \Prob(M_n \ge a) = \lim_n \operatorname{erfc}(a/\sqrt{2n}) = \operatorname{erfc}(0) = 1\).</p>
            </div>
            [ANNOTATION HIDDEN]
