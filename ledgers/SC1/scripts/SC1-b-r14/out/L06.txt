52:       Reflect a Brownian path after its first visit to a level \(a\). The reflected value at a fixed time has the same law as the original value (Proposition 2.3, under Assumptions A3 and A4 of Lesson 5); the path-level reflection is proved in Proposition 2.7. The reflection turns "how far does the path rise" into "where does it end", a Gaussian answer. The lesson proves the discrete version exactly for the symmetric simple random walk (defined in Section 1), including the correction term \(\Prob(S_N=a)\). It then proves the continuous identity \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge a)\), where \(M_t\) is the running maximum of \(B\) on \([0,t]\), under the strong Markov property of Lesson 5 (Assumption A3) and the facts of Assumption A4.
54:       1The discrete reflection principle, exactly
56:       Let \(N\ge 1\) be an integer. A step sequence is \((e_1,\dots,e_N)\in\{-1,1\}^N\). The walk is \(S_0=0\) and \(S_k=e_1+\dots+e_k\) for \(1\le k\le N\). This is the simple random walk of length \(N\). Its \(2^N\) step sequences have equal weight \(2^{-N}\), so each probability below is a count divided by \(2^N\). The running maximum is \(M_N=\max_{0\le k\le N}S_k\). For an integer level \(a\ge 1\), let \(\kappa=\min\{k : S_k=a\}\) be the first visit to \(a\), when it occurs at some \(k\le N\). Because the walk moves by \(\pm 1\), it visits every integer between \(0\) and \(S_N\).
59:         Definition 1.1 (reflected walk)
60:         Let \(a\ge 1\) and let the step sequence satisfy \(\kappa\le N\). The reflected walk has steps \(e'_i=e_i\) for \(i\le\kappa\) and \(e'_i=-e_i\) for \(\kappa<i\le N\). Its walk is \(S'_k\).
64:         Lemma 1.2 (the reflected walk)
65:         Assumptions. \(N\ge 1\), \(a\ge 1\), and a step sequence with \(\kappa\le N\).
66:         Statement. \(S'_k=S_k\) for \(k\le\kappa\), and \(S'_k=2a-S_k\) for \(\kappa\le k\le N\). In particular \(S'_\kappa=a\), \(S'_N=2a-S_N\), and \(\kappa'=\kappa\), where \(\kappa'=\min\{k : S'_k=a\}\) is the first visit of the reflected walk to \(a\). Reflection is an involution on the step sequences with \(\kappa\le N\).
70:         Proof
71:         For \(k\le\kappa\) the steps agree, so \(S'_k=S_k\). For \(k\ge\kappa\), \(S'_k=S'_\kappa+\sum_{\kappa<i\le k}(-e_i)=a-(S_k-S_\kappa)=a-(S_k-a)=2a-S_k\), using \(S_\kappa=a\) and \(S'_\kappa=S_\kappa\). The prefix up to \(\kappa\) is unchanged, so \(\kappa'=\kappa\), because \(\kappa\) depends only on the prefix. Applying the reflection to \((e'_i)\) flips the steps after \(\kappa\) back, so it returns \((e_i)\). \(\blacksquare\)

77:         Statement. The number of step sequences with \(M_N\ge a\) and \(S_N=b\) equals the number of step sequences with \(S_N=2a-b\).

87:         Corollary 1.4 (discrete tail identity)
88:         Assumptions. Those of Proposition 1.3, for every integer \(a\ge 1\).
89:         Statement. \(\#\{M_N\ge a\}=\#\{S_N\ge a\}+\#\{S_N>a\}\), where \(\#\) counts step sequences. Hence
90:         \[
91:           \Prob(M_N\ge a)=\Prob(S_N\ge a)+\Prob(S_N>a)=2\,\Prob(S_N>a)+\Prob(S_N=a).
92:         \]
96:         Proof
97:         Split \(\{M_N\ge a\}\) by the value of \(S_N\). Every sequence with \(S_N\ge a\) has \(M_N\ge a\). For \(b<a\), the sequences with \(M_N\ge a\) and \(S_N=b\) are counted by Proposition 1.3. So \(\#\{M_N\ge a\}=\#\{S_N\ge a\}+\sum_{b<a}\#\{S_N=2a-b\}\). The map \(b\mapsto 2a-b\) is a bijection from the integers below \(a\) onto the integers above \(a\), so the sum is \(\#\{S_N>a\}\). The probability form divides by \(2^N\), and \(\Prob(S_N\ge a)=\Prob(S_N>a)+\Prob(S_N=a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

123:         Proposition 2.3 (the reflected value has the law of B at a fixed time)
124:         Assumptions.
126:           \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion with continuous paths, and \(a>0\), \(t>0\).
127:           Assumption A3 of Lesson 5 (strong Markov property), applied to the bounded stopping time \(\rho=\tau_a\wedge t\).
128:           Assumptions A4(b), A4(c), A4(g) and A4(h) of Lesson 5 (freezing; hitting times are stopping times; joint measurability; measurability at a stopping time).

134:         Proof
135:         By A4(c), \(\tau_a\) is a stopping time, because \(\{a\}\) is closed and \(B\) is continuous and adapted (each \(B_r\) is \(\mathcal F_r\)-measurable). 136:         Let \(X_r=B_{\rho+r}-B_\rho\) for \(r\ge 0\). By A3, \(X\) is a standard Brownian motion, independent of \(\mathcal F_\rho\). Since \(\rho+(t-\rho)=t\), we have \(B_t-B_\rho=X_{t-\rho}\), and therefore
137:         \[
138:           W_t=2B_\rho-B_t=B_\rho-X_{t-\rho},\qquad B_t=B_\rho+X_{t-\rho}.
139:         \]
140:         Let \(g\) be bounded and Borel. Also \(X\) is independent of \(\mathcal F_\rho\). Assumption A4(g) makes \((u,\omega)\mapsto X_u(\omega)\) jointly measurable on \([0,t]\times\Omega\), so \(X_{t-\rho}\) is a random variable. 142:           \E[g(W_t)\mid\mathcal F_\rho]=h_-(B_\rho,t-\rho),\qquad \E[g(B_t)\mid\mathcal F_\rho]=h_+(B_\rho,t-\rho),
143:         \]
144:         where \(h_\mp(x,u)=\E[g(x\mp X_u)]\). Since \(X_u\) has the law \(\Normal(0,u)\) by (D1) and (D2) for \(X\), and this law is symmetric, \(-X_u\) has the same law as \(X_u\). So \(h_-(x,u)=h_+(x,u)\) for all \(x\) and \(u\in[0,t]\). Hence \(\E[g(W_t)\mid\mathcal F_\rho]=\E[g(B_t)\mid\mathcal F_\rho]\) almost surely (that is, with probability one). Taking expectations gives \(\E g(W_t)=\E g(B_t)\) for every bounded Borel \(g\), so \(W_t\) and \(B_t\) have the same law. \(\blacksquare\)

153:         Theorem 2.5 (the reflection identity for Brownian motion)
154:         Assumptions.
156:           \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion with continuous paths, and \(a>0\), \(t>0\).
157:           Assumption A3 of Lesson 5 (applied in Proposition 2.3) and Assumption A4 of Lesson 5.
159:         Statement. Assuming A3 and A4,
160:         \[
161:           \Prob(M_t\ge a)=\Prob(B_t\ge a)+\Prob(B_t>a)=2\,\Prob(B_t>a)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge a).
162:         \]
166:         Proof
167:         Let \(\rho\) and \(W_t\) be as in Proposition 2.3. Write \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\) for the event of Lemma 2.1(i), so \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)=\Prob(\tau_a\le t)\).
168:         By Lemma 2.1(ii), \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\) contains \(\{B_t\ge a\}\). Split \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\) into \(\{B_t\ge a\}\) and \(E=\{\tau_a\le t, B_t<a\}\). So \(\Prob(\tau_a\le t)=\Prob(B_t\ge a)+\Prob(E)\).
169:         We identify \(E\). On \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\), \(W_t=2a-B_t\), so \(W_t>a\) exactly when \(B_t<a\). Hence \(E=\{\tau_a\le t,\,W_t>a\}\). The event \(\{W_t>a\}\) lies inside \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\): on \(\{\tau_a>t\}\), \(W_t=B_t<a\) by Lemma 2.1(iii). So \(E=\{W_t>a\}\).
170:         By Proposition 2.3, \(\Prob(W_t>a)=\Prob(B_t>a)\). Therefore \(\Prob(\tau_a\le t)=\Prob(B_t\ge a)+\Prob(B_t>a)\). Since \(B_t\) has the normal law \(\Normal(0,t)\) with \(t>0\), it has a density, so \(\Prob(B_t=a)=0\). The two tail probabilities are equal, and the result is \(2\,\Prob(B_t>a)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge a)\). \(\blacksquare\)
174:         Corollary 2.6 (closed form of the tail)
175:         Assumptions. \(t>0\), \(a>0\), and the standard definition \(\operatorname{erfc}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_x^\infty e^{-w^2}\,dw\).

326:         3Where the canon breaks
328:         Discrete grid. Corollary 1.4 has the extra term \(\Prob(S_N=a)\) that Theorem 2.5 does not have. Part D of Lab 1 shows the term at \(N=10\), \(a=2\). This page does not compute how the term behaves as \(N\) grows.
329:         Path continuity. Lemma 2.1 uses the intermediate value theorem, which needs continuous paths. A path that jumps from below \(a\) to above \(a\) has \(M_t\ge a\) without ever taking the value \(a\), so \(\{M_t\ge a\}\neq\{\tau_a\le t\}\) for such a path. The process \(\tilde B\) of Lesson 1, Section 4, has discontinuous paths, and nothing on this page applies it.
330:         Infinite hitting times. Propositions 2.3 and 2.7 use \(\rho=\tau_a\wedge t\), which is bounded. The page does not establish that \(\tau_a\) is finite, and it does not state the law of \(\tau_a\). Lesson 8 does.
331:         Symmetry. The factor 2 comes from adding two events of equal probability in the proof of Theorem 2.5. Since \(E=\{W_t>a\}\), Proposition 2.3 gives \(\Prob(E)=\Prob(B_t>a)\), and that proposition uses the symmetry of \(\Normal(0,u)\) at the step \(h_-=h_+\). The fact \(\Prob(B_t=a)=0\), that \(B_t\) has no atom (no point of positive probability), follows from the normal density, and it is also needed to make \(\Prob(B_t\ge a)=\Prob(B_t>a)\). The page makes no claim about the tail of a process whose increments are not symmetric.
332:         Assumptions. Theorem 2.5 is conditional on A3 and A4 of Lesson 5, which are stated and not proved there. The path-level reflection principle is proved in Proposition 2.7, with A3 applied only to the bounded times \(\tau_a\wedge t\). Theorem 2.5 uses only the fixed-time form.

360:             M1 · Count the paths of length 3 that reach 1
361:             For \(N=3\) and \(a=1\), list the step sequences with \(M_3\ge 1\), count them, and check Corollary 1.4.
362:             Solution
363:               The sequences with \(e_1=+1\) reach 1 at \(k=1\): there are 4 of them. The sequence \((-1,+1,+1)\) has \(S=(0,-1,0,1)\), which reaches 1, so it counts. The other three sequences starting with \(-1\), namely \((-1,-1,-1)\), \((-1,+1,-1)\) and \((-1,-1,+1)\), have maxima 0, 0 and 0, so they do not count. The count is \(4+1=5\) of \(8\). For Corollary 1.4: \(\#\{S_3\ge 1\}=3+1=4\), from \(S_3=1\) (three sequences) and \(S_3=3\) (one), and \(\#\{S_3>1\}=1\). The sum is \(5\), which agrees.
367:             M2 · The probability of reaching 2 in two steps
368:             For \(N=2\) and \(a=2\), find \(\Prob(M_2\ge 2)\), and check Corollary 1.4.
369:             Solution
370:               Only the sequence \((+1,+1)\) reaches 2, so \(\Prob(M_2\ge 2)=1/4\). Corollary 1.4 gives \(\#\{S_2\ge 2\}+\#\{S_2>2\}=1+0=1\), since \(S_2\ge 2\) only for \((+1,+1)\) and \(S_2\) cannot exceed 2. The probability is \(1/4\), as before.
374:             M3 · Reflect a specific path
375:             Take \(N=5\), \(a=2\), and steps \((+1,+1,-1,-1,+1)\). Find \(\kappa\), the reflected steps, the reflected path and its endpoint, and check Lemma 1.2.
376:             Solution
377:               The path is \(S=(0,1,2,1,0,1)\), so \(\kappa=2\). The steps after \(\kappa\) are \((-1,-1,+1)\), which reflect to \((+1,+1,-1)\). The reflected steps are \((+1,+1,+1,+1,-1)\), with path \(S'=(0,1,2,3,4,3)\). Lemma 1.2 says \(S'_k=2a-S_k=4-S_k\) for \(k\ge 2\): \(4-2=2\), \(4-1=3\), \(4-0=4\), \(4-1=3\). 384:             P1 · Reflection is an involution
385:             Show that applying the reflection of Definition 1.1 twice returns the original step sequence, for every step sequence with \(\kappa\le N\).
386:             Solution
387:               Let \(e'\) be the reflected steps of \(e\). Then \(e'_i=e_i\) for \(i\le\kappa\) and \(e'_i=-e_i\) for \(\kappa<i\le N\). The first time the walk built from \(e'\) reaches \(a\) is \(\kappa'=\kappa\), by Lemma 1.2, so the reflection of \(e'\) negates the steps after the same \(\kappa\). Negating twice gives \(e''_i=e'_i\) for \(i\le\kappa\) and \(e''_i=-e'_i=e_i\) for \(\kappa<i\le N\). So \(e''=e\).
391:             P2 · Parts (ii) and (iii) of Lemma 2.1 from continuity
392:             Prove Lemma 2.1 (ii) and (iii) from continuity of \(B\) and \(B_0=0\), without using part (i).
393:             Solution
394:               (ii) If \(B_t\ge a\) with \(a>0=B_0\), then the continuous function \(s\mapsto B_s\) on \([0,t]\) takes the values 0 at time 0 and at least \(a\) at time \(t\). The intermediate value theorem gives some \(s\in[0,t]\) with \(B_s=a\), so \(\tau_a\le s\le t\). (iii) Suppose \(\tau_a>t\), so \(B_s\neq a\) for all \(s\le t\) by the definition of \(\tau_a\). If \(B_s>a\) for some \(s\le t\), the intermediate value theorem on \([0,s]\) gives a time in \((0,s)\) where \(B=a\), which would make \(\tau_a<s\le t\). So \(B_s<a\) for all \(s\le t\), and in particular \(B_t<a\).
398:             P3 · Corollary 1.4 from Proposition 1.3
399:             Derive \(\#\{M_N\ge a\}=\#\{S_N\ge a\}+\#\{S_N>a\}\) from Proposition 1.3 for a single integer \(a\ge 1\), without repeating the bijection.
400:             Solution
401:               Split the sequences with \(M_N\ge a\) by \(S_N\). Those with \(S_N\ge a\) all have \(M_N\ge a\), and there are \(\#\{S_N\ge a\}\) of them. Those with \(S_N=b<a\) and \(M_N\ge a\) are counted, for each \(b\), by \(\#\{S_N=2a-b\}\), from Proposition 1.3. As \(b\) runs over the integers below \(a\), \(2a-b\) runs over the integers above \(a\), so the sum is \(\#\{S_N>a\}\).
