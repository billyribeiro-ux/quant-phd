  36: A martingale is a process whose best forecast of its future value is its present value. Brownian motion is the basic example in continuous time. This lesson defines continuous-time martingales relative to a filtration, and it proves that \(B_t\), \(B_t^2 - t\) and the exponential \(\exp(\theta B_t - \theta^2 t/2)\) are martingales. The proofs use two stated hypotheses: each future increment is independent of the past, and the standard properties of conditional expectation hold. Lab 1 checks the expectation of the exponential martingale by quadrature.

  38: 1Filtrations and continuous-time martingales

  40: Let \((\Omega, \mathcal F, \Prob)\) be a [CONCEPT-LINK probability-space]probability space on which the Brownian motion \((B_t)_{t \ge 0}\) of Lesson 1 is defined. Lesson 1 gives its laws, and this lesson adds the information that is available over time.

  42: Definition 1.1 (filtration in continuous time)

  44: A family \((\mathcal F_t)_{t \ge 0}\) of sub-\(\sigma\)-algebras of \(\mathcal F\) is a [DFN id=None concept=None]filtration in continuous time when \(\mathcal F_s \subseteq \mathcal F_t\) for \(0 \le s \le t\). It is the continuous-time form of the discrete [CONCEPT-LINK filtration]filtration of M6, with the index set \([0,\infty)\) in place of \(\{0, 1, \dots, N\}\). The natural filtration of \(B\) is \(\mathcal F_t = \sigma(B_u : 0 \le u \le t)\), the smallest \(\sigma\)-algebra under which each \(B_u\) with \(u \le t\) is measurable.

  46: Assumption 4.1 (increments independent of the past)

  48: Let \((\mathcal F_t)\) be a filtration such that \(B_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable for each \(t\), and for \(0 \le s \le t\) the increment \(B_t - B_s\) is independent of \(\mathcal F_s\).

  50: Sketch, not a proof, for the natural filtration of \(B\). Let \(s \le t\). Each \(B_{u}\) with \(u \le s\) is a sum of increments over disjoint intervals inside \([0,s]\), by (D1) of Lesson 1. So the vector \((B_{u_1}, \dots, B_{u_m})\) is a function of increments over intervals inside \([0,s]\). Those increments are independent of \(B_t - B_s\) by (D3) of Lesson 1, and the grouping of independent blocks, which the sketch takes as a standard fact and does not prove, makes the vector independent of \(B_t - B_s\). Assumption 3.2(a) of Lesson 3 covers functions of single variables, not blocks. The sketch skips two steps: that the finite-dimensional cylinder sets \(\{(B_{u_1}, \dots, B_{u_m}) \in A\}\) form a \(\pi\)-system (a family closed under intersection) generating \(\mathcal F_s\), and the \(\pi\)–\(\lambda\) extension of the independence from these sets to all of \(\mathcal F_s\) (independence that holds for the sets of a \(\pi\)-system holds for the \(\sigma\)-algebra it generates).

  52: Assumption 4.2 (standard properties of conditional expectation)

  54: Let \(\mathcal G \subseteq \mathcal F\) be a \(\sigma\)-algebra and let \(X\), \(Y\) be square-integrable. (a) Linearity: \(\E[aX + bY \mid \mathcal G] = a\,\E[X \mid \mathcal G] + b\,\E[Y \mid \mathcal G]\). (b) Tower: \(\E[\E[X \mid \mathcal G]] = \E[X]\). (c) Pull-out: if \(X\) is \(\mathcal G\)-measurable and \(XY\) is integrable, then \(\E[XY \mid \mathcal G] = X\,\E[Y \mid \mathcal G]\). Taking \(Y = 1\), a \(\mathcal G\)-measurable \(X\) satisfies \(\E[X \mid \mathcal G] = X\). (d) Independence: if \(Y\) is independent of \(\mathcal G\) and \(f\) is a function with \(f(Y)\) integrable, then \(\E[f(Y) \mid \mathcal G] = \E[f(Y)]\). The page does not prove these properties. Each result that uses them says "Assuming Assumption 4.2".

  56: Definition 1.2 (continuous-time martingale)

  58: Let \((\mathcal F_t)\) be a filtration. A process \((Z_t)_{t \ge 0}\) is a [DFN id=def-continuous-time-martingale concept=continuous-time-martingale]continuous-time martingale with respect to \((\mathcal F_t)\) when (i) \(Z_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable for each \(t\), (ii) \(\E[Z_t^2] < \infty\) for each \(t\), and (iii) \(\E[Z_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = Z_s\) for all \(0 \le s \le t\). Condition (iii) is the discrete condition of M6 with the index extended to \([0,\infty)\). Condition (ii) makes the conditional expectation the L2 conditional expectation of M6.

  60: 2Brownian martingales

  62: Lemma 2.1 (moment-generating function of a normal)

  64: Let \(Y\) be normal with mean zero and variance \(h > 0\). Then for every real \(\theta\), \(\E[e^{\theta Y}] = e^{\theta^2 h/2}\).

  66: Proof

  68: The density of \(Y\) is \(p(y) = (2\pi h)^{-1/2} e^{-y^2/(2h)}\). Completing the square,

  70: \[\theta y - \frac{y^2}{2h} = \frac{\theta^2 h}{2} - \frac{(y - \theta h)^2}{2h}.\]

  72: Hence \(\E[e^{\theta Y}] = e^{\theta^2 h/2} \int (2\pi h)^{-1/2} e^{-(y - \theta h)^2/(2h)}\,dy\). The integrand is the density of the normal law with mean \(\theta h\) and variance \(h\), which integrates to 1. So \(\E[e^{\theta Y}] = e^{\theta^2 h/2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

  74: Proposition 2.2 (Brownian motion is a martingale)

  76: (H1) \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion, and \((\mathcal F_t)\) satisfies Assumption 4.1.

  78: (H2) Assumption 4.2 holds.

  80: Conclusion: assuming Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, \((B_t)\) is a continuous-time martingale with respect to \((\mathcal F_t)\).

  82: Proof

  84: Condition (i) is (H1). For (ii), \(\E[B_t^2] = t\) by (D2) of Lesson 1, which is finite. For (iii), let \(0 \le s \le t\) and put \(Y = B_t - B_s\). Then \(B_t = B_s + Y\), and by Assumption 4.2(a) and (c), with \(B_s\) being \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable,

  86: \[\E[B_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = B_s + \E[Y \mid \mathcal F_s].\]

  88: By Assumption 4.1, \(Y\) is independent of \(\mathcal F_s\), so Assumption 4.2(d) gives \(\E[Y \mid \mathcal F_s] = \E[Y] = 0\), by (D2). Therefore \(\E[B_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = B_s\). \(\blacksquare\)

  90: Proposition 2.3 (\(B_t^2 - t\) is a martingale)

  92: (H1) \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion, and \((\mathcal F_t)\) satisfies Assumption 4.1.

  94: (H2) Assumption 4.2 holds.

  96: Conclusion: assuming Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, \((B_t^2 - t)\) is a continuous-time martingale with respect to \((\mathcal F_t)\).

  98: Proof

 100: Condition (i) holds because \(B_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable. For (ii), \(B_t\) is normal with variance \(t\), so \(\E[B_t^2] = t\) and \(\E[B_t^4] = 3t^2\) by Lemma 2.2 of Lesson 3. Expanding the square, \(\E[(B_t^2 - t)^2] = \E[B_t^4] - 2t\,\E[B_t^2] + t^2 = 3t^2 - 2t^2 + t^2 = 2t^2\), which is finite. For (iii), let \(0 \le s \le t\), \(Y = B_t - B_s\) and \(h = t - s\). Then \(B_t^2 = B_s^2 + 2B_s Y + Y^2\). By Assumption 4.2(a) and (c), and since \(B_s\) is \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable,

 102: \[\E[B_t^2 \mid \mathcal F_s] = B_s^2 + 2B_s\,\E[Y \mid \mathcal F_s] + \E[Y^2 \mid \mathcal F_s].\]

 104: By Assumption 4.1 and Assumption 4.2(d), \(\E[Y \mid \mathcal F_s] = \E[Y] = 0\) and \(\E[Y^2 \mid \mathcal F_s] = \E[Y^2] = h\), using (D2) for the variance of \(Y\). Hence \(\E[B_t^2 \mid \mathcal F_s] = B_s^2 + h\), and

 106: \[\E[B_t^2 - t \mid \mathcal F_s] = B_s^2 + (t - s) - t = B_s^2 - s.\]

 108: This is the required value for \(B_s^2 - s\). \(\blacksquare\)

 110: Remark (the link with Lesson 3). Lesson 3 showed that the sum of squared increments over a partition has mean \(t\), since \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = \Delta_k\) and the \(\Delta_k\) add to \(t\). Proposition 2.3 is the conditional version for one interval: \(\E[(B_t - B_s)^2 \mid \mathcal F_s] = t - s\), which is the statement that the squared increment has conditional mean equal to its length. The proof of (iii) uses only the mean and the variance of the increment, together with Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, so it does not use the mean-square limit of Lesson 3.

 112: Proposition 2.4 (the exponential martingale)

 114: (H1) \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion, \((\mathcal F_t)\) satisfies Assumption 4.1, and \(\theta\) is a real number.

 116: (H2) Assumption 4.2 holds.

 118: Conclusion: assuming Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, \(Z_t = \exp(\theta B_t - \theta^2 t/2)\) is a continuous-time martingale with respect to \((\mathcal F_t)\), with \(Z_0 = 1\) and \(\E[Z_t^2] = e^{\theta^2 t}\).

 120: Proof

 122: Second moment. For \(t > 0\), Lemma 2.1 with \(Y = B_t\), \(h = t\), and \(2\theta\) in place of \(\theta\) gives \(\E[e^{2\theta B_t}] = e^{2\theta^2 t}\). So \(\E[Z_t^2] = e^{-\theta^2 t}\,\E[e^{2\theta B_t}] = e^{\theta^2 t}\), which is finite. At \(t = 0\) both \(\E[Z_0^2]\) and \(e^{0}\) equal 1, because \(Z_0 = 1\). Condition (ii) holds, and condition (i) holds because \(Z_t\) is a function of \(B_t\).

 124: Martingale identity. Let \(0 \le s < t\), \(Y = B_t - B_s\) and \(h = t - s > 0\). The case \(s = t\) is trivial: \(\E[Z_t \mid \mathcal F_t] = Z_t\) by the last sentence of Assumption 4.2, because \(Z_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable. Then \(\theta B_t - \theta^2 t/2 = (\theta B_s - \theta^2 s/2) + (\theta Y - \theta^2 h/2)\), so

 126: \[Z_t = Z_s \cdot W, \qquad W = \exp(\theta Y - \theta^2 h/2).\]

 128: The variable \(W\) is square-integrable, since \(\E[W^2] = e^{-\theta^2 h}\,\E[e^{2\theta Y}] = e^{\theta^2 h}\) by Lemma 2.1. The variable \(Z_s\) is square-integrable and \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable. By Assumption 4.2(c), \(\E[Z_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = Z_s\,\E[W \mid \mathcal F_s]\). By Assumption 4.1 and 4.2(d), \(\E[W \mid \mathcal F_s] = \E[W] = e^{-\theta^2 h/2}\,\E[e^{\theta Y}] = e^{-\theta^2 h/2} e^{\theta^2 h/2} = 1\), using Lemma 2.1 with the variance \(h\) of \(Y\). Hence \(\E[Z_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = Z_s\). \(\blacksquare\)

 132: Lab 1 · Quadrature check of the exponential martingale

 134: Predicted before running. The lab is numerical, with no simulation. For \(t = 2\) and \(\theta \in \{-1, 0.5, 2\}\), the integral of \(e^{\theta\sqrt{t}\,z}\) against the standard normal density should match \(e^{\theta^2 t/2}\) to a relative 1e-9, which is Lemma 2.1 with \(h = t\). The integral of \(Z_t\) against the same density should equal \(Z_0 = 1\) to an absolute 1e-9, which is Proposition 2.4 at \(s = 0\), together with the tower property of Assumption 4.2(b).

 136: """SC1 lab L04: the exponential martingale of Brownian motion, checked by numerical quadrature.

 138: Numerical, not simulated. Nothing is random and nothing is read from a file, so every run prints the same lines.
 139: B_t is normal with mean zero and variance t, so B_t has the law of sqrt(t) times a standard normal variable. Hence
 140: E[exp(theta * B_t)] is the integral of
 141: exp(theta * sqrt(t) * z) against the standard normal density phi(z), over the real line. The lab evaluates each
 142: integral by composite Simpson's rule with PANELS panels on [-LIMIT, LIMIT].

 144: For each theta the lab prints two checks. First, the quadrature of E[exp(theta * B_t)] against the closed form
 145: exp(theta**2 * t / 2). Second, the quadrature of E[Z_t] for Z_t = exp(theta * B_t - theta**2 * t / 2) against Z_0 = 1.
 146: Agreement with the closed form to the stated tolerance is consistent with the truncation at LIMIT being negligible
 147: for these integrands; the lab does not compute the mass beyond LIMIT.

 149: These are finite computations for three values of theta and one value of t. They are not a proof for all theta and t.
 150: """

 152: from __future__ import annotations

 154: import math
 155: from collections.abc import Callable

 157: THETAS = (-1.0, 0.5, 2.0)
 158: TIME = 2.0
 159: LIMIT = 12.0
 160: PANELS = 20000
 161: TOL = 1e-9

 163: def standard_normal_density(z: float) -> float:
 164: """The standard normal density phi(z) = exp(-z**2 / 2) / sqrt(2 pi)."""
 165: return math.exp(-0.5 * z * z) / math.sqrt(2.0 * math.pi)

 167: def simpson(f: Callable[[float], float], lower: float, upper: float, panels: int) -> float:
 168: """Composite Simpson's rule on [lower, upper] with `panels` panels; `panels` must be even."""
 169: width = (upper - lower) / panels
 170: total = f(lower) + f(upper)
 171: for i in range(1, panels):
 172: weight = 4.0 if i % 2 else 2.0
 173: total += weight * f(lower + i * width)
 174: return total * width / 3.0

 176: def exp_quadrature(theta: float, t: float, shift: float) -> float:
 177: """The integral of exp(theta * sqrt(t) * z - shift) * phi(z) over [-LIMIT, LIMIT], with shift a constant."""
 178: scale = theta * math.sqrt(t)

 180: def integrand(z: float) -> float:
 181: return math.exp(scale * z - shift) * standard_normal_density(z)

 183: return simpson(integrand, -LIMIT, LIMIT, PANELS)

 185: def main() -> None:
 186: print(f"B_t ~ N(0, t) with t = {TIME:g}; each integral over [-{LIMIT:g}, {LIMIT:g}] with {PANELS} Simpson panels")
 187: for theta in THETAS:
 188: closed = math.exp(theta * theta * TIME / 2.0)
 189: mgf = exp_quadrature(theta, TIME, 0.0)
 190: martingale = exp_quadrature(theta, TIME, theta * theta * TIME / 2.0)
 191: mgf_ok = abs(mgf - closed) <= TOL * closed
 192: martingale_ok = abs(martingale - 1.0) <= TOL
 193: print(
 194: f"theta = {theta:+.1f}: E[exp(theta B_t)] = {mgf:.9f}, exp(theta^2 t / 2) = {closed:.9f}, "
 195: f"agree within 1e-9 relative: {mgf_ok}"
 196: )
 197: print(f" E[Z_t] = {martingale:.9f}, equal to Z_0 = 1 within 1e-9: {martingale_ok}")

 199: main()

 201: B_t ~ N(0, t) with t = 2; each integral over [-12, 12] with 20000 Simpson panels
 202: theta = -1.0: E[exp(theta B_t)] = 2.718281828, exp(theta^2 t / 2) = 2.718281828, agree within 1e-9 relative: True
 203: E[Z_t] = 1.000000000, equal to Z_0 = 1 within 1e-9: True
 204: theta = +0.5: E[exp(theta B_t)] = 1.284025417, exp(theta^2 t / 2) = 1.284025417, agree within 1e-9 relative: True
 205: E[Z_t] = 1.000000000, equal to Z_0 = 1 within 1e-9: True
 206: theta = +2.0: E[exp(theta B_t)] = 54.598150033, exp(theta^2 t / 2) = 54.598150033, agree within 1e-9 relative: True
 207: E[Z_t] = 1.000000000, equal to Z_0 = 1 within 1e-9: True

 209: The printed values agree with the closed forms: \(\E[e^{\theta B_t}]\) prints 2.718281828, 1.284025417 and 54.598150033 for the three values of \(\theta\), which are \(e^{1}\), \(e^{1/4}\) and \(e^{4}\). Each \(\E[Z_t]\) prints 1.000000000. The check is numerical and uses one time, \(t = 2\), and three values of \(\theta\). It shows that the quadrature and the closed forms agree there. It does not prove the martingale property for other \(\theta\) or \(t\), which Proposition 2.4 does, assuming Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2.

 211: [SECTION class=canon-breaks id=canon-breaks]

 213: 3Where the canon breaks

 215: A drift breaks the martingale property. Let \(X_t = B_t + t\). Assuming Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, \(\E[X_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = B_s + t\), by Proposition 2.2 and Assumption 4.2(a), where the constant \(t\) satisfies \(\E[t \mid \mathcal F_s] = t\) by the last sentence of Assumption 4.2. This equals \(X_s = B_s + s\) only when \(t = s\). So \(X\) is not a martingale, although \(X_0 = 0\) and \(X\) is adapted (each \(X_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable) and square-integrable.

 217: Assumption 4.1 carries the three proofs. Each of Propositions 2.2 to 2.4 uses it to replace a function of the future increment \(Y = B_t - B_s\) (in Proposition 2.4, of the variable \(W\) defined in its proof) by its unconditional expectation. Without it, \(\E[Y \mid \mathcal F_s]\) need not equal \(\E[Y]\), and the identities need not hold. This page does not construct a process that violates Assumption 4.1 and then tests the martingale property.

 219: Optional stopping is not used. The martingales of M6 are defined on a discrete index set, Lesson 8 of M6 (filtration and martingale), and the optional-stopping theorem is not restated here. This lesson makes no claim about \(\E[B_\tau]\) or \(\E[Z_\tau]\) for a random time \(\tau\). The page proves the martingale property at fixed times only.

 221: [SECTION class=misconceptions id=misconceptions]

 223: 4Misconceptions

 225: “A process with mean zero at every time is a martingale.”

 227: Take \(X_t = B_t^3\). Its mean is zero at every time, because the law of \(B_t\) is symmetric, so \(\E[B_t^3] = 0\). But expanding \((B_s + Y)^3\) with \(Y = B_t - B_s\) gives \(B_s^3 + 3B_s^2 Y + 3B_s Y^2 + Y^3\). Taking conditional expectations, with \(\E[Y] = \E[Y^3] = 0\) (symmetry of the normal law) and \(\E[Y^2] = t - s\), gives \(\E[X_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = B_s^3 + 3(t - s)B_s\). This step assumes Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2(a), (c), (d): they give \(\E[Y^k \mid \mathcal F_s] = \E[Y^k]\) for \(k = 1, 2, 3\), and they allow \(B_s\) to be pulled out. For \(s < t\), this differs from \(X_s = B_s^3\) whenever \(B_s \ne 0\), so \(X\) is not a martingale. The martingale property is a condition on conditional means, not on the unconditional mean.

 229: “Since \(B\) is a martingale, \(\exp(\theta B_t)\) is a martingale.”

 231: A martingale has constant expectation, \(\E[Z_t] = \E[Z_0]\), by the tower property of Assumption 4.2(b) applied at \(s = 0\). Lemma 2.1 gives \(\E[e^{\theta B_t}] = e^{\theta^2 t/2}\), which changes with \(t\) when \(\theta \ne 0\). This expectation equals 1 at \(t = 0\), so for \(\theta \ne 0\) it is not constant in \(t\), and, assuming Assumption 4.2, \(\exp(\theta B_t)\) is not a martingale. The correction \(-\theta^2 t/2\) in Proposition 2.4 is what restores the martingale property.

 233: “The quadrature in Lab 1 proves that the exponential process is a martingale.”

 235: Lab 1 checks three values of \(\theta\) at one time, \(t = 2\). A finite check at these values is not a proof for all \(\theta\) and \(t\). Proposition 2.4 is the proof, and its hypotheses are Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2.

 253: M2 · The exponential martingale at \(\theta = 1\), \(t = 2\)

 255: Let \(Z_t = \exp(B_t - t/2)\). Compute \(\E[e^{B_2}]\) and \(\E[Z_2]\).

 257: Solution

 259: By Lemma 2.1 with \(h = 2\) and \(\theta = 1\), \(\E[e^{B_2}] = e^{1 \cdot 2/2} = e\). Also \(Z_2 = e^{B_2} e^{-1}\), so \(\E[Z_2] = e^{-1} \cdot e = 1\), which equals \(Z_0 = 1\). Problem P2 gives this constant expectation for the martingale of Proposition 2.4, assuming Assumption 4.2.

 261: M3 · The variance of the exponential martingale

 263: For \(Z_t = \exp(\theta B_t - \theta^2 t/2)\), compute \(\E[Z_t]\), \(\E[Z_t^2]\) and \(\Var(Z_t)\).

 265: Solution

 267: Assuming Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, Proposition 2.4 and Problem P2 give \(\E[Z_t] = 1\), and the second-moment computation in the proof of Proposition 2.4 gives \(\E[Z_t^2] = e^{\theta^2 t}\). So \(\Var(Z_t) = e^{\theta^2 t} - 1\). For \(\theta = 0\) this gives \(0\), as it must, because \(Z_t = 1\) when \(\theta = 0\).

 271: P1 · The drift counterexample

 273: Assuming Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2, prove that \(X_t = B_t + t\) is not a martingale with respect to \((\mathcal F_t)\).

 275: Solution

 277: \(X_t\) is \(\mathcal F_t\)-measurable and square-integrable. For \(s \le t\), Proposition 2.2 gives \(\E[B_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = B_s\), and the constant \(t\) satisfies \(\E[t \mid \mathcal F_s] = t\). So \(\E[X_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = B_s + t\), while \(X_s = B_s + s\). These are equal only when \(t = s\). For \(s < t\) the martingale condition fails, so \(X\) is not a martingale.

 279: P2 · A martingale has constant expectation

 281: Let \((Z_t)\) be a martingale with respect to \((\mathcal F_t)\). Prove that \(\E[Z_t] = \E[Z_0]\) for every \(t \ge 0\), assuming Assumption 4.2.

 283: Solution

 285: Apply condition (iii) at \(s = 0\): \(\E[Z_t \mid \mathcal F_0] = Z_0\). By the tower property of Assumption 4.2(b), \(\E[Z_t] = \E[\E[Z_t \mid \mathcal F_0]] = \E[Z_0]\).

 287: P3 · The product moment of a martingale at two times

 289: Let \((Z_t)\) be a martingale with respect to \((\mathcal F_t)\). Prove that \(\E[Z_s Z_t] = \E[Z_s^2]\) for \(0 \le s \le t\), assuming Assumption 4.2.

 291: Solution

 293: \(Z_s\) is \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable and \(Z_s Z_t\) is integrable, since both are square-integrable. By Assumption 4.2(c), \(\E[Z_s Z_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = Z_s\,\E[Z_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = Z_s \cdot Z_s\). Taking expectations with the tower property, \(\E[Z_s Z_t] = \E[Z_s^2]\).

 305: E2 · A second moment, computed exactly

 323: R1 · Which deterministic shifts give a martingale

 325: Assume Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2. Let \(g\) be a deterministic function. Prove that \(B_t^2 - g(t)\) is a martingale if and only if \(g(t) = t + c\) for a constant \(c\).

 327: Solution

 329: Write \(B_t^2 - g(t) = (B_t^2 - t) + k(t)\), where \(k(t) = t - g(t)\) is deterministic. By Proposition 2.3 the first term is a martingale. By linearity (Assumption 4.2(a)), a sum of two martingales is a martingale, and the difference of \(B_t^2 - g(t)\) and \(B_t^2 - t\) is \(k\). So \(B_t^2 - g(t)\) is a martingale exactly when \(k\) is one. For a deterministic \(k\), Assumption 4.2(c) gives \(\E[k(t) \mid \mathcal F_s] = k(t)\), and condition (iii) requires this to equal \(k(s)\). So \(k(t) = k(s)\) for all \(s \le t\), which means \(k\) is constant, say \(k = -c\). Then \(g(t) = t + c\). The converse holds, because adding a constant to a martingale leaves it a martingale.

 331: R2 · When the exponential \(\exp(cB_t)\) is a martingale

 333: Assume Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2. For a real constant \(c\), let \(Z_t = \exp(c B_t)\). Prove that \((Z_t)\) is a martingale for every \(t \ge 0\) only when \(c = 0\).

 335: Solution

 337: Let \(0 \le s < t\), \(Y = B_t - B_s\) and \(h = t - s\). As in Proposition 2.4, \(Z_t = Z_s \cdot \exp(cY)\), with \(Z_s\) \(\mathcal F_s\)-measurable and positive. Then \(\E[Z_t \mid \mathcal F_s] = Z_s\,\E[e^{cY}] = Z_s e^{c^2 h/2}\), by Assumption 4.2(c) and (d) and Lemma 2.1. For this to equal \(Z_s\) we need \(e^{c^2 h/2} = 1\), so \(c^2 h = 0\). Since \(h > 0\), \(c = 0\). Conversely, \(Z\) is the constant process 1 when \(c = 0\), which is a martingale.

 339: R3 · Conditional variance of the increment

 341: Assume Assumptions 4.1 and 4.2. For \(0 \le s \le t\), compute \(\E[(B_t - B_s)^2 \mid \mathcal F_s]\) and \(\Var(B_t - B_s \mid \mathcal F_s)\) defined as \(\E[(B_t - B_s)^2 \mid \mathcal F_s] - (\E[B_t - B_s \mid \mathcal F_s])^2\).

 343: Solution

 345: By Assumption 4.1 and Assumption 4.2(d), \(\E[(B_t - B_s)^2 \mid \mathcal F_s] = \E[(B_t - B_s)^2] = t - s\), by (D2). Also \(\E[B_t - B_s \mid \mathcal F_s] = 0\). So the conditional variance is \(t - s\), which is a constant, not a random variable, because the increment is independent of the past.
