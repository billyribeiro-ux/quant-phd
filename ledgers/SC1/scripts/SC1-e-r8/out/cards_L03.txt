SC1-c046 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Proof of Theorem 2.3 (mean), Section 2, assuming Assumption 3.2

SC1-c047 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Proof of Theorem 2.3 (variance and error bound), Section 2, assuming Assumption 3.2
  F: Theorem 2.3, error bound. Hypotheses: (H1) B is a standard Brownian motion and 𝒫 is a partition of [0,t]; (H2) Assumption 3.2. What are E[(Q(𝒫)-t)^2] and its upper bound?
  B: E[(Q(𝒫)-t)^2]=2Σ_kΔ_k^2≤ 2 t |𝒫|. The variance adds over independent squared increments, each with variance 2Δ_k^2.
SC1-c048 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Proof of Theorem 2.3, Section 2, assuming Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2
  F: Theorem 2.3, limit. Hypotheses: (H1) B is a standard Brownian motion and 𝒫 is a partition of [0,t]; (H2) Assumption 3.2. Assuming also Assumption 3.1, for a sequence (𝒫_n) of partitions of [0,t], what is the limit of Q(𝒫_n)?
  B: Q(𝒫_n)→ t in mean square. The mean-square error is at most 2t|𝒫_n|, which tends to zero.
SC1-c049 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Proof of Proposition 2.4(a), Section 2

SC1-c050 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Proof of Proposition 2.4(b), Section 2 (induction on N)
  F: Proposition 2.4(b). Hypothesis: f(s)=s^2, and 𝒫 splits [0,H] into N equal steps. What is the sum of squared increments exactly?
  B: H^4(4N^2-1)/(3N^3). The sum of (2j+1)^2 for j=0,…,N-1 is N(4N^2-1)/3, by induction on N.

SC1-c053 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Section 3 (Where the canon breaks), proof of Theorem 2.3 with the fourth-moment bound, assuming Assumption 3.2
  F: Canon break: does the mean-square limit need Gaussian increments? Assume the increments are independent with mean 0, variance Δ_k, and E[(Δ B_k)^4]≤ c Δ_k^2, with Assumption 3.2.
  B: No. Var((ΔB_k)^2) ≤ E[(ΔB_k)^4] ≤ cΔ_k^2, so the proof of Theorem 2.3 gives E[(Q-t)^2]≤ c t |𝒫|. The bound's constant is c, not 2. For Gaussian increments E[(ΔB_k)^4]=3Δ_k^2 by Lemma 2.2, so c=3 is admissible there, and the sharper constant 2 comes from Var((ΔB_k)^2)=2Δ_k^2 (Lemma 2.2). A fourth-moment bound is enough for the proof.
SC1-c054 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Section 3 (Where the canon breaks), direct computation with Lemma 2.2
  F: Canon break: independence is not optional. If all N increments of the equal partition of [0,t] equal one variable X_N∼N(0,t/N), what is Var(Q)?
  B: Var(Q)=2t^2 for every N, although |𝒫|→ 0. Since E[Q]=t, E[(Q-t)^2]=Var(Q)=2t^2 for every N, which does not tend to 0. So Theorem 2.3 does not apply: (H1) fails, because the increments are not independent as (D3) requires, and (H2) does not apply, because Assumption 3.2 is stated for independent variables and these squares are not independent.
SC1-c056 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Proposition 2.4(a) and Theorem 2.3, Section 4
  F: Misconception: a smooth function such as f(s)=s^2 has the same quadratic variation as Brownian motion. What happens to its squared-increment sums?
  B: They vanish as the mesh shrinks: Σ_k(f(t_(k+1))-f(t_k))^2≤ 4H^3|𝒫| (Proposition 2.4(a)). For Brownian motion, assuming Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2, the sums converge in mean square to the elapsed time t (Theorem 2.3).
SC1-c057 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Section 4 misconception, with Problem P3
  F: Misconception: the total absolute variation of Brownian motion converges as the partition is refined. What is EΣ_k|Δ B_k| over N equal steps of [0,t]?
  B: √(2Nt/π), which grows without bound in N. It uses E|Z|=√(2/π) for a standard normal Z (Problem P3).
SC1-c058 [L03] grade=T cite=[] basis=Theorem 2.3 (mean-square statement), Section 4
  F: Assuming Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2, misconception: the quadratic variation of a Brownian path is a random number that changes from path to path. What is established about this?
  B: Nothing about a single path is established. Theorem 2.3 is a mean-square statement: E[(Q(𝒫_n)-t)^2] ≤ 2t|𝒫_n|, which tends to 0. It says nothing about Q(𝒫_n) on an individual path, and no pathwise claim is made.

SC1-c060 [L03] grade=- cite=[] basis=-
  F: Assumption 3.2 (independence and additivity of variance). What does it state, and is it proved?
  B: (a) Measurable functions of independent variables are independent, in particular their squares. (b) For independent variables with finite second moments, the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances. They are assumed, not proved.
