  36: The sums of squared increments of Brownian motion do not vanish under refinement, unlike those of a smooth function. Each squared increment of \(B\) has mean equal to the length of its interval, so the sum of squared increments over a partition (defined in §1) has mean equal to the elapsed time, however fine the partition. For the smooth function \(s \mapsto s^2\) the same sums shrink to zero. This lesson proves both statements with explicit constants. The limit holds in mean square, that is, the expected squared error tends to zero under Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2 (stated below, in §1). Lab 1 simulates Brownian increments on partitions into \(2^k\) equal steps and prints the sums with their standard errors.

  38: 1Quadratic variation along partitions

  40: Let \(0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_N = t\) be a partition \(\mathcal P\) of \([0,t]\). Its [DFN id=None concept=None]mesh is \(|\mathcal P| = \max_k (t_{k+1} - t_k)\), the length of its longest interval. Write \(\Delta_k = t_{k+1} - t_k\) for the length of the \(k\)-th interval and \(\Delta B_k = B_{t_{k+1}} - B_{t_k}\) for the increment of \(B\) over it. The partition is finite and fixed. By Lesson 1, \(B_0 = 0\), each \(\Delta B_k\) is normal with mean zero and variance \(\Delta_k\), and increments over disjoint intervals are independent. A partition of \([0,t]\) into \(N=2^k\) equal steps, for an integer \(k\ge 0\), is a dyadic partition of depth \(k\).

  42: Definition 1.1 (quadratic variation along a partition)

  44: For a partition \(\mathcal P\) of \([0,t]\), the sum \(Q(\mathcal P) = \sum_k (\Delta B_k)^2\) of squared increments is the [DFN id=def-quadratic-variation concept=quadratic-variation]quadratic variation of \(B\) along \(\mathcal P\). The quadratic variation of \(B\) on \([0,t]\) is the limit of \(Q(\mathcal P_n)\) along a sequence of partitions with mesh tending to zero, in mean square. Theorem 2.3 shows that this limit exists and equals \(t\), under Assumptions 3.1 and 3.2 (stated below).

  46: The sum depends on the partition, so a limit needs a condition on how the partitions are refined. The condition is the mesh condition below.

  48: Assumption 3.1 (mesh tends to zero)

  50: \((\mathcal P_n)\) is a sequence of partitions of \([0,t]\) with \(|\mathcal P_n| \to 0\).

  52: Assumption 3.2 (independence and additivity of variance)

  54: (a) If \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) are independent, then so are \(g_1(X_1), \dots, g_N(X_N)\) for measurable functions \(g_k\), in particular \(X_1^2, \dots, X_N^2\). (b) If \(U_1, \dots, U_N\) are independent with finite second moments, then \(\Var(U_1 + \dots + U_N) = \sum_k \Var(U_k)\). The page does not prove these facts. Each result that uses them says "Assuming Assumption 3.2".

  56: 2The mean-square limit

  58: Lemma 2.1 (moments of the standard normal)

  60: Let \(Z\) have the standard normal law, with density \(\varphi(z) = e^{-z^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\). Then \(\E[Z^2] = 1\) and \(\E[Z^4] = 3\).

  62: Proof

  64: The standard normal has variance 1, so \(\E[Z^2] = 1\). Differentiating gives \(\varphi'(z) = -z\,\varphi(z)\), so \(z\,\varphi(z) = -\varphi'(z)\). For \(R > 0\), integrate by parts on \([-R, R]\):

  66: \[\int_{-R}^{R} z^4 \varphi(z)\,dz = \int_{-R}^{R} z^3 \bigl(z\varphi(z)\bigr)\,dz = -\int_{-R}^{R} z^3 \varphi'(z)\,dz = -\bigl[z^3\varphi(z)\bigr]_{-R}^{R} + \int_{-R}^{R} 3z^2 \varphi(z)\,dz.\]

  68: The boundary term \(-\bigl[z^3\varphi(z)\bigr]_{-R}^{R} = -2R^3\varphi(R)\) tends to zero as \(R \to \infty\), because \(e^{-R^2/2}\) decays faster than any power of \(R\) grows. The integrals converge absolutely, so letting \(R \to \infty\) gives \(\E[Z^4] = 3\,\E[Z^2] = 3\). \(\blacksquare\)

  70: Lemma 2.2 (moments of one increment)

  72: Let \(X\) be normal with mean zero and variance \(\Delta > 0\). Then \(\E[X^2] = \Delta\), \(\E[X^4] = 3\Delta^2\), and \(\Var(X^2) = 2\Delta^2\).

  74: Proof

  76: Write \(X = \sqrt{\Delta}\,Z\) with \(Z\) standard normal. Then \(X^2 = \Delta Z^2\), so \(\E[X^2] = \Delta\,\E[Z^2] = \Delta\). Also \(X^4 = \Delta^2 Z^4\), so \(\E[X^4] = 3\Delta^2\) by Lemma 2.1. Hence \(\Var(X^2) = \E[X^4] - (\E[X^2])^2 = 3\Delta^2 - \Delta^2 = 2\Delta^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

  78: Theorem 2.3 (mean-square limit of the sums of squared increments)

  80: (H1) \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion (Lesson 1, Definition 1.1), and \(\mathcal P\) is a partition of \([0,t]\) with lengths \(\Delta_k\) and increments \(\Delta B_k\), as in §1.

  82: (H2) Assuming Assumption 3.2.

  84: Conclusion: \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)] = t\), and \(\E\bigl[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2\bigr] = 2\sum_k \Delta_k^2 \le 2\,t\,|\mathcal P|\). Assuming also Assumption 3.1, for every sequence \((\mathcal P_n)\) of partitions of \([0,t]\) the right-hand side tends to zero, so \(Q(\mathcal P_n) \to t\) in mean square.

  86: Proof

  88: Mean. By Lemma 2.2 with \(\Delta = \Delta_k\), \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = \Delta_k\). Summing and telescoping, \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)] = \sum_k \Delta_k = \sum_k (t_{k+1} - t_k) = t_N - t_0 = t\).

  90: Independence and variance. The increments \(X_k = \Delta B_k\) are independent, by Lesson 1. By Assumption 3.2(a), the squares \(U_k = X_k^2\) are independent. Each has finite second moment, since \(\E[U_k^2] = 3\Delta_k^2\) by Lemma 2.2. By Assumption 3.2(b) and Lemma 2.2,

  92: \[\Var(Q(\mathcal P)) = \sum_k \Var(U_k) = \sum_k 2\Delta_k^2.\]

  94: Error bound. Since \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)] = t\), \(\E[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2] = \Var(Q(\mathcal P)) = 2\sum_k \Delta_k^2\). Also \(\sum_k \Delta_k^2 \le \bigl(\max_k \Delta_k\bigr)\sum_k \Delta_k = |\mathcal P|\,t\). So \(\E[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2] \le 2t|\mathcal P|\). Under Assumption 3.1, \(|\mathcal P_n| \to 0\), so the bound tends to zero. \(\blacksquare\)

  96: Theorem 2.3 is a mean-square statement. It says nothing about the limit of \(Q(\mathcal P_n)\) for a single path, and this page does not claim an almost-sure limit. The limit \(t\) is a constant: the random sums converge to a deterministic value in mean square, and, under Assumption 3.2, the mean-square error is exactly \(2\sum_k \Delta_k^2\).

  98: Proposition 2.4 (the same sums for \(s \mapsto s^2\))

 100: (H1) \(f(s) = s^2\), and \(\mathcal P\) is a partition of \([0,H]\), with \(H\) the endpoint. The endpoint is written \(H\) here and in Lab 1, because the program's notation reserves \(T\) for the number of return observations.

 102: Conclusion: (a) \(\sum_k \bigl(f(t_{k+1}) - f(t_k)\bigr)^2 \le 4H^3\,|\mathcal P|\). (b) If \(\mathcal P\) splits \([0,H]\) into \(N\) equal steps, the sum equals \(H^4(4N^2 - 1)/(3N^3)\) exactly.

 104: Proof

 106: (a) For \(0 \le a < b \le H\), \(b^2 - a^2 = (b - a)(b + a)\) with \(0 \le b + a \le 2H\). So \((b^2 - a^2)^2 \le 4H^2 (b - a)^2\). Summing over the intervals and using \(\sum_k \Delta_k^2 \le |\mathcal P|\,H\) from the proof of Theorem 2.3 gives \(\sum_k (f(t_{k+1}) - f(t_k))^2 \le 4H^2 \cdot |\mathcal P|\,H = 4H^3|\mathcal P|\).

 108: (b) For the equal partition, \(t_j = Hj/N\), so \(t_{j+1}^2 - t_j^2 = H^2(2j+1)/N^2\). The sum of squares is \(H^4 S(N)/N^4\), where \(S(N) = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1} (2j+1)^2\). The claim \(S(N) = N(4N^2 - 1)/3\) holds for \(N = 1\), since \(S(1) = 1 = 3/3\). If it holds for \(N\), then \(S(N+1) = N(4N^2 - 1)/3 + (2N+1)^2 = (2N+1)(N+1)(2N+3)/3\), which equals \((N+1)\bigl(4(N+1)^2 - 1\bigr)/3\). By induction it holds for every \(N \ge 1\). Hence the sum is \(H^4 N(4N^2 - 1)/(3N^4) = H^4(4N^2 - 1)/(3N^3)\). \(\blacksquare\)

 233: [SECTION class=canon-breaks id=canon-breaks]

 235: 3Where the canon breaks

 237: The mean-square limit holds under a fourth-moment bound, without the Gaussian law. Let the increments be independent with mean zero, variance \(\Delta_k\), and \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^4] \le c\,\Delta_k^2\) for a constant \(c\). Then \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = \Delta_k\) and \(\Var((\Delta B_k)^2) \le c\,\Delta_k^2\), so the proof of Theorem 2.3 gives \(\E[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2] \le c\,t\,|\mathcal P|\), assuming Assumption 3.2. The constant changes from 2 to \(c\). The proof uses the second and fourth moments of each increment, not the Gaussian law.

 239: Independence is not optional. Take, for each \(N\), the \(N\) increments of the equal partition of \([0,t]\) all equal to one variable \(X_N\) with law \(\Normal(0,t/N)\). Then \(Q = N X_N^2\), so \(\E[Q] = t\) and \(\Var(Q) = N^2 \cdot 2(t/N)^2 = 2t^2\) for every \(N\). The mesh tends to zero, but the variance does not, so the mean-square conclusion of Theorem 2.3 fails for this family. The family is not a standard Brownian motion, so the theorem does not apply to it, and the example shows that the independence in Assumption 3.2 cannot be dropped.

 241: The page does not state an almost-sure limit. Lab 1 is a finite sample on finitely many partitions. It can show that the sums are consistent with \(t\) at these sizes. It cannot establish a pathwise limit, and no sentence on this page claims one.

 243: [SECTION class=misconceptions id=misconceptions]

 245: 4Misconceptions

 247: “A smooth function has the same quadratic variation as Brownian motion.”

 249: For \(f(s) = s^2\), Proposition 2.4(a) gives \(\sum_k (f(t_{k+1}) - f(t_k))^2 \le 4H^3|\mathcal P|\), which tends to zero as the mesh does. For \(H = 2\) and \(N = 128\), Lab 1 prints the exact sum 0.166664, while the Brownian sum over the same partition has mean \(H = 2\) (Theorem 2.3). For \(s \mapsto s^2\) the sums vanish in the limit, and for Brownian motion their mean is the elapsed time for every partition, with mean-square error tending to zero (Theorem 2.3).

 251: “The total absolute variation of Brownian motion converges as the partition is refined, as the length of a smooth curve does.”

 253: For \(N\) equal steps of \([0,t]\), the increments are normal with variance \(t/N\). Using \(\E|Z| = 2\varphi(0) = \sqrt{2/\pi}\), which follows from \(z\varphi(z) = -\varphi'(z)\) as in Problem P3, the expected sum of absolute increments is \(N\sqrt{2/\pi}\sqrt{t/N} = \sqrt{2Nt/\pi}\). This grows without bound in \(N\). The absolute sum therefore has no finite limit in expectation, whereas Theorem 2.3 controls the squared sum.

 255: “The quadratic variation of a Brownian path is a random number that changes from path to path.”

 257: Theorem 2.3 gives the mean-square limit \(t\), which is a constant. The random sums \(Q(\mathcal P_n)\) fluctuate around \(t\) with mean-square error at most \(2t|\mathcal P_n|\), and that bound tends to zero. The randomness of the sums vanishes in mean square as the mesh does. The page makes no pathwise claim about a single path.

 265: Mechanics

 267: M1 · A two-step partition

 269: Let \(t = 1\) and let \(\mathcal P\) be the partition \(0 < 1/2 < 1\). Compute \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)]\) and \(\Var(Q(\mathcal P))\) exactly, assuming Assumption 3.2.

 271: Solution

 273: Here \(\Delta_1 = \Delta_2 = 1/2\). By Lemma 2.2, each \(\E[(\Delta B_k)^2] = 1/2\), so \(\E[Q(\mathcal P)] = 1\). Also \(\Var((\Delta B_k)^2) = 2(1/2)^2 = 1/2\). By Assumption 3.2(a) the two squares are independent, and by 3.2(b) the variance of their sum is the sum of the variances, so \(\Var(Q(\mathcal P)) = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1\). This equals \(2\sum_k \Delta_k^2 = 2 \cdot 1/2 = 1\).

 275: M2 · Moments of a normal with variance 4

 277: Let \(X\) be normal with mean zero and variance 4. Compute \(\E[X^2]\), \(\E[X^4]\) and \(\Var(X^2)\).

 279: Solution

 281: Here \(\Delta = 4\) in Lemma 2.2. So \(\E[X^2] = 4\), \(\E[X^4] = 3 \cdot 4^2 = 48\), and \(\Var(X^2) = 2 \cdot 4^2 = 32\). As a check, \(\E[X^4] - (\E[X^2])^2 = 48 - 16 = 32\).

 283: M3 · A smooth function over equal steps

 285: Let \(f(s) = s\) and let \(\mathcal P\) be the equal partition of \([0,1]\) into \(N\) steps. Compute \(\sum_k \bigl(f(t_{k+1}) - f(t_k)\bigr)^2\) exactly, and find its limit as \(N\) grows.

 287: Solution

 289: Each increment of \(f\) is \(1/N\), so each squared increment is \(1/N^2\). There are \(N\) of them, so the sum is \(N \cdot 1/N^2 = 1/N\). As \(N\) grows the sum tends to 0.

 293: P1 · The mean and variance of a sum of squared independent normals

 295: Let \(X_1, \dots, X_N\) be independent, with \(X_k\) normal with mean zero and variance \(\Delta_k\), and let \(S = \sum_k X_k^2\). Assuming Assumption 3.2, prove \(\E[S] = \sum_k \Delta_k\) and \(\Var(S) = 2\sum_k \Delta_k^2\), and state each assumption used.

 297: Solution

 299: By Lemma 2.2, \(\E[X_k^2] = \Delta_k\), so \(\E[S] = \sum_k \Delta_k\) by linearity of expectation. By Assumption 3.2(a), the squares \(X_1^2, \dots, X_N^2\) are independent, and each has finite second moment by Lemma 2.2. By Assumption 3.2(b), \(\Var(S) = \sum_k \Var(X_k^2)\). Lemma 2.2 gives \(\Var(X_k^2) = 2\Delta_k^2\), so \(\Var(S) = 2\sum_k \Delta_k^2\). The assumptions used are Lemma 2.2, Lemma 2.1 through it, and Assumption 3.2 parts (a) and (b).

 301: P2 · A bound on squared differences of \(s^2\)

 303: Prove that \((b^2 - a^2)^2 \le 4H^2(b - a)^2\) for \(0 \le a < b \le H\).

 305: Solution

 307: Factor \(b^2 - a^2 = (b - a)(b + a)\). Since \(0 \le a < b \le H\), we have \(0 \le b + a \le 2H\). Squaring the factored form gives \((b^2 - a^2)^2 = (b - a)^2 (b + a)^2 \le (b - a)^2 \cdot 4H^2\).

 309: P3 · The mean of the absolute value of a standard normal

 311: Let \(Z\) be standard normal. Prove that \(\E|Z| = \sqrt{2/\pi}\).

 313: Solution

 315: The density is even, so \(\E|Z| = 2\int_0^\infty z\,\varphi(z)\,dz\). Using \(z\varphi(z) = -\varphi'(z)\), \(\int_0^R z\varphi(z)\,dz = \varphi(0) - \varphi(R)\). Letting \(R \to \infty\) and using \(\varphi(R) \to 0\) gives \(\int_0^\infty z\varphi(z)\,dz = \varphi(0) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}\). Hence \(\E|Z| = 2/\sqrt{2\pi} = \sqrt{2/\pi}\).

 325: Only one value exceeds 2 in absolute value: \(z = 2.39\), at \(H = 1\) and \(N = 2\). All 16 values lie within \(\pm 3\), which is consistent with mean \(H\). A single seeded run cannot tell chance from a bias of that size, so the z values check one sample, not the theorem.

 327: E2 · Recomputing an exact sum

 329: Lab 1 prints a sum of 2.656250 for \(H = 2\) and \(N = 8\). Recompute it from \(H^4(4N^2 - 1)/(3N^3)\).

 331: Solution

 333: Here \(H^4 = 16\), \(4N^2 - 1 = 255\) and \(3N^3 = 1536\). So the sum is \(16 \cdot 255/1536 = 4080/1536 = 2.65625\), which is the printed value, to six decimal places.

 335: E3 · A population standard error

 337: Lab 1 prints a standard error of 0.0125 for \(H = 2\) and \(N = 128\). Compute the population value \(H\sqrt{2/N}/20\) and compare.

 339: Solution

 341: \(\sqrt{2/128} = \sqrt{1/64} = 1/8\), so \(H\sqrt{2/N} = 2/8 = 1/4\). Dividing by \(\sqrt{400} = 20\) gives \(1/80 = 0.0125\). The printed sample standard error agrees with the population value at four decimal places for this seed.

 343: Research

 345: R1 · The equal partition in closed form

 347: For the equal partition of \([0,t]\) into \(N\) steps, prove \(\E[Q] = t\) and \(\E[(Q - t)^2] = 2t^2/N\), assuming Assumption 3.2.

 349: Solution

 351: Each \(\Delta_k = t/N\), so \(\E[Q] = N \cdot t/N = t\) by Theorem 2.3. The mean-square error is \(2\sum_k \Delta_k^2 = 2N(t/N)^2 = 2t^2/N\).

 353: R2 · The least constant in the mean-square bound

 355: Show that \(c = 2\) is the least constant with \(\E[(Q(\mathcal P) - t)^2] \le c\,t\,|\mathcal P|\) for every partition \(\mathcal P\) of \([0,t]\), assuming Assumption 3.2.

 357: Solution

 359: Theorem 2.3 shows that \(c = 2\) works. For the equal partition into \(N\) steps, \(|\mathcal P| = t/N\) and \(\sum_k \Delta_k^2 = t^2/N = t|\mathcal P|\), so \(\E[(Q - t)^2] = 2t|\mathcal P|\) exactly. A constant \(c < 2\) fails at this partition, because \(c\,t|\mathcal P| < 2t|\mathcal P|\) for \(t > 0\).

 361: R3 · Dependent increments

 363: For each \(N\), let all \(N\) increments of the equal partition of \([0,t]\) equal one variable \(X_N\) with law \(\Normal(0,t/N)\). Compute \(\E[Q]\) and \(\Var(Q)\), and name the hypothesis of Theorem 2.3 that fails.

 365: Solution

 367: Here \(Q = N X_N^2\). So \(\E[Q] = N \cdot t/N = t\), and \(\Var(Q) = N^2 \cdot 2(t/N)^2 = 2t^2\) for every \(N\), by Lemma 2.2. The mesh \(t/N\) tends to zero, so Assumption 3.1 holds, but the variance does not tend to zero. One hypothesis fails. (H1) fails, because the increments are not independent as (D3) requires, so the family is not a standard Brownian motion. (H2) does not apply here, because Assumption 3.2 is stated for independent variables, and these squares are not independent.
