  36: The first-passage time \(\tau_a\) (Definition 1.1 of this lesson) is the first time a path reaches \(a\). Its distribution is the running-maximum tail of Lesson 7 read in time: the events \(\{\tau_a\le H\}\) and \(\{M_H\ge a\}\) coincide on every continuous path for \(a>0\), and under Assumptions A3 and A4 the closed form is \(\operatorname{erfc}(a/\sqrt{2H})\), where \(H\) is the horizon and erfc is the complementary error function of Lesson 7. Under Assumptions A3 and A4, this lesson shows that \(\tau_a\) is finite with probability one. It does not evaluate \(\E[\tau_a]\). Lab 1 checks the discrete tail identity of Lesson 6 by enumeration, computes exact truncated means (Proposition 2.3 of this lesson), and compares a seeded simulation with them.

  38: 1The first-passage time of Brownian motion

  40: Let \(B\) be a standard Brownian motion with continuous paths, as in Lesson 2. The closed forms below use Assumptions A3 and A4 of Lesson 5 through Lesson 7. Every result that uses them says so.

  42: Definition 1.1 (first-passage time)

  44: For \(a>0\), the [DFN id=def-first-passage-time concept=first-passage-time]first-passage time of \(B\) at \(a\) is the time \(\tau_a\) of Lesson 6, Section 2, the first time the path reaches \(a\). It is \(\infty\) if the path never reaches \(a\). This lesson studies its law and its finiteness.

  46: Proposition 1.2 (running maximum and first passage on each path)

  48: (H1) For a fixed outcome, \(s\mapsto B_s\) is continuous and \(B_0=0\).

  50: (H2) \(a>0\) and \(H\ge 0\) are fixed.

  52: Conclusion: \(\{\tau_a\le H\}=\{M_H\ge a\}\), where \(M_H=\max_{0\le s\le H}B_s\).

  54: Proof

  56: Suppose \(\tau_a\le H\). The set \(\{s: B_s=a\}\) is closed by continuity and nonempty, so it contains \(\tau_a\), and \(B_{\tau_a}=a\). Since \(\tau_a\in[0,H]\), \(M_H\ge B_{\tau_a}=a\). Conversely, suppose \(M_H\ge a\). The continuous function \(s\mapsto B_s\) attains its maximum on \([0,H]\) at some \(s^\ast\le H\), so \(B_{s^\ast}\ge a>0=B_0\). By the intermediate value theorem there is \(u\in(0,s^\ast]\) with \(B_u=a\). Then \(\tau_a\le u\le H\). \(\blacksquare\)

  58: Corollary 1.3 (distribution function of the first passage)

  60: Under H1 and H2 of Proposition 1.2, and assuming A3 and A4 of Lesson 5, for \(H>0\), \(\Prob(\tau_a\le H)=\Prob(M_H\ge a)=2\,(1-\Phi(a/\sqrt H))=\operatorname{erfc}(a/\sqrt{2H})\), where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function of Lesson 7.

  62: Proof

  64: The first equality is Proposition 1.2, which holds on every path that satisfies H1 and H2 and needs no probability assumption. The second is Theorem 1.3 of Lesson 7, which assumes A3 and A4 through Theorem 2.5 of Lesson 6. The erfc form is Corollary 2.6 of Lesson 6. \(\blacksquare\)

  66: Corollary 1.4 (finite with probability one)

  68: Assuming A3 and A4 of Lesson 5, and under H1 and H2 of Proposition 1.2, \(\Prob(\tau_a<\infty)=1\).

  70: Proof

  72: The event \(\{\tau_a<\infty\}\) is the union of the increasing events \(\{\tau_a\le m\}\) over integers \(m\ge 1\), because a finite \(\tau_a\) is at most some integer \(m\). By continuity of probability from below and Corollary 1.3, \(\Prob(\tau_a<\infty)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\Prob(\tau_a\le m)=\lim_{m\to\infty}2(1-\Phi(a/\sqrt m))\). Since \(a/\sqrt m\to 0\) and \(\Phi\) is continuous, the limit is \(2(1-\tfrac12)=1\). The argument is a limit of probabilities, so it establishes finiteness with probability one and nothing about any single outcome. \(\blacksquare\)

  74: Corollary 1.5 (density of the first passage)

  76: Assuming A3 and A4 of Lesson 5, and under H1 and H2 of Proposition 1.2, the law of \(\tau_a\) restricted to \((0,\infty)\) has the density \(f_a(H)=\dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,H^{3/2}}\,e^{-a^2/(2H)}\) for \(H>0\).

  78: Proof

  80: Let \(F(H)=2(1-\Phi(a\,H^{-1/2}))\) for \(H>0\), so \(F(H)=\Prob(\tau_a\le H)\) by Corollary 1.3. Differentiating with \(\Phi'=\varphi\), where \(\varphi(u)=e^{-u^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\), and the chain rule with \(\frac{d}{dH}\,aH^{-1/2}=-\tfrac{a}{2}H^{-3/2}\) give \(F'(H)=-2\varphi(a H^{-1/2})\cdot\bigl(-\tfrac{a}{2}H^{-3/2}\bigr)=\dfrac{a}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,H^{3/2}}e^{-a^2/(2H)}=f_a(H)\). For \(0<\varepsilon<H\), \(F(H)-F(\varepsilon)=\int_\varepsilon^H f_a(s)\,ds\), because \(F\) is continuously differentiable on \((0,\infty)\). As \(\varepsilon\downarrow 0\), \(a/\sqrt\varepsilon\to\infty\), so \(F(\varepsilon)\to 0\). Hence \(F(H)=\int_0^H f_a(s)\,ds\), which says that \(f_a\) is the density of the law of \(\tau_a\) on \((0,\infty)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  82: Remark 1.6 (what is not evaluated)

  84: Corollary 1.4 gives the probability of finiteness, and Corollary 1.5 gives the law. This page does not compute \(\E[\tau_a]\), and it states no value or bound for it. Assuming A3 and A4, the density of Corollary 1.5 behaves like \(a\,H^{-3/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\) as \(H\to\infty\). The walk truncated means (Proposition 2.3 of this lesson) in Lab 1, Part B are sums of probabilities up to a fixed number of steps for the walk, and they are not a value of \(\E[\tau_a]\).

  86: 2First passage of the simple random walk

  88: The lab works with the simple random walk of Lesson 6, the sum \(S_k=e_1+\dots+e_k\) with \(S_0=0\) and each step \(e_i\) equal to \(+1\) or \(-1\). Its counts are exact integers, and they check discrete analogues of the events that the Brownian results use.

  90: Definition 2.1 (first passage of the walk)

  92: For an integer \(a\ge 1\), let \(\nu_a=\min\{k\ge 1: S_k\ge a\}\), with \(\nu_a=\infty\) if no such \(k\) exists. Then \(\{\nu_a\le N\}\) is the event that the walk is at or above \(a\) by step \(N\).

  94: Proposition 2.2 (walk identity for first passage)

  96: For integers \(a\ge 1\) and \(N\ge 1\), on every step sequence, \(\{\nu_a\le N\}=\{\max_{0\le k\le N}S_k\ge a\}\). Hence \(\#\{\nu_a\le N\}=\#\{S_N\ge a\}+\#\{S_N>a\}\) over the \(2^N\) step sequences.

  98: Proof

 100: If \(\nu_a\le N\), then \(S_{\nu_a}\ge a\) with \(\nu_a\le N\), so the maximum is at least \(a\). Conversely, let \(\max_{k\le N}S_k\ge a\), and let \(k^\ast\) be the first index with \(S_{k^\ast}\ge a\). Since \(S_0=0<a\), \(k^\ast\ge 1\), and \(S_{k^\ast-1}\le a-1\). The step is \(\pm 1\), so \(S_{k^\ast}\le a\), which gives \(S_{k^\ast}=a\) and \(\nu_a=k^\ast\le N\). Since the two events coincide, their counts coincide, and the count of \(\{\max_{k\le N}S_k\ge a\}\) is \(\#\{S_N\ge a\}+\#\{S_N>a\}\) by the discrete tail identity of Corollary 1.4 of Lesson 6. \(\blacksquare\)

 102: Proposition 2.3 (exact truncated mean of the walk)

 104: For integers \(a\ge 1\) and \(N\ge 1\), \(\E[\min(\nu_a,N)]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Prob(\nu_a>k)\), and \(\Prob(\nu_a>k)=1-\Prob(\max_{0\le j\le k}S_j\ge a)\). Each probability in the first sum is an integer divided by \(2^k\).

 106: Proof

 108: For each outcome, \(\min(\nu_a,N)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\indic\{\nu_a>k\}\). If \(\nu_a\le N\), the terms with \(k\ge \nu_a\) vanish and the sum counts \(k=0,\dots,\nu_a-1\), which is \(\nu_a\). If \(\nu_a>N\), every term equals 1 and the sum is \(N\). Taking expectations of this finite sum gives the first identity. The second follows from Proposition 2.2 with \(N\) replaced by \(k\ge 1\), since \(\{\nu_a>k\}\) is the complement of \(\{\nu_a\le k\}\). For \(k=0\), both sides equal 1, because \(S_0=0<a\) and \(\nu_a\ge 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

 328: [SECTION class=canon-breaks id=canon-breaks]

 330: 3Where the canon breaks

 332: The identity needs continuous paths. Proposition 1.2 uses the intermediate value theorem on a continuous path. Let the path be \(0\) on \([0,\tfrac12)\), jump to \(2\) at \(\tfrac12\), and stay at 2. Then \(M_1=2\ge 1\), but the path never equals 1, so \(\tau_1=\infty\) and the event \(\{\tau_1\le 1\}\) fails. The identity is therefore not asserted for paths with jumps.

 334: The closed form is conditional. Corollaries 1.3 to 1.5 assume A3 and A4 of Lesson 5; Section 2 does not. Corollary 1.4 is a statement about probability, so it does not say that the set of outcomes with \(\tau_a=\infty\) is empty.

 336: Finite does not mean integrable. Under A3 and A4, Corollary 1.4 gives finiteness with probability one. It gives no bound on \(\E[\tau_a]\). This page does not evaluate it.

 340: 4Misconceptions

 342: “The first-passage time \(\tau_a\) is finite on every path.”

 344: Under A3 and A4, Corollary 1.4 gives \(\Prob(\tau_a<\infty)=1\). That is a statement about the probability of the event \(\{\tau_a=\infty\}\), which is zero. It does not say that the event is empty. The proof passes to the limit in probabilities and examines no single outcome.

 346: “The first passage to \(a\) has the same probability as the terminal value: \(\Prob(\tau_a\le H)=\Prob(B_H\ge a)\).”

 348: Assuming A3 and A4, Corollary 1.3 gives \(\Prob(\tau_a\le H)=2\,\Prob(B_H\ge a)\). At \(H=1\) and \(a=1\), Lab 1, Part D prints \(\Prob(\tau_1\le 1)=0.317311\), while \(\Prob(B_1\ge 1)=0.1586552539\) (Lesson 7, Part D). For the walk, the ratio of \(\Prob(\nu_3\le 12)\) to \(\Prob(S_{12}\ge 3)\) is exactly two at \(n=12\) and \(a=3\): Lab 1, Part A prints 0.387695 for the first passage by step 12 and 0.193848 for the endpoint. Problem R1 gives the factor two up to the term \(\#\{S_{12}=3\}\), and parity makes that term vanish here.

 350: “Finiteness with probability one implies that the mean of \(\tau_a\) is finite.”

 352: Finiteness does not bound a mean. Under A3 and A4, the density of Corollary 1.5 behaves like a constant times \(H^{-3/2}\) for large \(H\), and this page evaluates no moment of \(\tau_a\). Lab 1, Part B prints truncated means that increase with the number of steps, and the page states no limit for them.

 360: Mechanics

 362: M1 · A first passage at horizon one

 364: Let \(a=1\) and \(H=1\). Compute \(\Prob(\tau_1\le 1)\), assuming A3 and A4.

 366: Solution

 368: By Corollary 1.3, \(\Prob(\tau_1\le 1)=2(1-\Phi(1))=\operatorname{erfc}(1/\sqrt2)\). Lab 1, Part D prints \(\Prob(\tau_1\le 1)=0.317311\), which is, to six decimals, twice the value \(0.1586552539\) of \(1-\Phi(1)\) printed by Lesson 7, Lab 1, Part D (\(2\times 0.1586552539=0.3173105078\), which rounds to \(0.317311\)).

 370: M2 · The density at a point

 372: Assuming A3 and A4, let \(a=1\). Evaluate \(f_1(1)\) from Corollary 1.5 and compare it with Lab 1, Part D.

 374: Solution

 376: Here \(f_1(1)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-1/2}\). Since \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=0.398942\) and \(e^{-1/2}=0.606531\), the product is \(0.241971\) (the factors rounded to six decimals; unrounded \(0.2419707\)). Part D prints the density 0.241971 at \(H=1\), and the central difference of the distribution function gives the same value.

 378: M3 · A walk of three steps

 380: For the simple random walk of 3 steps, find \(\Prob(\nu_1\le 3)\) by listing the sequences, and check it with the discrete tail identity of Lesson 6.

 382: Solution

 384: Sequences starting with \(+1\) reach 1 at step 1, which gives 4 sequences. Sequences starting with \(-1\) reach 1 only through \((-1,+1,+1)\), which gives 1 more. The total is 5 of \(2^3=8\), so \(\Prob(\nu_1\le 3)=5/8=0.625\). The walk identity gives \(\#\{S_3\ge 1\}+\#\{S_3>1\}=(3+1)+1=5\), which agrees.

 386: Proof

 388: P1 · Proposition 1.2 on one path

 390: Prove that on a continuous path with \(B_0=0\), \(\{\tau_a\le H\}=\{M_H\ge a\}\) for \(a>0\). State each hypothesis used.

 392: Solution

 394: If \(\tau_a\le H\), the set \(\{s:B_s=a\}\) is closed and nonempty by continuity, so \(B_{\tau_a}=a\) with \(\tau_a\in[0,H]\), giving \(M_H\ge a\). If \(M_H\ge a\), the maximum over \([0,H]\) is attained at \(s^\ast\le H\) with \(B_{s^\ast}\ge a>0=B_0\), and the intermediate value theorem gives \(u\in(0,s^\ast]\) with \(B_u=a\), so \(\tau_a\le u\le H\). Hypotheses used: continuity of the path, \(B_0=0\), and \(a>0\).

 396: P2 · The walk identity

 398: Prove that for integers \(a\ge 1\) and \(N\ge 1\), the events \(\{\nu_a\le N\}\) and \(\{\max_{k\le N}S_k\ge a\}\) coincide on every step sequence.

 400: Solution

 402: If \(\nu_a\le N\), then \(S_{\nu_a}\ge a\), so the maximum is at least \(a\). If the maximum is at least \(a\), let \(k^\ast\) be the first index with \(S_{k^\ast}\ge a\). Then \(k^\ast\ge 1\), since \(S_0=0<a\). The previous value satisfies \(S_{k^\ast-1}\le a-1\), and the step is \(\pm 1\), so \(S_{k^\ast}=a\). Hence \(\nu_a=k^\ast\le N\).

 404: P3 · The truncated mean

 406: Prove that \(\E[\min(\nu_a,N)]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\Prob(\nu_a>k)\) for integers \(a\ge 1\) and \(N\ge 1\).

 408: Solution

 410: For each outcome, \(\min(\nu_a,N)=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\indic\{\nu_a>k\}\). If \(\nu_a\le N\), the sum counts \(k=0,\dots,\nu_a-1\), which is \(\nu_a\). If \(\nu_a>N\), each of the \(N\) terms is 1. Taking expectations of a finite sum of indicators gives \(\Prob(\nu_a>k)\) for each \(k\), and linearity of expectation gives the identity.

 412: Empirical

 414: E1 · Parity of the first-passage counts

 416: Lab 1, Part A prints counts of 512, 0, 384, 0, 288, 0, 224, 0, 180 and 0 for steps 3 to 12 when \(a=3\) and \(n=12\). Explain the zeros and check the total.

 418: Solution

 420: \(S_k\) has the parity of \(k\), because each step changes the sum by \(\pm1\). Reaching level 3 at step \(k\) needs \(S_k=3\), so \(k\) must be odd, and the even steps have count 0. The odd counts are 512 at step 3, 384 at step 5, 288 at step 7, 224 at step 9 and 180 at step 11. Their sum is 1588, which is the walk count the lab prints.

 422: E2 · Reading the seeded means

 424: Lab 1, Part C prints sample means 5.2850, 11.3840 and 23.1240, against exact values 5.4806, 11.8157 and 24.5572. State the z values the lab prints and what one seed shows.

 426: Solution

 428: The lab prints z values of \(-1.05\), \(-0.69\) and \(-0.77\), so each sample mean lies below its exact value by less than 1.1 standard errors. The walks are the same for all three step counts, so the three comparisons are correlated. One seed of 1000 walks cannot test the exact values separately, and it cannot show a bias either way.

 430: E3 · The density at a second point

 432: Lab 1, Part D prints a density of 0.109848 at \(a=1\) and \(H=2\). Assuming A3 and A4, recompute it from Corollary 1.5.

 434: Solution

 436: The formula is \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\,H^{-3/2}e^{-1/(2H)}\) with \(a=1\). At \(H=2\), \(H^{-3/2}=2^{-3/2}=0.353553\) and \(e^{-1/4}=0.778801\). The product with \(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}=0.398942\) is \(0.109848\) from the rounded factors (factors rounded to six decimals; unrounded 0.1098478), which matches the printed value.

 438: Research

 440: R1 · Why the walk ratio is exactly two here

 442: Show that \(\#\{\nu_a\le N\}=2\,\#\{S_N\ge a\}-\#\{S_N=a\}\) for integers \(a\ge 1\) and \(N\ge 1\). Use it to explain why Lab 1 gives \(\Prob(\nu_3\le 12)=2\,\Prob(S_{12}\ge 3)\) exactly.

 444: Solution

 446: By Proposition 2.2, \(\#\{\nu_a\le N\}=\#\{S_N\ge a\}+\#\{S_N>a\}\). Since \(\#\{S_N>a\}=\#\{S_N\ge a\}-\#\{S_N=a\}\), the identity follows. For \(N=12\) and \(a=3\), \(S_{12}\) is even, so \(\#\{S_{12}=3\}=0\). Hence \(\#\{\nu_3\le 12\}=2\,\#\{S_{12}\ge 3\}\), which gives 1588 = 2 × 794, matching the printed probabilities 0.387695 and 0.193848.

 448: R2 · Increments of the truncated mean

 450: Show that \(\E[\min(\nu_a,N+1)]-\E[\min(\nu_a,N)]=\Prob(\nu_a>N)\), and use it to show that the truncated mean is nondecreasing in \(N\).

 452: Solution

 454: For each outcome, \(\min(\nu_a,N+1)-\min(\nu_a,N)=\indic\{\nu_a>N\}\). Taking expectations gives the increment \(\Prob(\nu_a>N)\), which is nonnegative. So the truncated mean is nondecreasing in \(N\).

 456: R3 · The mode of the first-passage density

 458: For fixed \(a>0\), find the time \(H\) at which \(f_a\) is largest, assuming A3 and A4.

 460: Solution

 462: Write \(\log f_a(H)=\text{const}-\tfrac32\log H-\dfrac{a^2}{2H}\). Its derivative in \(H\) is \(-\dfrac{3}{2H}+\dfrac{a^2}{2H^2}=\dfrac{a^2-3H}{2H^2}\). This is positive for \(H<a^2/3\) and negative for \(H>a^2/3\), because the denominator is positive. So \(\log f_a\), and hence \(f_a\), increases up to \(H=a^2/3\) and decreases after it, and \(f_a\) is largest at \(H=a^2/3\). The result uses Corollary 1.5, and so it assumes A3 and A4.
