  36: Reflect a Brownian path after its first visit to a level \(a\). The reflected value at a fixed time has the same law as the original value (Proposition 2.3, under Assumptions A3 and A4 of Lesson 5); the path-level reflection is not proved here (Remark 2.4). The reflection turns "how far does the path rise" into "where does it end", a Gaussian answer. The lesson proves the discrete version exactly for the symmetric simple random walk (defined in Section 1), including the correction term \(\Prob(S_N=a)\). It then proves the continuous identity \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge a)\), where \(M_t\) is the running maximum of \(B\) on \([0,t]\), under the strong Markov property of Lesson 5 (Assumption A3) and the facts of Assumption A4.

  38: 1The discrete reflection principle, exactly

  40: Let \(N\ge 1\) be an integer. A step sequence is \((e_1,\dots,e_N)\in\{-1,1\}^N\). The walk is \(S_0=0\) and \(S_k=e_1+\dots+e_k\) for \(1\le k\le N\). This is the [DFN id=None concept=None]simple random walk of length \(N\). Its \(2^N\) step sequences have equal weight \(2^{-N}\), so each probability below is a count divided by \(2^N\). The running maximum is \(M_N=\max_{0\le k\le N}S_k\). For an integer level \(a\ge 1\), let \(\kappa=\min\{k : S_k=a\}\) be the first visit to \(a\), when it occurs at some \(k\le N\). Because the walk moves by \(\pm 1\), it visits every integer between \(0\) and \(S_N\).

  42: Definition 1.1 (reflected walk)

  44: Let \(a\ge 1\) and let the step sequence satisfy \(\kappa\le N\). The [DFN id=None concept=None]reflected walk has steps \(e'_i=e_i\) for \(i\le\kappa\) and \(e'_i=-e_i\) for \(\kappa<i\le N\). Its walk is \(S'_k\).

  46: Lemma 1.2 (the reflected walk)

  48: Assumptions. \(N\ge 1\), \(a\ge 1\), and a step sequence with \(\kappa\le N\).

  50: Statement. \(S'_k=S_k\) for \(k\le\kappa\), and \(S'_k=2a-S_k\) for \(\kappa\le k\le N\). In particular \(S'_\kappa=a\), \(S'_N=2a-S_N\), and \(\kappa'=\kappa\), where \(\kappa'=\min\{k : S'_k=a\}\) is the first visit of the reflected walk to \(a\). Reflection is an involution on the step sequences with \(\kappa\le N\).

  52: Proof

  54: For \(k\le\kappa\) the steps agree, so \(S'_k=S_k\). For \(k\ge\kappa\), \(S'_k=S'_\kappa+\sum_{\kappa<i\le k}(-e_i)=a-(S_k-S_\kappa)=a-(S_k-a)=2a-S_k\), using \(S_\kappa=a\) and \(S'_\kappa=S_\kappa\). The prefix up to \(\kappa\) is unchanged, so \(\kappa'=\kappa\), because \(\kappa\) depends only on the prefix. Applying the reflection to \((e'_i)\) flips the steps after \(\kappa\) back, so it returns \((e_i)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  56: Proposition 1.3 (discrete reflection identity)

  58: Assumptions. \(N\ge 1\) and \(a\ge 1\) are integers, and \(b<a\) is an integer. The walk is the simple random walk of length \(N\).

  60: Statement. The number of step sequences with \(M_N\ge a\) and \(S_N=b\) equals the number of step sequences with \(S_N=2a-b\).

  62: Proof

  64: Since \(S_0=0<a\) and the walk moves by \(\pm 1\), \(M_N\ge a\) holds exactly when \(\kappa\le N\). So the left side counts the step sequences with \(\kappa\le N\) and \(S_N=b\). By Lemma 1.2, reflection maps this set into the set of step sequences with \(\kappa\le N\) and \(S'_N=2a-b\), and it is an involution, so it is a bijection between the two sets.

  66: The last set is the set of all step sequences with \(S_N=2a-b\). Each has \(\kappa\le N\), because \(S_0=0<a<2a-b=S_N\), and the walk moves by \(\pm 1\), so it visits \(a\) at some \(k\le N\). So the set of sequences with \(\kappa\le N\) and \(S_N=2a-b\) is the set of all sequences with \(S_N=2a-b\). The two counts are equal. \(\blacksquare\)

  68: Corollary 1.4 (discrete tail identity)

  70: Assumptions. Those of Proposition 1.3, for every integer \(a\ge 1\).

  72: Statement. \(\#\{M_N\ge a\}=\#\{S_N\ge a\}+\#\{S_N>a\}\), where \(\#\) counts step sequences. Hence

  74: \[
  75: \Prob(M_N\ge a)=\Prob(S_N\ge a)+\Prob(S_N>a)=2\,\Prob(S_N>a)+\Prob(S_N=a).
  76: \]

  78: Proof

  80: Split \(\{M_N\ge a\}\) by the value of \(S_N\). Every sequence with \(S_N\ge a\) has \(M_N\ge a\). For \(b<a\), the sequences with \(M_N\ge a\) and \(S_N=b\) are counted by Proposition 1.3. So \(\#\{M_N\ge a\}=\#\{S_N\ge a\}+\sum_{b<a}\#\{S_N=2a-b\}\). The map \(b\mapsto 2a-b\) is a bijection from the integers below \(a\) onto the integers above \(a\), so the sum is \(\#\{S_N>a\}\). The probability form divides by \(2^N\), and \(\Prob(S_N\ge a)=\Prob(S_N>a)+\Prob(S_N=a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

  82: 2The Brownian reflection, under Assumptions A3 and A4

  84: Let \(B\) be a standard Brownian motion with continuous paths, with the laws of Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1. Lesson 2 gives such a process on \([0,1]\), under Assumption A2 and Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 2 (Theorem 2.5 there). Remark 2.6 of Lesson 2 extends it to \([0,\infty)\), with a proof under Assumptions A1(a), A1(b) and A1(e) of Lesson 1, and Assumption A2 and Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 2; this page does not reprove it. The results below need the path only on \([0,t]\) for a fixed \(t>0\). For \(a>0\), write \(\tau_a=\inf\{s\ge 0 : B_s=a\}\), with \(\inf\varnothing=\infty\), for the first time the path reaches \(a\). Lesson 8 studies the law of this time. Write \(\mathcal F_t=\sigma(B_r : 0\le r\le t)\) for the information the path has revealed by time \(t\), as in Lesson 5, Section 1, and let \(\mathcal F_\rho\) be the \(\sigma\)-field of events known at a stopping time \(\rho\), as in Lesson 5, Section 3. The results use the events \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\) and the value \(B_t\), and Proposition 2.3 also uses the random time \(\rho=\tau_a\wedge t\) and the reflected value \(W_t\) defined in its statement.

  86: Lemma 2.1 (running maximum and first visit)

  88: Assumptions. \(B\) has continuous paths, \(a>0\), \(t>0\), and \(B_0=0\).

  90: Statement. (i) \(\{M_t\ge a\}=\{\tau_a\le t\}\), where \(M_t=\max_{0\le s\le t}B_s\). (ii) \(\{B_t\ge a\}\subseteq\{\tau_a\le t\}\). (iii) On \(\{\tau_a>t\}\), \(B_t<a\).

  92: Proof

  94: (i) Suppose \(M_t\ge a\). The continuous function \(s\mapsto B_s\) attains its maximum on \([0,t]\) at some \(s^\ast\le t\), so \(B_{s^\ast}\ge a>0=B_0\). By the intermediate value theorem there is \(s\le s^\ast\) with \(B_s=a\), so \(\tau_a\le s\le t\). Conversely, if \(\tau_a\le t\), the set \(\{s : B_s=a\}\) is closed by continuity and nonempty, so \(B_{\tau_a}=a\) and \(M_t\ge B_{\tau_a}=a\).

  96: (ii) If \(B_t\ge a\), the same intermediate value argument on \([0,t]\) gives \(\tau_a\le t\).

  98: (iii) Let \(\tau_a>t\). If \(B_s>a\) for some \(s\le t\), then the intermediate value theorem gives a time in \((0,s)\) where \(B=a\), which contradicts \(\tau_a>t\). So \(B_s\le a\) on \([0,t]\). Since \(B_s\neq a\) for \(s<\tau_a\), we get \(B_t<a\). \(\blacksquare\)

 100: Definition 2.2 (reflection principle)

 102: The [DFN id=def-reflection-principle concept=reflection-principle]reflection principle for \(B\) at \(\tau_a\) is the statement that the path reflected after \(\tau_a\), namely \(W_s=B_s\) for \(s\le\tau_a\) and \(W_s=2a-B_s\) for \(s>\tau_a\), is again a standard Brownian motion. Assumption A3 of Lesson 5 covers only bounded stopping times, and this page does not show that \(\tau_a\) is bounded, so the statement is not asserted from A3. On this page the path-level statement is not proved. Only its fixed-time form is proved, under the assumptions of Proposition 2.3.

 104: Proposition 2.3 (the reflected value has the law of B at a fixed time)

 106: Assumptions.

 108: \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion with continuous paths, and \(a>0\), \(t>0\).

 110: Assumption A3 of Lesson 5 (strong Markov property), applied to the bounded stopping time \(\rho=\tau_a\wedge t\).

 112: Assumptions A4(b), A4(c), A4(g) and A4(h) of Lesson 5 (freezing; hitting times are stopping times; joint measurability; measurability at a stopping time).

 114: Statement. Assuming A3 and A4(b), A4(c), A4(g) and A4(h) of Lesson 5, define \(W_t=2B_\rho-B_t\) with \(\rho=\tau_a\wedge t\). Then \(W_t\) has the same law as \(B_t\). Explicitly, \(W_t=B_t\) on \(\{\tau_a>t\}\) and \(W_t=2a-B_t\) on \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\).

 116: Proof

 118: By A4(c), \(\tau_a\) is a stopping time, because \(\{a\}\) is closed and \(B\) is continuous and adapted (each \(B_r\) is \(\mathcal F_r\)-measurable). So \(\rho=\tau_a\wedge t\) is a stopping time with \(\rho\le t\): \(\{\rho\le r\}=\{\tau_a\le r\}\in\mathcal F_r\) for \(r<t\), and \(\{\rho\le r\}=\Omega\) for \(r\ge t\). On \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\), \(B_\rho=B_{\tau_a}=a\), so \(W_t=2a-B_t\). On \(\{\tau_a>t\}\), \(\rho=t\) and \(W_t=B_t\). This gives the explicit form.

 120: Let \(X_r=B_{\rho+r}-B_\rho\) for \(r\ge 0\). By A3, \(X\) is a standard Brownian motion, independent of \(\mathcal F_\rho\). Since \(\rho+(t-\rho)=t\), we have \(B_t-B_\rho=X_{t-\rho}\), and therefore

 122: \[
 123: W_t=2B_\rho-B_t=B_\rho-X_{t-\rho},\qquad B_t=B_\rho+X_{t-\rho}.
 124: \]

 126: Let \(g\) be bounded and Borel. By Assumption A4(h) of Lesson 5, applied to \(\rho\le t\), the variables \(B_\rho\) and \(\rho\) are \(\mathcal F_\rho\)-measurable, so \(t-\rho\in[0,t]\) is too. Also \(X\) is independent of \(\mathcal F_\rho\). Assumption A4(g) makes \((u,\omega)\mapsto X_u(\omega)\) jointly measurable on \([0,t]\times\Omega\), so \(X_{t-\rho}\) is a random variable. By A4(b), applied with \(\mathcal G=\mathcal F_\rho\), \(Z=(B_\rho,t-\rho)\), \(Y=X\) (a random continuous path, as A4(b) allows), and the bounded jointly measurable functions \(f_\mp((x,u),y)=g(x\mp y_u)\),

 128: \[
 129: \E[g(W_t)\mid\mathcal F_\rho]=h_-(B_\rho,t-\rho),\qquad \E[g(B_t)\mid\mathcal F_\rho]=h_+(B_\rho,t-\rho),
 130: \]

 132: where \(h_\mp(x,u)=\E[g(x\mp X_u)]\). Since \(X_u\) has the law \(\Normal(0,u)\) by (D1) and (D2) for \(X\), and this law is symmetric, \(-X_u\) has the same law as \(X_u\). So \(h_-(x,u)=h_+(x,u)\) for all \(x\) and \(u\in[0,t]\). Hence \(\E[g(W_t)\mid\mathcal F_\rho]=\E[g(B_t)\mid\mathcal F_\rho]\) almost surely (that is, with probability one). Taking expectations gives \(\E g(W_t)=\E g(B_t)\) for every bounded Borel \(g\), so \(W_t\) and \(B_t\) have the same law. \(\blacksquare\)

 134: Remark 2.4 (what is not proved)

 136: Proposition 2.3 is a statement about one time \(t\) at a time. It does not show that the whole reflected path \(W\) is a standard Brownian motion, which is the reflection principle of Definition 2.2. That path-level statement is not used below, and this page does not claim it.

 138: Theorem 2.5 (the reflection identity for Brownian motion)

 140: Assumptions.

 142: \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion with continuous paths, and \(a>0\), \(t>0\).

 144: Assumption A3 of Lesson 5 (applied in Proposition 2.3) and Assumption A4 of Lesson 5.

 146: Statement. Assuming A3 and A4,

 148: \[
 149: \Prob(M_t\ge a)=\Prob(B_t\ge a)+\Prob(B_t>a)=2\,\Prob(B_t>a)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge a).
 150: \]

 152: Proof

 154: Let \(\rho\) and \(W_t\) be as in Proposition 2.3. Write \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\) for the event of Lemma 2.1(i), so \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)=\Prob(\tau_a\le t)\).

 156: By Lemma 2.1(ii), \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\) contains \(\{B_t\ge a\}\). Split \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\) into \(\{B_t\ge a\}\) and \(E=\{\tau_a\le t, B_t<a\}\). So \(\Prob(\tau_a\le t)=\Prob(B_t\ge a)+\Prob(E)\).

 158: We identify \(E\). On \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\), \(W_t=2a-B_t\), so \(W_t>a\) exactly when \(B_t<a\). Hence \(E=\{\tau_a\le t,\,W_t>a\}\). The event \(\{W_t>a\}\) lies inside \(\{\tau_a\le t\}\): on \(\{\tau_a>t\}\), \(W_t=B_t<a\) by Lemma 2.1(iii). So \(E=\{W_t>a\}\).

 160: By Proposition 2.3, \(\Prob(W_t>a)=\Prob(B_t>a)\). Therefore \(\Prob(\tau_a\le t)=\Prob(B_t\ge a)+\Prob(B_t>a)\). Since \(B_t\) has the normal law \(\Normal(0,t)\) with \(t>0\), it has a density, so \(\Prob(B_t=a)=0\). The two tail probabilities are equal, and the result is \(2\,\Prob(B_t>a)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

 162: Corollary 2.6 (closed form of the tail)

 164: Assumptions. \(t>0\), \(a>0\), and the standard definition \(\operatorname{erfc}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_x^\infty e^{-w^2}\,dw\).

 166: Statement. \(2\,\Prob(B_t>a)=\operatorname{erfc}\big(a/\sqrt{2t}\big)\). Assuming A3 and A4 of Lesson 5, this is \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)\).

 168: Proof

 170: By (D2), \(B_t\) has the law \(\Normal(0,t)\), so \(B_t/\sqrt t\) is standard normal \(Z\). Then \(\Prob(B_t>a)=\Prob(Z>a/\sqrt t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{a/\sqrt t}^\infty e^{-z^2/2}\,dz\). Substitute \(z=\sqrt2\,w\), so \(dz=\sqrt2\,dw\) and \(z^2/2=w^2\). The lower limit becomes \(a/\sqrt{2t}\), and the integral becomes \(\frac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{a/\sqrt{2t}}^\infty e^{-w^2}\,dw=\frac{1}{\sqrt\pi}\int_{a/\sqrt{2t}}^\infty e^{-w^2}\,dw=\tfrac12\operatorname{erfc}(a/\sqrt{2t})\). Multiply by 2. \(\blacksquare\)

 290: 3Where the canon breaks

 292: Discrete grid. Corollary 1.4 has the extra term \(\Prob(S_N=a)\) that Theorem 2.5 does not have. Part D of Lab 1 shows the term at \(N=10\), \(a=2\). This page does not compute how the term behaves as \(N\) grows.

 294: Path continuity. Lemma 2.1 uses the intermediate value theorem, which needs continuous paths. A path that jumps from below \(a\) to above \(a\) has \(M_t\ge a\) without ever taking the value \(a\), so \(\{M_t\ge a\}\neq\{\tau_a\le t\}\) for such a path. The process \(\tilde B\) of Lesson 1, Section 4, has discontinuous paths, and nothing on this page applies it.

 296: Infinite hitting times. Proposition 2.3 uses \(\rho=\tau_a\wedge t\), which is bounded. The page does not establish that \(\tau_a\) is finite, and it does not state the law of \(\tau_a\). Lesson 8 does.

 298: Symmetry. The factor 2 comes from adding two events of equal probability in the proof of Theorem 2.5. Since \(E=\{W_t>a\}\), Proposition 2.3 gives \(\Prob(E)=\Prob(B_t>a)\), and that proposition uses the symmetry of \(\Normal(0,u)\) at the step \(h_-=h_+\). The fact \(\Prob(B_t=a)=0\), that \(B_t\) has no atom (no point of positive probability), follows from the normal density, and it is also needed to make \(\Prob(B_t\ge a)=\Prob(B_t>a)\). The page makes no claim about the tail of a process whose increments are not symmetric.

 300: Assumptions. Theorem 2.5 is conditional on A3 and A4 of Lesson 5, which are stated and not proved there. The path-level reflection principle is not proved, only its fixed-time form.

 304: 4Misconceptions

 306: “The running maximum has the same tail as the endpoint: \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)=\Prob(B_t\ge a)\).”

 308: Assuming A3 and A4 of Lesson 5, Theorem 2.5 gives \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge a)\). At \(t=1\) and \(a=1\), Corollary 2.6 gives \(2\,\Prob(B_1>1)=\operatorname{erfc}(1/\sqrt2)=0.3173\), so under that assumption \(\Prob(M_1\ge 1)=0.3173\), while \(\Prob(B_1\ge 1)=0.1587\) (both values rounded to four decimal places). Part E of Lab 1 prints the first value to ten decimal places.

 310: “The discrete identity \(\Prob(M_N\ge a)=2\,\Prob(S_N>a)\) holds exactly, so the continuum has the same form.”

 312: Corollary 1.4 has the extra term \(\Prob(S_N=a)\). At \(N=10\) and \(a=2\), Part D of Lab 1 prints \(\Prob(M_{10}\ge 2)=281/512\) and \(2\,\Prob(S_{10}>2)=11/32\). The difference \(105/512\) is \(\Prob(S_{10}=2)\), which is not zero.

 314: “Reflecting after the first visit to a level always gives an equal-law path, with no path assumption.”

 316: The identity \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)=\Prob(\tau_a\le t)\) needs continuity (Lemma 2.1), and the fixed-time law of the reflected value needs the strong Markov property (Assumption A3 of Lesson 5) and the facts of Assumption A4 of Lesson 5. Section 3 gives the failure for a jump.

 324: Mechanics

 326: M1 · Count the paths of length 3 that reach 1

 328: For \(N=3\) and \(a=1\), list the step sequences with \(M_3\ge 1\), count them, and check Corollary 1.4.

 330: Solution

 332: The sequences with \(e_1=+1\) reach 1 at \(k=1\): there are 4 of them. The sequence \((-1,+1,+1)\) has \(S=(0,-1,0,1)\), which reaches 1, so it counts. The other three sequences starting with \(-1\), namely \((-1,-1,-1)\), \((-1,+1,-1)\) and \((-1,-1,+1)\), have maxima 0, 0 and 0, so they do not count. The count is \(4+1=5\) of \(8\). For Corollary 1.4: \(\#\{S_3\ge 1\}=3+1=4\), from \(S_3=1\) (three sequences) and \(S_3=3\) (one), and \(\#\{S_3>1\}=1\). The sum is \(5\), which agrees.

 334: M2 · The probability of reaching 2 in two steps

 336: For \(N=2\) and \(a=2\), find \(\Prob(M_2\ge 2)\), and check Corollary 1.4.

 338: Solution

 340: Only the sequence \((+1,+1)\) reaches 2, so \(\Prob(M_2\ge 2)=1/4\). Corollary 1.4 gives \(\#\{S_2\ge 2\}+\#\{S_2>2\}=1+0=1\), since \(S_2\ge 2\) only for \((+1,+1)\) and \(S_2\) cannot exceed 2. The probability is \(1/4\), as before.

 342: M3 · Reflect a specific path

 344: Take \(N=5\), \(a=2\), and steps \((+1,+1,-1,-1,+1)\). Find \(\kappa\), the reflected steps, the reflected path and its endpoint, and check Lemma 1.2.

 346: Solution

 348: The path is \(S=(0,1,2,1,0,1)\), so \(\kappa=2\). The steps after \(\kappa\) are \((-1,-1,+1)\), which reflect to \((+1,+1,-1)\). The reflected steps are \((+1,+1,+1,+1,-1)\), with path \(S'=(0,1,2,3,4,3)\). Lemma 1.2 says \(S'_k=2a-S_k=4-S_k\) for \(k\ge 2\): \(4-2=2\), \(4-1=3\), \(4-0=4\), \(4-1=3\). These equal \(S'_2,\dots,S'_5=2,3,4,3\). The endpoint is \(S'_5=3=2a-S_5\).

 350: Proof

 352: P1 · Reflection is an involution

 354: Show that applying the reflection of Definition 1.1 twice returns the original step sequence, for every step sequence with \(\kappa\le N\).

 356: Solution

 358: Let \(e'\) be the reflected steps of \(e\). Then \(e'_i=e_i\) for \(i\le\kappa\) and \(e'_i=-e_i\) for \(\kappa<i\le N\). The first time the walk built from \(e'\) reaches \(a\) is \(\kappa'=\kappa\), by Lemma 1.2, so the reflection of \(e'\) negates the steps after the same \(\kappa\). Negating twice gives \(e''_i=e'_i\) for \(i\le\kappa\) and \(e''_i=-e'_i=e_i\) for \(\kappa<i\le N\). So \(e''=e\).

 360: P2 · Parts (ii) and (iii) of Lemma 2.1 from continuity

 362: Prove Lemma 2.1 (ii) and (iii) from continuity of \(B\) and \(B_0=0\), without using part (i).

 364: Solution

 366: (ii) If \(B_t\ge a\) with \(a>0=B_0\), then the continuous function \(s\mapsto B_s\) on \([0,t]\) takes the values 0 at time 0 and at least \(a\) at time \(t\). The intermediate value theorem gives some \(s\in[0,t]\) with \(B_s=a\), so \(\tau_a\le s\le t\). (iii) Suppose \(\tau_a>t\), so \(B_s\neq a\) for all \(s\le t\) by the definition of \(\tau_a\). If \(B_s>a\) for some \(s\le t\), the intermediate value theorem on \([0,s]\) gives a time in \((0,s)\) where \(B=a\), which would make \(\tau_a<s\le t\). So \(B_s<a\) for all \(s\le t\), and in particular \(B_t<a\).

 368: P3 · Corollary 1.4 from Proposition 1.3

 370: Derive \(\#\{M_N\ge a\}=\#\{S_N\ge a\}+\#\{S_N>a\}\) from Proposition 1.3 for a single integer \(a\ge 1\), without repeating the bijection.

 372: Solution

 374: Split the sequences with \(M_N\ge a\) by \(S_N\). Those with \(S_N\ge a\) all have \(M_N\ge a\), and there are \(\#\{S_N\ge a\}\) of them. Those with \(S_N=b<a\) and \(M_N\ge a\) are counted, for each \(b\), by \(\#\{S_N=2a-b\}\), from Proposition 1.3. As \(b\) runs over the integers below \(a\), \(2a-b\) runs over the integers above \(a\), so the sum is \(\#\{S_N>a\}\).

 376: Empirical

 378: E1 · How many cases does Part B check?

 380: Part B of Lab 1 prints \(78\) pairs \((N,a)\) checked, and all equal. Explain why the number is 78, and why the check covers every pair with \(1\le a\le N\le 12\).

 382: Solution

 384: For each \(N\), the code takes \(a\) from 1 to \(N\), so there are \(N\) pairs. Summing over \(N=1,\dots,12\) gives \(1+2+\dots+12=\tfrac{12\cdot 13}{2}=78\). All 78 pairs print as equal, so the tail identity holds on every pair the lab checks. The lab does not check \(a>N\), where \(\#\{M_N\ge a\}\) is zero.

 386: E2 · The discrete correction at N = 10

 388: From Part D of Lab 1, verify that \(\Prob(M_{10}\ge 2)-2\,\Prob(S_{10}>2)=\Prob(S_{10}=2)\), using the printed fractions.

 390: Solution

 392: The printed values are \(281/512\), \(11/32=352/1024\) and \(105/512=210/1024\). Then \(562/1024-352/1024=210/1024\), which is \(\Prob(S_{10}=2)\). The check agrees with Corollary 1.4 in the form \(\Prob(M_N\ge a)=2\,\Prob(S_N>a)+\Prob(S_N=a)\). The printed value \(\Prob(S_{10}=2)=\binom{10}{6}/1024=210/1024\) also matches the binomial count of sequences with six up-steps.

 394: E3 · Check the closed form at t = 1 and a = 1

 396: From Part E of Lab 1, the printed values are \(2\,\Prob(B_1>1)=0.3173105079\) and \(\Prob(B_1>1)=0.1586552539\). Check that the second is half the first, and that the first equals \(\operatorname{erfc}(1/\sqrt2)\).

 398: Solution

 400: The lab computes \(\Prob(B_1>1)\) as \(\operatorname{erfc}(1/\sqrt2)/2\), so the two printed values are the same closed form at two scales, and their ratio is 2 by construction. Halving the printed \(0.3173105079\) gives \(0.15865525395\), which differs from the printed \(0.1586552539\) only because the first value is itself rounded: the unrounded half is \(0.15865525393\ldots\), which prints as \(0.1586552539\). Corollary 2.6 with \(t=1\) and \(a=1\) gives \(2\,\Prob(B_1>1)=\operatorname{erfc}(1/\sqrt2)\).

 402: Research

 404: R1 · The identity without a parity condition

 406: Show that Proposition 1.3 needs no parity assumption on \(b\), and explain why both sides vanish when \(b\) and \(N\) have different parity.

 408: Solution

 410: Every \(S_N\) has the parity of \(N\), since it is a sum of \(N\) terms \(\pm 1\). If \(b\not\equiv N\pmod 2\), then no sequence has \(S_N=b\), so the left side is zero. Also \(2a-b\equiv b\pmod 2\), so \(2a-b\not\equiv N\pmod 2\), and no sequence has \(S_N=2a-b\), so the right side is zero. The identity holds with both sides zero, and the proof of Proposition 1.3 does not use parity anywhere.

 412: R2 · The minimum of B, assuming A3 for −B

 414: Let \(a>0\). Assuming A3 and A4 hold for \(-B\) as well, show that \(\Prob\big(\min_{s\le t}B_s\le -a\big)=2\,\Prob(B_t<-a)\).

 416: Solution

 418: Let \(B'=-B\). It is a standard Brownian motion with continuous paths, since (D1) to (D3) are preserved under sign change: \(B'_t-B'_s=-(B_t-B_s)\) has the law \(\Normal(0,t-s)\), and independence is preserved. Then \(\min_{s\le t}B_s\le -a\) holds exactly when \(\max_{s\le t}B'_s\ge a\). By Theorem 2.5 applied to \(B'\), \(\Prob(\max_{s\le t}B'_s\ge a)=2\,\Prob(B'_t>a)=2\,\Prob(-B_t>a)=2\,\Prob(B_t<-a)\).

 420: R3 · A strict inequality in the continuum

 422: Assuming A3 and A4, show that \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)>\Prob(B_t\ge a)>0\) for \(a>0\) and \(t>0\).

 424: Solution

 426: Theorem 2.5 gives \(\Prob(M_t\ge a)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge a)\). Since \(\Prob(B_t\ge a)>0\), the first inequality is \(2x>x\) for \(x=\Prob(B_t\ge a)>0\). Positivity holds because \(B_t\) has the normal law \(\Normal(0,t)\), which gives positive probability to every half-line. So both inequalities hold.
