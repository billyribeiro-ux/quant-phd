=== exemplar-a (blind) ===
[Summary] For seed 0 the tail is about 0.0865, and the grid estimates scatter around it. The reflection principle holds, so the running maximum follows the same law as twice the endpoint tail, and the first-passage identity follows from it. The results hold for every grid, as the run shows.

[The checks] The tail is \(\operatorname{erfc}(2.474/\sqrt{2 \cdot 2.083})\), which the run gives as 0.0865. The walk of 12 steps has 1588 walks reaching 3. The one-step estimate is 0.0595, with a standard error of 0.0107, and it lies 0.0163 above half the tail (0.0432), which is 1.52 standard errors (0.016300 / 0.010735, both printed by Lab 1).

[Appendix · Proofs] P1. Let \(\tau = \tau_a\), and let \(\rho\) be \(\tau\) cut off at \(t\). The paths are continuous, so \(\{\tau \le t\}\) is the event that the path has reached \(a\) by time \(t\). By symmetry, the restarted path is as likely to be above \(a\) as below, so \(\Prob(\tau \le t, B_t > a) = \tfrac12 \Prob(\tau \le t)\). Adding the paths that end at or above \(a\) gives \(\Prob(M_t \ge a) = 2\,\Prob(B_t \ge a)\).

[Discretization] Finer grids give estimates closer to the continuous tail, so the discretization error falls as the grid refines.

=== exemplar-b (blind) ===
[Summary] For seed 0 (H = 2.083, a = 2.474, N = 487 paths), the continuous tail is \(\Prob(M_H \ge a) = 0.086497\), which by P3 is also the first-passage probability by \(H\); no run checks it. The closed form matches Simpson's rule to within 10 to the minus 14. The one-step estimate, 0.059548 with standard error 0.010735, lies within the band for half the tail. On the same 256-step paths coarse counts never exceed the fine count of 40.

[Setup and conventions] The horizon is \(H\), not \(T\). The level is \(a\), and \(N\) is the number of paths. reflection_tail computes the continuous tail, discrete_max_exceedance the grid estimates with their standard errors, and nested_grid_counts the same-path counts. Every computed decimal is the unrounded float, rounded once to six places by Python's formatting, except the Simpson difference, which is given to three significant digits. The horizon and the level are printed as generated, to three decimals, and N is an integer. The walk counts are exact integers from srw_max_count and srw_terminal_count .

[The checks] The continuous tail is 0.086497. Simpson's rule for \(2\,\Prob(B_H \ge a)\) differs from it by 9.13e-15 in absolute value, as Lab 1 prints. The one-step row is the grid of one step, whose maximum is \(B_H\), so its target is half the tail, 0.043248. Its estimate is 0.059548, 0.016300 above the target, and inside the band of five target standard errors plus one path, 0.048142. For the walk of 12 steps at level 3, Lab 3 prints 1588 walks reaching the level and 794 ending at 3 or above. The walk identity says that the walks reaching 3 equal the walks ending at 3 or above plus the walks ending at 4 or above. A 12-step walk ends at an even value, so ending at 3 or above is the same as ending at 4 or above, and that count is also 794: 1588 = 794 + 794.

[Discretization] Lab 3 gives the grid estimates for 1, 4, 16, 64 and 256 steps. Their standard errors are 0.010735, 0.009819, 0.011239, 0.012870 and 0.012455. The tail minus each estimate is 0.026948 at 1 step, 0.037215 at 4 steps, 0.020788 at 16 steps, −0.001799 at 64 steps and 0.004361 at 256 steps. The one-step bias is tail/2 = 0.043248, and the printed figure 0.026948 estimates it. These rows use separate paths, so their differences mix bias with noise, and I do not order them by bias. On the 256-step paths, the nested counts are 35, 29, 27 and 22 for coarse grids of 64, 16, 4 and 1 steps, each at most the fine count of 40. The coarse maximum is at most the fine maximum on every path, so the coarse grid never has the higher estimate on these paths.

[Limitations] The check uses one seed, one instance and 487 paths, so it is a sample and not a proof. The tail is for the continuous maximum on \([0, H]\), and each grid estimate is a sample from a discrete law. The nested inequality holds on every path, but these counts come from one sample, so they say nothing about the size of the bias for other seeds or other path counts, and nothing about any statement that holds almost surely. The three results are proved for every case that meets their assumptions, in the appendix. Each printed decimal is rounded once, to six places, from the unrounded value, so a difference of two printed decimals can differ from the printed difference in the sixth place. No conclusion here turns on that place: the band check passes by far more than one unit in the sixth place.

[Appendix · Proofs] Assumptions for P1. A0: \(B\) is a standard Brownian motion with \(B_0 = 0\) and continuous paths. A3 of Lesson 5, for the bounded stopping time \(\rho = \tau \wedge t\); A4(b), A4(c), A4(g) and A4(h) of Lesson 5, for the freezing step, the first hitting time \(\tau\), the joint measurability of the restarted path, and the \(\mathcal F_\rho\)-measurability of \(B_\rho\) and \(\rho\). \(a > 0\) and \(t > 0\) are fixed. P1. Let \(\tau = \tau_a\), and let \(\rho = \tau \wedge t\). By A4(c), \(\tau\) is a stopping time. So \(\rho\) is one as well: \(\{\rho \le r\} = \{\tau \le r\}\) for \(r < t\), and \(\{\rho \le r\}\) is the whole space for \(r \ge t\), so both lie in \(\mathcal F_r\), by the definition of a stopping time in Section 3 of Lesson 5. Also \(\rho \le t\), so \(\rho\) is bounded and A3 applies to it. Let \(K = \{\tau \le t\}\). If \(\tau \le t\), then \(B_\tau = a\) by continuity, so \(M_t \ge a\). If \(M_t \ge a\), the maximum is attained at some \(s \le t\), because a continuous function attains its maximum on the compact interval \([0,t]\). The intermediate value theorem on \([0,s]\), with \(B_0 = 0 < a\) (A0 and \(a > 0\)), gives a time in \([0,s]\) at which \(B = a\), so \(\tau \le t\). Hence \(K = \{M_t \ge a\}\). Also \(\Prob(\tau = t) \le \Prob(B_t = a) = 0\), because \(\tau = t\) implies \(B_t = a\), and \(B_t\) is normal with variance \(t > 0\) (A0 and Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1), so it has no atom. Let \(\tilde B_r = B_{\rho + r} - B_\rho\). By A3, \(\tilde B\) is a standard Brownian motion independent of \(\mathcal F_\rho\). On \(K\), \(\rho = \tau\) and \(B_\rho = a\), so \(B_t - a = \tilde B_{t - \rho}\). The variable \(\rho\) is \(\mathcal F_\rho\)-measurable by A4(h), so \(t - \rho\) is too. The event \(K\) is \(\mathcal F_\rho\)-measurable: for \(r \ge t\), \(K \cap \{\rho \le r\} = K \in \mathcal F_t \subseteq \mathcal F_r\), because \(K = \{\tau \le t\} \in \mathcal F_t\) by the stopping property of \(\tau\); for \(r < t\), \(K \cap \{\rho \le r\} = \{\tau \le r\} \in \mathcal F_r\). Apply A4(b) with \(\mathcal G = \mathcal F_\rho\), \(Z = (t - \rho, \indic_K)\) and \(Y = \tilde B\), which is independent of \(\mathcal F_\rho\) by A3. The function \(f(z,y) = \indic_{\{z_2 = 1\}}\indic_{\{y(z_1) < 0\}}\) is bounded, and it is jointly measurable because \((u,\omega) \mapsto \tilde B_u(\omega)\) is jointly measurable by A4(g). Since \(\Prob(\tilde B_u < 0) = \tfrac12\) for \(u > 0\) (\(\tilde B_u\) is normal with mean 0) and \(\tilde B_0 = 0\), we get \(\bar f(z) = \tfrac12\indic_{\{z_2 = 1\}}\indic_{\{z_1 > 0\}}\). So A4(b) gives \(\Prob(K, B_t < a) = \Prob(K, \tilde B_{t-\rho} < 0) = \tfrac12\Prob(K, \rho < t) = \tfrac12\Prob(K)\), the last step because \(\Prob(\tau = t) = 0\). Also \(B_t \ge a\) implies \(M_t \ge a\), so \(\{B_t \ge a\} \subseteq K\), and \(\Prob(K) = \Prob(B_t \ge a) + \Prob(K, B_t < a) = \Prob(B_t \ge a) + \tfrac12\Prob(K)\). Therefore \(\Prob(M_t \ge a) = \Prob(K) = 2\,\Prob(B_t \ge a)\). Since \(\Prob(B_t = a) = 0\), this equals \(2\,\Prob(B_t > a)\). Assumptions for P2. The assumptions of P1. \(B_t \sim \Normal(0,t)\), by the definition of Brownian motion in Lesson 1 (Definition 1.1: \(B_0 = 0\), and each increment is normal with variance equal to its length). P2. By P1 and \(B_t \sim \Normal(0,t)\), \(\Prob(B_t \ge a) = 1 - \Phi(a/\sqrt{t})\), so \(\Prob(M_t \ge a) = 2(1 - \Phi(a/\sqrt{t}))\). For the erfc form, substitute \(w = z/\sqrt{2}\) in \(\operatorname{erfc}(x) = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_x^\infty e^{-w^2}\,dw\): this gives \(\operatorname{erfc}(x) = \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{x\sqrt2}^\infty e^{-z^2/2}\,dz = 2(1 - \Phi(x\sqrt{2}))\). With \(x = a/\sqrt{2t}\), \(x\sqrt{2} = a/\sqrt{t}\), so \(\operatorname{erfc}(a/\sqrt{2t}) = 2(1 - \Phi(a/\sqrt{t}))\). Assumptions for P3. A0 with continuous paths, and \(a > 0\), for the equality of the events. The assumptions of P2, for the second part. P3. The equality \(\{\tau_a \le t\} = \{M_t \ge a\}\) for every \(t \ge 0\) is the first part of the P1 argument, and it uses continuity, \(B_0 = 0\) and \(a > 0\). For the second part, \(\{\tau_a < \infty\} = \bigcup_{n} \{\tau_a \le n\}\), an increasing union. By continuity of probability from below and P2, \(\Prob(\tau_a < \infty) = \lim_n \Prob(M_n \ge a) = \lim_n \operatorname{erfc}(a/\sqrt{2n}) = \operatorname{erfc}(0) = 1\).
