  20: An option's exercise payoff depends on the spot price on that date. This lesson fixes the call and put payoffs, separates American exercise (at any date up to expiry) from European exercise (at expiry only), and proves two bounds on the price of an American option: it is worth at least its intrinsic value (Definition 2.1 and Proposition 2.2 of Lesson 4), and, at date 0, at least the price of the European option. Lesson 5 uses the payoff identity to derive put–call parity and treats the date-0 comparison of an American and a European call. Lab 1 checks the payoffs and one SIMULATED path.
  21: 1Standing assumptions and the payoffs at expiry
  22: The assumptions below hold throughout Lessons 4 and 5. Each result names the ones it uses.
  23: Standing assumptions (A1 to A5)
  24: A1 (no income). The underlying pays no dividend, coupon or storage cost over the life of the contract.
  25: A2 (no frictions). There are no transaction costs and no bid–ask spread. The underlying and the options can be sold short, and cash can be lent and borrowed at the riskless rate.
  26: A3 (no arbitrage). Let a portfolio have initial value \(V_0 \le 0\) and a payoff \(X\) that is non-negative in every state. Then \(V_0 = 0\) and \(X = 0\) in every state. This is a hypothesis about markets, used in the proofs, not a fact about them.
  27: A4 (constant rate). The riskless rate \(r\) is constant over the life of the contract and compounded continuously. The discount factor from date \(t\) to date \(\tau\) is \(e^{-r(\tau - t)}\).
  28: A5 (European exercise). A European option may be exercised only at its expiry \(\tau\).
  29: Definition 1.1 (call and put payoffs)
  30: Let \(S_t\) be the spot price of the underlying at date \(t\), let \(K > 0\) be the strike, and write \((x)^+ = \max(x, 0)\). A call option with strike \(K\) and expiry \(\tau\) pays \((S_\tau - K)^+\) at \(\tau\). A put option with the same strike and expiry pays \((K - S_\tau)^+\). The call is in the money at a date when \(S_t > K\), and the put when \(S_t < K\). The functions \(s \mapsto (s - K)^+\) and \(s \mapsto (K - s)^+\) are the call and put payoffs.
  31: Holding an option costs a premium, paid at date 0. The profit at expiry of a call bought for premium \(c\) is \((S_\tau - K)^+ - c\). This lesson ignores interest on the premium. That is a convention, stated here so that every profit in Lab 1 is computed the same way, and it is not a result. The break-even spot is the expiry spot at which the profit is zero.
  32: Lemma 1.2 (payoff identity)
  33: For every real \(s\) and every \(K\), \((s - K)^+ - (K - s)^+ = s - K\).
  34: Proof
  35: If \(s \ge K\), the second term is zero and the first term is \(s - K\). If \(s < K\), the first term is zero and the second term is \(K - s\), so the difference is \(-(K - s) = s - K\). \(\blacksquare\)
  36: Remark 1.3 (what the identity says)
  37: A long call and a short put with the same strike and expiry pay \(S_\tau - K\) at expiry, which is the payoff of buying the underlying at the strike \(K\) at expiry. Lesson 5 uses this to derive put–call parity. The identity concerns payoffs. It does not say what the two options cost.
  38: 2American and European exercise
  39: Definition 2.1 (American and European exercise)
  40: Exercise is cash-settled: the holder receives a payment and no share changes hands. The American option with strike \(K\) and expiry \(\tau\) may be exercised at any date \(t\) in \([0, \tau]\) that its holder chooses, and the payment on exercise at \(t\) is \(h(S_t)\), where \(h(s) = (s - K)^+\) for a call and \(h(s) = (K - s)^+\) for a put. A European option with the same strike and expiry is exercised only at \(\tau\), as A5 states. The intrinsic value at date \(t\) is \(h(S_t)\), the payment the holder would receive by exercising at \(t\).
  41: Proposition 2.2 (intrinsic value bound)
  42: Under A2 and A3, the price \(V_A(t)\) of an American option at date \(t\) is at least its intrinsic value: \(V_A(t) \ge h(S_t)\).
  43: Proof
  44: Suppose \(V_A(t) < h(S_t)\). At date \(t\), buy the option for \(V_A(t)\) and exercise it at once, receiving \(h(S_t)\). Both cash flows occur at \(t\), so the position has initial value \(V_A(t) - h(S_t) < 0\) and payoff zero in every state. A3 excludes this, since its initial value is negative and its payoff is non-negative. \(\blacksquare\)
  45: Proposition 2.3 (an American option is worth at least its European counterpart)
  46: Under A2, A3 and A5, for an American option and a European option with the same type, strike and expiry, the prices at date 0 satisfy \(V_A(0) \ge V_E(0)\).
  47: Proof
  48: Suppose \(V_A(0) < V_E(0)\). At date 0 buy the American option for \(V_A(0)\) and sell the European option for \(V_E(0)\). The initial value of this portfolio is \(V_A(0) - V_E(0) < 0\). Keep the American option until \(\tau\), and exercise it at \(\tau\) if and only if it is in the money. It is never exercised before \(\tau\). Its payoff at \(\tau\) is \(h(S_\tau)\), which is the amount the short European option pays. The portfolio's payoff is therefore zero in every state. Its initial value is negative and its payoff is non-negative, which A3 excludes. \(\blacksquare\)
  49: Remark 2.4 (what the inequality does not say)
  50: Proposition 2.3 is an inequality between prices. It does not say which date an American holder should choose, and it does not say that the inequality is strict. Lesson 5 treats the date-0 comparison of an American and a European call. The proof of Proposition 2.3 uses no date other than 0 and \(\tau\), and it does not use A1 or A4.
  51: Example 2.5 (one SIMULATED path, hindsight)
  52: Let the strike be 100 USD, the expiry \(\tau = 4\) years, and let the one-year discount factor be \(e^{-r} = 9/10\), so that \(r = \ln(10/9)\), about 0.10536 per year (A4). Take the SIMULATED path \(S_0, \dots, S_4 = 100, 120, 90, 80, 110\) USD. The call's intrinsic value at dates 0 to 4 is 0, 20, 0, 0 and 10 USD. Holding the call to expiry gives 10 USD at \(\tau\), which is \(10(9/10)^4 = 6.561\) USD at date 0, or 6.56 USD to the cent. Exercising at date 1 gives 20 USD at date 1, which is \(20 \cdot 9/10 = 18\) USD at date 0. On this path, then, exercising at date 1 is worth more than holding to expiry. The put's intrinsic value at dates 0 to 4 is 0, 0, 10, 20 and 0 USD. Its best date on this path is date 3, worth \(20(9/10)^3 = 14.58\) USD at date 0, and holding it to expiry is worth 0.
  53: Remark 2.6 (the example is hindsight, not a rule)
  54: The best dates in Example 2.5 are known after the path is observed. The comparison also leaves out the choice to sell the option at date 1 for its market value, which is at least 20 USD by Proposition 2.2. The example shows which dates are best on one path. It does not show what a holder should do at date 1. Lesson 5 treats the date-0 comparison.

 157: 3Where the canon breaks
 158: The comparisons in Example 2.5 and Lab 1 use the payoff of holding to expiry. A holder who exercises early gives up the option's remaining value, and a holder who sells gets its market price. Proposition 2.2 bounds that price from below by the intrinsic value. Lesson 5 treats model-free bounds on European prices and the date-0 comparison of an American and a European call. Proposition 2.2 gives a lower bound on the American price.
 159: Proposition 2.2 uses A2 and A3, and Proposition 2.3 uses the same two together with A5. Neither uses A1, so both hold whether or not the underlying pays income. The lesson makes no claim about early exercise of an American put or call in that case.
 160: Remark 2.4 states the limit of Proposition 2.3. The inequality holds for both calls and puts, and the lesson shows that the American option is worth at least the European one. It does not show that the two are equal for puts, and it does not show that they differ.
 161: 4Misconceptions
 162: “An American option is always worth more than its European counterpart.”
 163: Proposition 2.3 proves only \(V_A(0) \ge V_E(0)\). This lesson does not claim that the inequality is strict for any put or call. Lesson 5 treats the date-0 comparison.
 164: “A call that is in the money should be exercised at once.”
 165: Exercise gives the intrinsic value \(S_t - K\). Selling the option gives its market value, and by Proposition 2.2 that value is at least \(S_t - K\). Exercising at once is therefore never better than selling at the market price. This lesson does not settle whether holding beats selling at a date after 0. Lesson 5 treats the date-0 comparison.
 166: “The payoff at expiry is the profit.”
 167: The profit subtracts the premium. In Lab 1 the call pays 25.00 USD at a spot of 125 USD, and its profit is 17.00 USD, because the premium is 8.00 USD. The break-even spot of the call is 108 USD, not 100 USD.
 168: “The best exercise date on a path is a trading rule.”
 169: Example 2.5 picks the best date after the path is known. A rule has to choose the date from the prices observed so far, and a rule that exercises on the path's best date needs the future price. Remark 2.6 explains what the example leaves out.

 172: Mechanics
 173: M1 · The payoff and profit of a call
 174: A call has strike 50 USD and premium 4 USD. At expiry the spot is 62 USD. Find the payoff, the profit under the convention of this lesson, and the break-even spot.
 175: Solution
 176: The payoff is \((62 - 50)^+ = 12\) USD. The profit is \(12 - 4 = 8\) USD, with no interest on the premium. The break-even spot is the strike plus the premium, \(50 + 4 = 54\) USD, because the profit is zero there and negative below it.
 177: M2 · The break-even of a put
 178: A put has strike 80 USD and premium 3 USD. Find the break-even spot at expiry, and the profit when the spot at expiry is 60 USD.
 179: Solution
 180: The break-even is the strike minus the premium, \(80 - 3 = 77\) USD. At a spot of 60 USD the payoff is \((80 - 60)^+ = 20\) USD, and the profit is \(20 - 3 = 17\) USD.
 181: M3 · Intrinsic values on the Example 2.5 path
 182: For the path \(100, 120, 90, 80, 110\) USD of Example 2.5, list the call's intrinsic value at each date, and the dates at which exercise gives a positive payment.
 183: Solution
 184: With strike 100 USD, the call's intrinsic values are \((S_t - 100)^+\), which are 0, 20, 0, 0 and 10 USD. Exercise gives a positive payment at date 1, worth 20 USD, and at date 4, worth 10 USD. At date 4 the expiry is reached, so exercise there is the same as holding.
 185: Proof
 186: P1 · The call payoff is non-decreasing
 187: Show that \(s \mapsto (s - K)^+\) is zero for \(s \le K\) and non-decreasing on the whole real line.
 188: Solution
 189: For \(s \le K\), \(s - K \le 0\), so \((s - K)^+ = 0\). For \(s_1 \le s_2\), the function \(x \mapsto \max(x, 0)\) is non-decreasing, and \(s_1 - K \le s_2 - K\), so \((s_1 - K)^+ \le (s_2 - K)^+\). The payoff is therefore non-decreasing, and it is constant at zero for \(s \le K\).
 190: P2 · The intrinsic bound for a put
 191: Under A2 and A3, show that the price of an American put at date \(t\) is at least \((K - S_t)^+\).
 192: Solution
 193: Suppose the put's price \(V(t)\) is less than \((K - S_t)^+\). At date \(t\), buy the put for \(V(t)\) and exercise it at once, receiving \((K - S_t)^+\). The two cash flows occur at \(t\), so the position has initial value \(V(t) - (K - S_t)^+ < 0\) and payoff zero in every state. A3 excludes this, since its initial value is negative and its payoff is non-negative. The contradiction shows \(V(t) \ge (K - S_t)^+\). The argument of Proposition 2.2 with \(h(s) = (K - s)^+\) is the same.
 194: P3 · A chord inequality for the put payoff
 195: Show that \(s \mapsto (K - s)^+\) satisfies \(f(\lambda s_1 + (1 - \lambda)s_2) \le \lambda f(s_1) + (1 - \lambda)f(s_2)\) for all \(s_1, s_2\) on the real line and all \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\), using only that \(f\) is the maximum of two affine functions.
 196: Solution
 197: Write \(f(s) = \max(K - s, 0)\), the maximum of the two affine functions \(a_1(s) = K - s\) and \(a_2(s) = 0\). Fix \(s_1\), \(s_2\) and \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\), and let \(s = \lambda s_1 + (1 - \lambda)s_2\). Each affine piece satisfies \(a_i(s) = \lambda a_i(s_1) + (1 - \lambda)a_i(s_2)\). Since \(a_i \le f\) everywhere, \(a_i(s_1) \le f(s_1)\) and \(a_i(s_2) \le f(s_2)\), so \(a_i(s) \le \lambda f(s_1) + (1 - \lambda)f(s_2)\) for \(i = 1, 2\). The left side is \(f(s) = \max(a_1(s), a_2(s))\), so \(f(s) \le \lambda f(s_1) + (1 - \lambda)f(s_2)\). This is the inequality to be shown.

 203: E2 · Changing the last spot in a copy
 204: In a copy of Lab 1, change the last spot on the path from 110 USD to 90 USD and keep the rest. State the holding value at date 0 of the call and of the put, and the put's best exercise date and its value at date 0.
 205: Solution
 206: At expiry the call pays \((90 - 100)^+ = 0\), so holding it is worth 0.00 USD. The put pays \(100 - 90 = 10\) USD at expiry, which is \(10(9/10)^4 = 6.561\) USD at date 0, or 6.56 USD to the cent. The put's intrinsic values on the path are 0, 0, 10, 20 and 10 USD, so its best date is still 3, worth \(20(9/10)^3 = 14.58\) USD at date 0. The call's best date is still 1, worth 18.00 USD.
 207: E3 · Early exercise of the put on the path
 208: On the Example 2.5 path, is exercising the put at date 2 worth more at date 0 than holding it to expiry? Give both values.
 209: Solution
 210: The put's intrinsic value at date 2 is \(100 - 90 = 10\) USD, worth \(10(9/10)^2 = 8.10\) USD at date 0. Holding the put to expiry pays \((100 - 110)^+ = 0\) at \(\tau = 4\). So on this path exercising at date 2 is worth more, 8.10 USD against 0.00 USD. This is a fact about one path, as Remark 2.6 says.
 211: Research
 212: R1 · The expiry spot at which holding and exercising tie
 213: On the Example 2.5 path, the spot at date 1 is 120 USD. Find the spot \(S_\tau\) at expiry at which the call held to expiry, valued at date 1, equals the exercise value of 20 USD at date 1. Use the discount factor of A4 and the call's payoff.
 214: Solution
 215: Holding to expiry, valued at date 1, gives \((S_\tau - 100)^+ (9/10)^{4 - 1}\). Setting this equal to 20 USD, with \(S_\tau > 100\), gives \(S_\tau - 100 = 20(10/9)^3 = 20000/729\), so \(S_\tau = 100 + 20000/729 = 127.43484\) USD to five decimals. For an expiry spot above this value, holding is worth more at date 1 than exercising, and for a lower value exercising is worth more. The calculation uses only the payoff and the discount factor, and it leaves out the sale of the option, as Remark 2.6 says.
 216: R2 · Monotonicity of the holding value
 217: For a call with strike \(K\), let \(H(s) = (s - K)^+ e^{-r(\tau - t)}\) be the value at date \(t\) of holding it to expiry, as a function of the expiry spot \(s\). Show that \(H\) is non-negative and non-decreasing in \(s\).
 218: Solution
 219: The discount factor \(e^{-r(\tau - t)}\) is positive, so \(H\) has the sign of \((s - K)^+\), which is non-negative. By P1, \((s - K)^+\) is non-decreasing, and multiplying by a positive constant keeps it non-decreasing. So \(H\) is non-negative and non-decreasing in \(s\).
 220: R3 · A path on which holding beats every earlier exercise
 221: Construct a path of spot prices at dates 0 to 4 with strike 100 USD on which the call held to expiry, discounted to date 0 by the one-year factor 9/10 of A4, is worth more than the discounted intrinsic value of exercising at any date before expiry. Say what the construction does and does not show.
 222: Solution
 223: Take \(S_0, \dots, S_4 = 100, 90, 95, 110, 150\) USD. The call's intrinsic values at dates 0 to 3 are 0, 0, 0 and 10 USD. The best of these, exercising at date 3, is worth \(10(9/10)^3 = 7.29\) USD at date 0, with the one-year factor 9/10 of A4. Holding to expiry pays \(150 - 100 = 50\) USD at date 4, worth \(50(9/10)^4 = 32.805\) USD at date 0. The hold value is larger, so the path has the required property. The path shows that exercising early can be worse than holding on this path. It does not show that holding is the better decision in general, because Remark 2.6 applies.
