  20: Put–call parity ties a call, a put, the stock and a loan into one identity. The identity is exact under the standing assumptions, and the same argument gives four bounds on European call and put prices, and a condition of convexity in strike (Definition 3.1 of Lesson 5) on calls. They also treat a Lesson 4 question: under A1 to A5, a non-negative spot at expiry and a non-negative rate, an American call with no income is worth as much as the European call at date 0 (Theorem 4.1), so early exercise adds no value at date 0. Lab 1 checks each identity on a SIMULATED price set and plants a parity error and a convexity error (three violated triples).
  21: 1Put–call parity
  22: Throughout this lesson, A1 to A5 are those of Lesson 4 (the standing assumptions). Let \(C\) and \(P\) be the prices at date 0 of a European call and a European put with the same strike \(K\) and expiry \(\tau\), and let \(S\) be the spot price at date 0. Write \(D = e^{-r\tau}\) for the discount factor from A4.
  23: Definition 1.1 (put–call parity)
  24: Put–call parity for European options with strike \(K\) and expiry \(\tau\) is the identity \(C - P = S - DK\). Its left side compares the two option prices. Its right side is the spot price minus the present value of the strike, with \(D = e^{-r\tau}\) as the discount factor. The identity is the put–call parity relation. The difference \(C - P - S + DK\) is the initial cost of the parity portfolio in the proof of Theorem 1.2, and Lab 1 calls it the parity cost.
  25: Theorem 1.2 (put–call parity)
  26: Under A1, A2, A3, A4 and A5, \(C - P = S - e^{-r\tau}K\) for every strike \(K > 0\).
  27: Proof
  28: Form the portfolio \(X\) at date 0: buy one call, sell one put, sell short one share of the underlying, and lend \(e^{-r\tau}K\). Its initial value is \(V_0 = C - P - S + e^{-r\tau}K\). At \(\tau\), the lent cash returns \(K\) (A4 and A2), the short share costs \(S_\tau\) to return (A1 and A2), and the options pay as in Definition 1.1 of Lesson 4. The payoff of \(X\) is therefore \((S_\tau - K)^+ - (K - S_\tau)^+ - S_\tau + K\), which equals \((S_\tau - K) - S_\tau + K = 0\) by Lemma 1.2 of Lesson 4. The payoff is zero in every state. If \(V_0 < 0\), then \(X\) has initial value at most zero and a non-negative payoff, which A3 excludes. If \(V_0 > 0\), the portfolio \(-X\) has initial value \(-V_0 < 0\) and payoff zero, which A3 excludes in the same way. Hence \(V_0 = 0\), which is the identity. \(\blacksquare\)
  29: Remark 1.3 (what the identity does not use)
  30: The proof uses no distribution for \(S_\tau\). It uses the payoffs of the four positions, the hypotheses A1, A2, A3 and A5 (no income, free short sales and lending, no arbitrage, and European exercise at expiry), and the rate of A4. It holds for every strike, and it does not say that the put and the call are equal at any strike.
  31: 2Four model-free bounds
  32: The bounds below use one more hypothesis, (N): the spot price at expiry is non-negative. It is used only where a payoff must be checked to be non-negative.
  33: Definition 2.1 (model-free option bounds)
  34: The model-free option bounds are inequalities that European call and put prices must satisfy under A1 to A5 and (N), without any model of how the spot price is distributed. Section 3 adds convexity in strike to them.
  35: Theorem 2.2 (model-free bounds)
  36: Under A1, A2, A3, A4, A5 and (N), for every strike \(K > 0\) and expiry \(\tau\):
  37: \(0 \le C \le S\);
  38: \(C \ge \max(S - DK, 0)\);
  39: \(0 \le P \le DK\);
  40: \(P \ge \max(DK - S, 0)\).
  41: Proof
  42: Each part uses one portfolio, with initial value \(V_0\) and payoff \(X\) at \(\tau\), and parts (a) and (c) use a second, simpler portfolio for the lower bound. A3 implies that every portfolio whose payoff is non-negative in every state has initial value at least zero: a negative initial value with a non-negative payoff is excluded. Each part below exhibits such a portfolio and reads off the inequality.
  43: (a) Sell one call and buy one share: \(V_0 = S - C\). Its payoff is \(S_\tau - (S_\tau - K)^+ = \min(S_\tau, K) \ge 0\), using (N) and \(K > 0\). A3 gives \(S - C \ge 0\), so \(C \le S\). Buy one call: \(V_0 = C\) and the payoff is \((S_\tau - K)^+ \ge 0\), so \(C \ge 0\).
  44: (b) Buy one call, sell one share and lend \(DK\): \(V_0 = C - S + DK\). At \(\tau\) the payoff is \((S_\tau - K)^+ - S_\tau + K = (K - S_\tau)^+ \ge 0\), by Lemma 1.2 of Lesson 4. A3 gives \(V_0 \ge 0\), which is \(C \ge S - DK\). Together with \(C \ge 0\) this is \(C \ge \max(S - DK, 0)\).
  45: (c) Sell one put and lend \(DK\): \(V_0 = DK - P\). The payoff is \(K - (K - S_\tau)^+ = \min(S_\tau, K) \ge 0\), using (N). A3 gives \(DK - P \ge 0\). Buy one put: \(V_0 = P\) and the payoff is \((K - S_\tau)^+ \ge 0\), so \(P \ge 0\).
  46: (d) Buy one put, buy one share and borrow \(DK\): \(V_0 = P + S - DK\). The payoff is \((K - S_\tau)^+ + S_\tau - K = (S_\tau - K)^+ \ge 0\), by Lemma 1.2 of Lesson 4. A3 gives \(P + S - DK \ge 0\), which is \(P \ge DK - S\). Together with \(P \ge 0\) this is \(P \ge \max(DK - S, 0)\). \(\blacksquare\)
  47: Remark 2.3 (where each hypothesis is used)
  48: Parts (a) and (c) use (N), because \(\min(S_\tau, K)\) is non-negative only when \(S_\tau \ge 0\). Parts (b) and (d) do not use (N). Every part uses A3 and the payoffs of Lesson 4. A1 is used because the share has no income that the portfolio would have to account for, and A4 because the loan returns \(K\) exactly.
  49: 3Convexity in strike
  50: In this lesson the word convexity refers to convexity in strike. It describes how call prices change across strikes at one expiry and one spot.
  51: Definition 3.1 (convexity in strike)
  52: Take strikes \(K_1 < K_2 < K_3\) and set \(\lambda = (K_3 - K_2)/(K_3 - K_1)\), so that \(0 < \lambda < 1\) and \(K_2 = \lambda K_1 + (1 - \lambda)K_3\). Call prices satisfy convexity in strike at this triple if \(C(K_2) \le \lambda C(K_1) + (1 - \lambda)C(K_3)\). The right side is the chord at \(K_2\): the height at \(K_2\) of the straight line joining the points \((K_1, C(K_1))\) and \((K_3, C(K_3))\). The weight \(\lambda\) depends on the strikes, so the condition is written for any spacing of strikes, not only for equal spacing.
  53: Theorem 3.2 (convexity in strike)
  54: Under A2, A3, A5 and (N), European call prices with the same spot and expiry satisfy convexity in strike at every triple \(K_1 < K_2 < K_3\).
  55: Proof
  56: Form the portfolio \(Z\): buy \(\lambda\) calls with strike \(K_1\), buy \(1 - \lambda\) calls with strike \(K_3\), and sell one call with strike \(K_2\). Z is a butterfly spread: long calls at the two outer strikes and short the call at the middle strike. The payoff is \(g(s) = \lambda(s - K_1)^+ + (1 - \lambda)(s - K_3)^+ - (s - K_2)^+\) at \(s = S_\tau\). We show \(g(s) \ge 0\) for every \(s \ge 0\), by four cases.
  57: (i) If \(s \le K_1\), every term is zero, so \(g(s) = 0\).
  58: (ii) If \(K_1 \le s \le K_2\), only the first term is positive, so \(g(s) = \lambda(s - K_1) \ge 0\).
  59: (iii) If \(K_2 \le s \le K_3\), the second term is zero, so \(g(s) = \lambda(s - K_1) - (s - K_2)\). Substitute \(K_2 = \lambda K_1 + (1 - \lambda)K_3\): \(g(s) = -(1 - \lambda)s + (1 - \lambda)K_3 = (1 - \lambda)(K_3 - s) \ge 0\).
  60: (iv) If \(s \ge K_3\), the three terms are linear: \(g(s) = \lambda(s - K_1) + (1 - \lambda)(s - K_3) - (s - K_2) = K_2 - \lambda K_1 - (1 - \lambda)K_3 = 0\).
  61: So the payoff of \(Z\) is non-negative for every \(s \ge 0\). The payoff at \(\tau\) is \(g(S_\tau)\), and \(S_\tau \ge 0\) by (N). The initial value of \(Z\) is \(V_0 = \lambda C(K_1) + (1 - \lambda)C(K_3) - C(K_2)\), and A3 forbids \(V_0 < 0\). Hence \(V_0 \ge 0\), which is the convexity condition. \(\blacksquare\)
  62: Remark 3.3 (evenly spaced strikes)
  63: When \(K_2 - K_1 = K_3 - K_2\), the weight is \(\lambda = 1/2\), and the condition reads \(C(K_1) - 2C(K_2) + C(K_3) \ge 0\). That is a second difference of call prices on the strike grid. With unequal spacing the weight \(\lambda\) of Definition 3.1 replaces the factor \(1/2\), and the condition must be checked with that weight.

 193: Corollary 3.4 (monotonicity in strike)
 194: Under A2, A3, A5 and (N), \(C(K_2) \le C(K_1)\) whenever \(K_1 < K_2\).
 195: Proof
 196: Buy one call with strike \(K_1\) and sell one call with strike \(K_2\). Its initial value is \(C(K_1) - C(K_2)\). Its payoff is \((s - K_1)^+ - (s - K_2)^+\), which is non-negative for every \(s\), because \(x \mapsto (x)^+\) is non-decreasing and \(s - K_1 \ge s - K_2\). A3 gives \(C(K_1) - C(K_2) \ge 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
 197: 4Early exercise of a call
 198: Theorem 4.1 (an American call on an underlying with no income)
 199: Under A1, A2, A3, A4, A5 and (N), with \(r \ge 0\), an American call and a European call with the same strike and expiry have the same price at date 0: \(V_A(0) = V_E(0)\).
 200: Proof
 201: Proposition 2.3 of Lesson 4 gives \(V_A(0) \ge V_E(0)\) under A2, A3 and A5, so only the reverse inequality needs proof. Suppose \(V_A(0) > V_E(0)\). At date 0, sell one American call for \(V_A(0)\) and buy one European call for \(V_E(0)\). The initial cash received is \(V_A(0) - V_E(0) > 0\).
 202: Consider any path and any date \(t \in [0, \tau]\) at which the buyer of our American call exercises. We pay the exercise value \((S_t - K)^+\), which is the payment on exercise by Definition 2.1 of Lesson 4. At \(t\), we sell our European call at its price \(V_E(t)\). We show \(V_E(t) \ge (S_t - K)^+\) at that same date. Form at \(t\) the portfolio of Theorem 2.2(b): buy one European call for \(V_E(t)\), sell one share for \(S_t\), and lend \(e^{-r(\tau - t)}K\) until \(\tau\). Its initial value is \(V_E(t) - S_t + e^{-r(\tau - t)}K\). By A1, A2 and A4 its payoff at \(\tau\) is \((S_\tau - K)^+ - S_\tau + K = (K - S_\tau)^+ \ge 0\) in every state, so A3 gives \(V_E(t) - S_t + e^{-r(\tau - t)}K \ge 0\). Since \(r \ge 0\) and \(K > 0\), \(e^{-r(\tau - t)}K \le K\), so \(V_E(t) \ge S_t - K\). A European call has a non-negative payoff, so A3 also gives \(V_E(t) \ge 0\). Together these give \(V_E(t) \ge (S_t - K)^+\), and the two cash flows at \(t\) net to \(V_E(t) - (S_t - K)^+ \ge 0\). If the buyer does not exercise before \(\tau\), then at \(\tau\) the buyer's option pays \((S_\tau - K)^+\), and we pay it, while our European call pays \((S_\tau - K)^+\) to us. These cancel, so the net is zero.
 203: Every cash flow of the position is therefore non-negative on every path, and each is received or paid at its own date. Lend each one to \(\tau\) at the riskless rate (A2 and A4), which keeps it non-negative. The final value of the position at \(\tau\) is non-negative in every state, while its initial value is \(V_E(0) - V_A(0) < 0\). A3 excludes this. Hence \(V_A(0) \le V_E(0)\). \(\blacksquare\)
 204: Remark 4.2 (what Theorem 4.1 says)
 205: Under A1 to A5 and (N), with \(r \ge 0\), the price of an American call on an underlying with no income equals the price of the European call at date 0. The theorem is proved at date 0 only. The proof writes out the date-\(t\) bound \(V_E(t) \ge (S_t - K)^+\) that it needs at the exercise date \(t\). It does not prove the equality \(V_A(t) = V_E(t)\) at a date \(t > 0\), and it does not prove Proposition 2.3 at such a date, so that equality is a remark, not a proved result. A holder who exercises at \(t\) receives \((S_t - K)^+\), and the theorem says nothing about the price of the American call at \(t\). The theorem concerns prices. It does not say how the buyer of an American call should behave on each path. It requires \(r \ge 0\): with \(r < 0\) the bound in Theorem 2.2(b) can fall below \(S_t - K\), and the step of the proof fails (Problem R2).
 206: 5Where the canon breaks
 207: The argument for a put does not carry over. Exercising a put at spot \(S_t\) gives \(K - S_t\). The bound of Theorem 2.2(d) for the European put with remaining life \(\tau - t\) is \(e^{-r(\tau - t)}K - S_t\), which is less than \(K - S_t\) whenever \(r > 0\) and \(\tau > t\). For example, with \(K = 100\), \(S_t = 50\), \(\tau - t = 1\) and \(r = 0.10\), the bound is \(100e^{-0.1} - 50 = 40.48\) USD, below the 50 USD that exercise pays now. The bound therefore does not show that holding a put is worth at least its exercise value. This lesson does not decide whether early exercise of an American put is ever optimal.
 208: Theorem 4.1 uses A1, and A1 excludes income on the underlying. With income, the share positions in the portfolios of Sections 1 and 2 would have to account for that income. This lesson does not check the identities that replace them, and it makes no claim about them.
 209: Each result here relies on A2: no transaction costs, no bid–ask spread, and free short sales and loans. A bid–ask spread or a short-sale constraint can stop a position from being available at the quoted prices. In that case the equality of Theorem 1.2 need not hold, and this lesson does not derive what replaces it.
 210: 6Misconceptions
 211: “Put–call parity holds only for at-the-money options.”
 212: Theorem 1.2 states the identity for every strike \(K > 0\), and the proof uses no property of the strike. Lab 1 computes its puts from parity, so its zero residuals hold by construction and are not evidence; the planted put error at 100 USD is detected because it breaks the identity (a parity cost of \(-300\) cents).
 213: “A call priced above the stock is a bargain to buy.”
 214: Theorem 2.2(a) gives \(C \le S\). A call priced above the stock is a sell: sell the call, buy the share, and the initial value \(S - C\) is negative with a non-negative payoff, which A3 excludes under (N).
 215: “A convexity violation means one call is mispriced on its own.”
 216: Convexity in strike is a condition on a triple of strikes. In Lab 1, the planted error at 90 USD produces three violated triples, all with 90 USD as the middle strike, and every one involves the same raised call. The check names the triple, not one price.
 217: “A deep in-the-money call should be exercised early.”
 218: For an underlying with no income, \(r \ge 0\) and (N), Theorem 4.1 gives equal American and European call prices at date 0 only. Proposition 2.2 of Lesson 4 bounds the American price below by the intrinsic value, but this lesson proves no rule for holding against exercising at a later date (Remark 4.2).
 219: 7Problem set
 220: Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.
 221: Mechanics
 222: M1 · Parity for a put
 223: A call with strike 105 USD costs 8 USD. The spot is 100 USD, and \(D = 0.97\). Find the put price with the same strike and expiry.
 224: Solution
 225: Parity gives \(P = C - S + DK = 8 - 100 + 0.97 \times 105 = 8 - 100 + 101.85 = 9.85\) USD.
 226: M2 · A call below its lower bound
 227: Spot is 50 USD, the strike is 40 USD, \(D = 0.95\), and the call costs 3 USD. Show that the price breaks Theorem 2.2(b), and describe the position that makes money from it.
 228: Solution
 229: The lower bound is \(\max(S - DK, 0) = \max(50 - 38, 0) = 12\) USD, and \(3 < 12\), so the bound is broken. The position is: buy the call for 3 USD, sell the share for 50 USD, and lend \(DK = 38\) USD. Its initial value is \(3 - 50 + 38 = -9\) USD, so the trader receives 9 USD now. At expiry the payoff is \((K - S_\tau)^+\), which is non-negative, so A3 is violated.
 230: M3 · Convexity on an evenly spaced grid
 231: Calls with strikes 90, 100 and 110 USD cost 15, 9 and 4 USD at one spot and expiry. Check convexity in strike at this triple.
 232: Solution
 233: The strikes are evenly spaced, so \(\lambda = 1/2\). The chord value is \((15 + 4)/2 = 9.5\) USD, and \(9 \le 9.5\), so the condition holds. Equivalently, the second difference is \(15 - 2 \times 9 + 4 = 1 \ge 0\).
 234: Proof
 235: P1 · The weight reproduces the middle strike
 236: For \(K_1 < K_2 < K_3\) and \(\lambda = (K_3 - K_2)/(K_3 - K_1)\), show that \(\lambda K_1 + (1 - \lambda)K_3 = K_2\), and that \(0 < \lambda < 1\).
 237: Solution
 238: We have \(\lambda K_1 + (1 - \lambda)K_3 = \dfrac{(K_3 - K_2)K_1 + (K_2 - K_1)K_3}{K_3 - K_1}\). The numerator is \(K_3K_1 - K_2K_1 + K_2K_3 - K_1K_3 = K_2(K_3 - K_1)\), so the expression equals \(K_2\). Since \(K_1 < K_2 < K_3\), both \(K_3 - K_2\) and \(K_2 - K_1\) are positive, so \(0 < \lambda < 1\).
 239: P2 · The upper bound on a put
 240: Under A1 to A5 and (N), prove \(P \le DK\) using a portfolio and A3.
 241: Solution
 242: Sell one put and lend \(DK\). The initial value is \(DK - P\). At expiry the put pays \((K - S_\tau)^+\) and the loan returns \(K\), so the payoff is \(K - (K - S_\tau)^+\). By Lemma 1.2 of Lesson 4, \(K - (K - S_\tau)^+ = \min(S_\tau, K)\), which is non-negative by (N) and \(K > 0\). If \(DK - P < 0\), A3 is violated, so \(DK - P \ge 0\), which is \(P \le DK\).
 243: P3 · Parity carries convexity to puts
 244: Under the hypotheses of Theorem 3.2 and Theorem 1.2, show that put prices also satisfy convexity in strike at every triple \(K_1 < K_2 < K_3\).
 245: Solution
 246: By parity, \(P(K) = C(K) - S + DK\). Let \(\lambda\) be the weight of Definition 3.1. Then \(P(K_2) - [\lambda P(K_1) + (1 - \lambda)P(K_3)] = [C(K_2) - \lambda C(K_1) - (1 - \lambda)C(K_3)] + D[K_2 - \lambda K_1 - (1 - \lambda)K_3] - S(1 - \lambda - (1 - \lambda))\). The last bracket of the \(S\) terms is zero, and by P1 the bracket with \(D\) is zero. The remaining term is the convexity gap of the calls, \(C(K_2) - \lambda C(K_1) - (1 - \lambda)C(K_3)\), which is at most zero by Theorem 3.2. So \(P(K_2) \le \lambda P(K_1) + (1 - \lambda)P(K_3)\).
 247: Empirical
 248: E1 · Second differences on the lab's calls
 249: From the synthetic calls printed in Lab 1 (3000, 2000, 1000, 100 and 0 cents at 80, 90, 100, 110 and 120 USD), compute the evenly spaced second difference at 90, 100 and 110 USD. Say which is zero and which is largest.
 250: Solution
 251: At 90 USD, \(3000 - 2 \times 2000 + 1000 = 0\) cents. At 100 USD, \(2000 - 2 \times 1000 + 100 = 100\) cents. At 110 USD, \(1000 - 2 \times 100 + 0 = 800\) cents. The second difference at 90 USD is zero, so the chord is tight there, and the largest is at 110 USD, at 800 cents, or 8.00 USD. All three are non-negative, as Theorem 3.2 requires.
 252: E2 · Reading the planted convexity error
 253: In Lab 1's second planted error, how many triples are violated, and which strike is the middle one in each? Explain why the lower strike of each violated triple is 80 USD.
 254: Solution
 255: Three triples are violated, and in each the middle strike is 90 USD. The lower strike must be 80 USD, the only strike below 90 USD on the grid, and the upper strike is 100, 110 or 120 USD. The printed lines show these three triples.
 256: E3 · Which bound binds at 120 USD
 257: In the synthetic set, the put at 120 USD is 8.00 USD. Compare it with the bound of Theorem 2.2(d). Which bound binds, and what is the bound's value?
 258: Solution
 259: The bound is \(DK - S = 0.9 \times 120 - 100 = 8\) USD, so the put equals its lower bound. The call at 120 USD is 0.00 USD, which equals the bound \(\max(S - DK, 0) = \max(100 - 108, 0) = 0\) in Theorem 2.2(b). So both the put bound and the call bound hold with equality at this strike, and the lab's parity identity is consistent with both.
 260: Research
 261: R1 · Why the payoff check cannot see a parity error
 262: Explain why the lab's payoff check over 40 pairs finds nothing for the planted parity error, while the cost check finds \(-300\) cents. Identify which quantity a parity error changes.
 263: Solution
 264: The payoff of the parity portfolio at expiry is \((S_\tau - K)^+ - (K - S_\tau)^+ - S_\tau + K\), which does not contain any price at date 0. A change to the put price therefore cannot change the payoff. It changes the initial value \(V_0 = C - P - S + DK\), by the same amount. The cost check reports \(V_0\) directly, which is why it finds \(-300\) cents. The payoff check tests the identity that the portfolio is riskless, and the cost check tests the identity that it costs nothing. Parity needs both.
 265: R2 · The early-exercise step fails for a negative rate
 266: Take \(r = -0.1\), \(K = 100\) USD, \(\tau - t = 1\) and \(S_t = 120\) USD. Compute the bound of Theorem 2.2(b) for the European call, and compare it with the intrinsic value \(S_t - K\). Say which step of Theorem 4.1 this breaks.
 267: Solution
 268: Here \(D = e^{0.1} = 1.10517\), so the bound is \(120 - 1.10517 \times 100 = 120 - 110.517 = 9.48\) USD, to two decimals. The intrinsic value is \(120 - 100 = 20\) USD. The bound lies below the intrinsic value, so the inequality \(V_E(t) \ge S_t - K\) cannot be derived from it. That is the step of Theorem 4.1 that uses \(r \ge 0\). A negative rate does not give a contradiction, and the theorem makes no claim for \(r < 0\).
 269: R3 · Monotonicity of call prices in strike
 270: Corollary 3.4 states that call prices do not rise with strike. Explain which portfolio proves it, and say what it does not show about the size of the fall.
 271: Solution
 272: The portfolio buys the call at \(K_1\) and sells the call at \(K_2 > K_1\). Its payoff is non-negative in every state, and A3 gives \(C(K_1) \ge C(K_2)\). The corollary does not give a size for the fall. It shows that the prices do not rise with strike.
