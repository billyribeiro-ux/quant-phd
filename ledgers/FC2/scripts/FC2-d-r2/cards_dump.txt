1 FC2-c001 L01 | What is an arbitrage opportunity in the one-period market of Lesson 1? || A portfolio θ with cost p·θ ≤ 0, payoff (Xθ)_s ≥ 0 in every state s, and either p·θ < 0 or (Xθ)_s > 0 for some state s (Definition 1.1). A zero-cost portfolio with zero payoff in every state is not one.
2 FC2-c002 L01 | What is a set of state prices? || A vector ψ with ψ_s > 0 for every state s and p_j = Σ_s ψ_s X_sj for every asset j, that is, p = Xᵀψ (Definition 3.1).
3 FC2-c003 L01 | What does Assumption A3 (no arbitrage) say, and is it a fact about markets? || No portfolio is an arbitrage opportunity in the market under study. It is a hypothesis, not a fact the lesson establishes, and a price set can violate it (Section 1; Lab 1, Part B).
4 FC2-c004 L01 | Under A3, do two portfolios with the same payoff in every state have the same cost? || Yes, under A3. If Xa = Xb then p·a = p·b (Proposition 2.1). The proof applies A3 to the portfolio a − b, whose payoff is zero.
5 FC2-c005 L01 | Under A3, if portfolio a pays at least as much as b in every state, how do their costs compare? || p·a ≥ p·b (Proposition 2.2). If a pays strictly more in every state, then p·a > p·b. Both results use A3 and nothing else (Remark 2.3).
6 FC2-c006 L01 | Do strictly positive state prices exclude arbitrage, and does this need A3? || Yes. A market with strictly positive state prices has no arbitrage opportunity, and A3 is not assumed (Proposition 3.2). The proof writes p·θ = Σ_s ψ_s (Xθ)_s, a sum of non-negative terms.
7 FC2-c007 L01 | Does no arbitrage imply that strictly positive state prices exist? || This lesson does not claim it. Proposition 3.2 runs one way, and Remark 3.3 states that the converse is not claimed. Uniqueness of the state prices is shown only for the market of Example 3.4.
8 FC2-c008 L01 | In the market of Example 3.4 at stock price 1, what are the state prices? || ψ_u = 1/20 and ψ_d = 17/20. Both are positive and reproduce both prices, so the market has no arbitrage (Example 3.4; Lab 1, Part A).
9 FC2-c009 L01 | With the bond at 9/10 and the payoffs of Example 3.4, for which stock prices q is there no arbitrage? || Exactly for 9/10 < q < 27/10 (Proposition 3.5). At q = 9/10 and at q = 27/10 there is arbitrage, although the state-price formulas give zero there (Remark 3.6).
10 FC2-c010 L01 | Do state prices add up to one, as probabilities do? || No. In the market of Example 3.4 at stock price 1, the state prices 1/20 and 17/20 add to 9/10, the price of the bond that pays 1 in every state (Problem P3). They carry discounting as well as likelihood.
11 FC2-c011 L01 | Does a portfolio that makes money in some state count as an arbitrage? || No. Definition 1.1 needs cost at most zero and payoff non-negative in every state. One stock and two short bonds at prices 1 and 9/10 cost −4/5 and pay (1, −1), so the portfolio can lose in state d.
12 FC2-c012 L01 | In Lab 1, Part B, which stock price is used with the Part A payoffs, and does that price set have arbitrage portfolios in the box of holdings from −10 to 10? || The stock is priced at 1/2 with the Part A payoffs. That price set has arbitrage portfolios in the box, so it violates A3 (Lab 1, Part B).
13 FC2-c013 L01 | Which zero-cost portfolio does Lab 1, Part B find, and what does it pay? || Holdings (bond, stock) = (−5, 9): cost 0, payoff 22 in state u and 4 in state d (Lab 1, Part B).
14 FC2-c014 L01 | Does a zero count of arbitrage portfolios in a search box prove that a market has no arbitrage? || No. The search covers holdings from −10 to 10 of each asset, so the count is a fact about that box. Proposition 3.2 gives the all-holdings result from the positive state prices that Part A prints (Problem E3).
15 FC2-c015 L01 | Does Proposition 2.2 order the costs of two portfolios whose payoffs are higher in one state and lower in another? || No. It applies only when one payoff is at least the other in every state. For a pair whose payoffs cross, these results determine no cost order (Remark 2.3).
16 FC2-c016 L02 | What does a forward contract pay at expiry, and what does it cost at date 0? || The long party pays the forward price F and receives one unit, so its payoff at expiry is S_τ − F. The short party's payoff is F − S_τ (Definition 1.1).
17 FC2-c017 L02 | What discount factor from date τ back to date 0 does A4 give? || e^{−rτ}. A4 fixes one rate r over the life of the contract, continuously compounded, so an amount x at date 0 becomes x e^{rt} at date t (Assumption A4).
18 FC2-c018 L02 | What is the cost-of-carry forward price? || F* = S e^{rτ}: the spot price S compounded at rate r to expiry τ (Definition 2.1).
19 FC2-c019 L02 | Under A1 to A4, what forward price does no arbitrage require? || F = F* = S e^{rτ} (Proposition 2.2). If F > F*, sell the forward and buy the asset with a loan; if F < F*, buy the forward, sell the asset short and lend. Each gives a zero-cost portfolio with a strictly positive payoff in every state, which A3 excludes.
20 FC2-c020 L02 | Does the no-arbitrage forward price depend on the expected spot price at expiry? || No. The proof uses no probability and no expectation of S_τ, so F* is a function of S, r and τ alone (Remark 2.3).
21 FC2-c021 L02 | An asset costs 50 dollars, r = 0.05 continuously compounded, and τ = 1/2. What is the no-arbitrage forward price? || 50e^{0.025} = 51.2658 dollars, which is 51.27 dollars to the cent (Example 2.4). Lab 2, Part A prints 5126.58 cents.
22 FC2-c022 L02 | Is a forward priced above today's spot overpriced when r > 0? || Not necessarily. For r > 0 and τ > 0, F* = S e^{rτ} exceeds S, and that excess is the cost of carry. Only a price different from F* is a mispricing (Remark 2.5).
23 FC2-c023 L02 | In Lab 2, Part B, does the forward at the fair price pay what the replicating holding pays in each spot outcome? || Yes. For spots at expiry of 3000, 4500, 6000 and 7500 cents, both payoffs are −2126.58, −626.58, 873.42 and 2373.42 cents (to two decimals), and they agree to 1e-6 cents.
24 FC2-c024 L02 | In Lab 2, Part C, what does selling a forward quoted at 5200 cents and buying the asset with a loan earn? || 73.42 cents in every expiry state, with zero net cash today. The payoff equals the quote minus the fair price (Lab 2, Part C; Solution E2).
25 FC2-c025 L02 | What is the value of a long forward with price F held at date t? || V_t = S_t − F e^{−r(τ − t)}, under A1 to A4 and A3 at each date t (Proposition 3.1). The proof buys the unit and borrows F e^{−r(τ − t)}.
26 FC2-c026 L02 | What is the value at inception of a forward priced at F*, and what sign does it take later? || Zero at date 0. At date t its value has the sign of S_t − F* e^{−r(τ − t)} (Corollary 3.2), under A1 to A4 and A3 at each date t.
27 FC2-c027 L02 | In Lab 2, Part E, what is the value to the long side of a forward struck at the fair price, after a quarter year with the spot at 5300 cents? || 237.11 cents (Lab 2, Part E): the spot 5300 minus the discounted fair price, computed from the unrounded fair price.
28 FC2-c028 L02 | If the asset pays one known income I at date t1 and A1 is dropped, what is the no-arbitrage forward price? || F = S e^{rτ} − I e^{r(τ − t1)} = (S − I e^{−r t1}) e^{rτ}, under A2 to A4 and A3, with 0 < t1 ≤ τ (Problem P1).
29 FC2-c029 L02 | Under A3, can two forward contracts on one asset with one expiry have different prices? || No. Long the contract with the lower price and short the one with the higher price gives a payoff F2 − F1 > 0 in every state at zero cost, which A3 excludes (Problem P3).
30 FC2-c030 L02 | With a lending rate r_ℓ at most a borrowing rate r_b, which forward prices admit arbitrage? || Under A1 and A3, with A4 at the two rates in place of r, and the frictionless parts of A2 (no transaction costs, free short sales, unrestricted lending and borrowing, and no default risk), a price F outside [S e^{r_ℓ τ}, S e^{r_b τ}] admits arbitrage when r_ℓ ≤ r_b (Problem R1). The lesson does not prove that every price inside the interval is arbitrage-free.
31 FC2-c032 L02 | Does a buyer of a forward pay a premium at inception? || No. Definition 1.1 makes no payment at date 0. The buyer pays the forward price F at expiry, in exchange for the unit.
32 FC2-c033 L03 | How does a futures contract differ from the forward contract of Lesson 2? || A futures contract is standardized and traded on an exchange, where a clearing house stands between the parties. Gains and losses are paid between the parties every day, not only at expiry (Definition 1.1).
33 FC2-c034 L03 | What is the daily variation margin of a long futures holder? || Δ_i = F_i − F_{i−1} per unit, where F_i is the close on day i and F_0 is the entry price. A short holder receives −Δ_i. Crediting these amounts each day is marking to market (Definition 1.2).
34 FC2-c035 L03 | What does Assumption A8′ assume about the margin account? || The margin account earns no interest, and the daily variation margins are the only cash flows of the position (Assumption A8′).
35 FC2-c036 L03 | Under A8′, what does the margin balance equal, and what do the daily margins sum to? || The balance after day i is F_i − F_0, and the daily margins over days 1 to n sum to F_n − F_0 (Proposition 1.3). The proof is a telescoping sum and needs no model of how prices move.
36 FC2-c037 L03 | Does the margining identity show that the account can meet margin requirements? || No. Margin requirements are not modelled, and the identity holds whatever the requirements are (Remark 1.4).
37 FC2-c038 L03 | In Lab 3, Part A, what is the margin balance after day 10 on the SIMULATED path? || 84 cents, which equals the settlement 5084 minus the initial price 5000 (Lab 3, Part A; the path is seeded and SIMULATED).
38 FC2-c039 L03 | What are the two legs of an interest-rate swap, and what does the payer of the fixed leg pay and receive? || The fixed leg pays R per unit of notional at each date i. The floating leg pays the coupon L_i for period i, set at date i − 1. The payer pays R and receives L_i (Definition 3.1).
39 FC2-c040 L03 | What does the single-curve assumption A8 say about the floating coupon? || One set of discount factors both discounts the payments and sets the floating coupons, so 1 + L_i = DF_{i-1} / DF_i, and every DF_i is strictly positive for i = 1 to n (Assumption A8).
40 FC2-c041 L03 | Under A8, what is the present value of the floating leg per unit of notional? || 1 − DF_n (Proposition 3.2). The coupon at date i has present value DF_{i−1} − DF_i, and the sum telescopes because DF_0 = 1.
41 FC2-c042 L03 | Under A8, what is the par rate of a swap? || R_par = (1 − DF_n) / Σ_{i=1}^{n} DF_i, the unique fixed rate at which the swap has zero value at date 0 (Proposition 3.3).
42 FC2-c043 L03 | On a flat curve with DF_i = (1 + ρ)^{−i}, what is the par rate? || The flat rate ρ (Example 3.4).
43 FC2-c044 L03 | What is the value to the fixed payer at a fixed rate K per unit of notional? || (R_par − K) Σ_i DF_i under A8. It is positive when K < R_par, zero when K = R_par, and negative when K > R_par (Proposition 3.5).
44 FC2-c045 L03 | In Lab 3, Part B, what is the par rate of the swap? || The par rate is 0.049026, which is 4.9026 percent (Lab 3, Part B).
45 FC2-c046 L03 | On the 1,000,000-dollar notional of Lab 3, Part B, what is the value to the payer of a swap struck one percentage point above the par rate, in cents? || −h Σ_i DF_i with h one percentage point, which the lab prints as −4415468 cents (Problem P2; Lab 3, Part B).
46 FC2-c047 L03 | If the margin account earns a constant rate ρ per day, what is the balance after n days? || B_n = Σ_{i=1}^{n} Δ_i (1 + ρ)^{n−i} (Problem P3). For n ≥ 2 it equals F_n − F_0 for every price path only when ρ = 0, so A8′ is needed for Proposition 1.3.
47 FC2-c048 L03 | Does a futures position have the same cash flows as a forward with the same expiry price? || The totals at expiry can match, but the timing differs. Under A8′ the futures margins sum to F_n − F_0 (Proposition 1.3), while a forward pays once, at expiry (Definition 1.1 of Lesson 2).
48 FC2-c049 L03 | Does the futures buyer pay the futures price up front? || Not in this model. The position costs nothing to enter, and the only cash flows are the daily variation margins. The collateral a clearing house requires is outside the model (Remark 1.4).
49 FC2-c050 L03 | Is the par rate of a swap the market's interest rate? || No. Under A8, R_par is computed from all the discount factors (Proposition 3.3), so it is a weighted summary of the whole curve and equals none of the typed-in zero rates (Lab 3, Part B).
50 FC2-c051 L04 | What are the payoffs of a call and a put at expiry, with strike K? || The call pays (S_τ − K)^+ and the put pays (K − S_τ)^+, where x^+ = max(x, 0) (Definition 1.1). The call is in the money when S_t > K, and the put when S_t < K.
51 FC2-c052 L04 | What does the payoff identity (s − K)^+ − (K − s)^+ equal? || s − K for every real s (Lemma 1.2). A long call and a short put with the same strike and expiry therefore pay S_τ − K, the payoff of a long forward (Remark 1.3).
52 FC2-c053 L04 | What is the difference between American and European exercise? || An American option may be exercised at any date t in [0, τ] for its intrinsic value h(S_t). A European option may be exercised only at expiry τ, as A5 states (Definition 2.1).
53 FC2-c054 L04 | Under A2 and A3, how does the price of an American option compare with its intrinsic value? || V_A(t) ≥ h(S_t) at every date t (Proposition 2.2).
54 FC2-c055 L04 | Under A2, A3 and A5, is an American option at date 0 worth at least the European option with the same terms? || Yes: V_A(0) ≥ V_E(0) (Proposition 2.3). The proof gives only this inequality, and Remark 2.4 does not claim that it is strict.
55 FC2-c056 L04 | On the SIMULATED path 100, 120, 90, 80, 110 USD, with strike 100 USD, expiry after four years and a one-year discount factor of 9/10, what is the date-0 value of holding the call to expiry? || 10(9/10)^4 = 6.561 USD, which is 6.56 USD to the cent (Example 2.5; Lab 1, Part 2).
56 FC2-c057 L04 | On the Example 2.5 path, what is the put's best exercise date, and what is it worth at date 0? || Date 3, worth 20(9/10)^3 = 14.58 USD at date 0. Holding the put to expiry is worth 0 on this path (Example 2.5; Lab 1, Part 2).
57 FC2-c058 L04 | What are the call and put break-even spots at expiry for the premiums of Lab 1? || Call 108 USD, the strike plus the 8 USD premium. Put 93 USD, the strike minus the 7 USD premium (Lab 1, Part 1).
58 FC2-c059 L04 | Is an American option always worth more than its European counterpart? || No. Under A2, A3 and A5, Proposition 2.3 proves only the weak inequality V_A(0) ≥ V_E(0), and the lesson does not claim that it is strict for any put or call (Remark 2.4).
59 FC2-c060 L04 | Should a call that is in the money be exercised at once? || Not on this lesson's argument. Under A2 and A3, exercise gives S_t − K, while selling the option gives its market value, which is at least S_t − K by Proposition 2.2. Exercising at once is therefore never better than selling at the market price.
60 FC2-c061 L04 | Is the payoff at expiry the profit? || No. The profit subtracts the premium. In Lab 1 the call pays 25.00 USD at a spot of 125 USD, but its profit is 17.00 USD after the 8.00 USD premium (Lab 1, Part 1).
61 FC2-c062 L04 | Is the best exercise date on a price path a trading rule? || No. Example 2.5 picks the best date after the path is known. A rule must choose the date from the prices observed so far (Remark 2.6).
62 FC2-c063 L05 | What is the put-call parity identity for European options with strike K and expiry τ? || C − P = S − DK, with D = e^{−rτ} the discount factor (Definition 1.1).
63 FC2-c064 L05 | Under A1 to A5, does put-call parity hold only at the money, or for every strike? || Every strike, not only the at-the-money one: C − P = S − e^{−rτ}K for every strike K > 0 (Theorem 1.2). The proof uses the four payoffs, A1 and A2 (no income, free short sales and lending), A3, and the rate of A4. It uses no distribution for S_τ (Remark 1.3).
64 FC2-c065 L05 | What are the model-free option bounds? || Inequalities that European call and put prices must satisfy under A1 to A5 and (N), where (N) says the spot is non-negative at expiry. They use no model of how the spot is distributed (Definition 2.1).
65 FC2-c066 L05 | Under A1 to A5 and (N), what bounds do European call and put prices satisfy? || 0 ≤ C ≤ S; C ≥ max(S − DK, 0); 0 ≤ P ≤ DK; P ≥ max(DK − S, 0) (Theorem 2.2).
66 FC2-c067 L05 | Which of the four model-free bounds use (N)? || Parts (a) and (c) use (N), because min(S_τ, K) is non-negative only when S_τ ≥ 0. Parts (b) and (d) do not use (N) (Remark 2.3).
67 FC2-c068 L05 | In this lesson, what is convexity in strike, and what is the weight λ? || For strikes K1 < K2 < K3, with λ = (K3 − K2)/(K3 − K1), call prices satisfy convexity in strike at the triple if C(K2) ≤ λ C(K1) + (1 − λ) C(K3) (Definition 3.1). The word refers to strikes only.
68 FC2-c069 L05 | Under A2, A3, A5 and (N), are European call prices convex in strike? || Yes, at every triple K1 < K2 < K3 with the same spot and expiry (Theorem 3.2). The proof is a payoff argument on the butterfly position, checked in four cases.
69 FC2-c070 L05 | Under A2, A3, A5 and (N), do call prices rise with strike? || No, they do not rise: C(K2) ≤ C(K1) whenever K1 < K2 (Corollary 3.4). The corollary gives no size for the fall.
70 FC2-c071 L05 | Under A1 to A5, (N) and r ≥ 0, what is the date-0 price of an American call on an underlying with no income? || The same as the European call: V_A(0) = V_E(0) (Theorem 4.1). The theorem is proved at date 0 only. The equality V_A(t) = V_E(t) at a later date t is a remark, not a proved result (Remark 4.2).
71 FC2-c072 L05 | On the synthetic price set of Lab 1, what do the parity and bound checks print? || The parity cost is zero at each of the five strikes, 80 to 120 USD. The parity payoff is zero at all 40 (strike, expiry spot) pairs, and no model-free bound or convexity triple is violated (Lab 1).
72 FC2-c073 L05 | What does a put raised by 300 cents at 100 USD do to the parity cost and to the payoff check in Lab 1? || The parity cost at that strike shows −300 cents. The payoff check finds nothing, because the payoff at expiry does not depend on the put price (Lab 1; Research R1).
73 FC2-c074 L05 | When a call and a put at 90 USD are both raised by 500 cents in Lab 1, does put-call parity still hold? || Yes. The parity cost stays zero at every strike, because the call and the put rise by the same amount (Lab 1).
74 FC2-c075 L05 | What are the evenly spaced second differences of the Lab 1 calls at 90, 100 and 110 USD? || 0, 100 and 800 cents. All three are non-negative as Theorem 3.2 requires, and the one at 90 USD is zero (Problem E1).
75 FC2-c076 L05 | Does the early-exercise bound for a put show that holding a put is worth at least its exercise value? || No. With K = 100, S_t = 50, one year left and r = 0.10, the put's bound is 100e^{−0.1} − 50 = 40.48 USD, below the 50 USD that exercise pays now (Section 5).
76 FC2-c078 L05 | Is a call priced above the stock a bargain to buy? || No. Under A1 to A5 and (N), C ≤ S (Theorem 2.2(a)). Selling the call and buying the share has initial value S − C < 0 and payoff min(S_τ, K) ≥ 0 (uses (N)), which A3 excludes.
77 FC2-c079 L05 | Does a convexity violation show that one call is mispriced on its own? || No. Convexity in strike is a condition on a triple of strikes. In Lab 1 the planted error at 90 USD produces three violated triples, all sharing the same raised call (Lab 1).
78 FC2-c080 L05 | Does Theorem 4.1 still give equal American and European call prices when the rate r is negative? || The theorem makes no claim for r < 0. With r < 0 the bound in Theorem 2.2(b) can fall below S_t − K, so the step of the proof that uses r ≥ 0 fails (Remark 4.2; Problem R2).
79 FC2-c081 L06 | What are the gross riskless return and the value today of a sure payoff of 1 at the end of the period? || R_f = 1 + r_1, where r_1 is the one-period rate, and a sure payoff of 1 is worth 1/R_f today (Definition 1.1). The unsubscripted r of A4 is not used in this lesson.
80 FC2-c082 L06 | What is the one-period binomial model, and what does A6 require? || The stock is worth S_0 today and uS_0 or dS_0 at the end of the period. A6 requires 0 < d < R_f < u. The call pays max(uS_0 − K, 0) after an up move and max(dS_0 − K, 0) after a down move (Definition 1.2).
81 FC2-c083 L06 | What does a replicating portfolio hold, and what equations does it satisfy? || Δ shares and a bond position B, where B > 0 is cash lent and B < 0 is cash borrowed. It satisfies Δ uS_0 + R_f B = V_1(u) and Δ dS_0 + R_f B = V_1(d). Its cost today is V_0 = Δ S_0 + B (Definition 1.3).
82 FC2-c084 L06 | Under A1, A2, A3, A5 and A6, is the one-period replicating portfolio unique, and what is the price? || It is unique, because u ≠ d and S_0 > 0. Its cost is V_0 = (q V_1(u) + (1 − q) V_1(d)) / R_f with q = (R_f − d)/(u − d), and 0 < q < 1 (Theorem 1.4(a) and (b)).
83 FC2-c085 L06 | Under A1, A2, A3, A5 and A6, what happens if a claim trades at a price π different from the replicating cost V_0 in the one-period binomial model? || The price is not the replicating cost, so a zero-cost portfolio with a strictly positive payoff in every state exists, which A3 excludes; under A3 the price is V_0. If π > V_0, sell the claim, buy the replicating portfolio and lend the surplus; the total is (π − V_0) R_f > 0 in both states at zero cost. If π < V_0, reverse the positions (Theorem 1.4(c); Example 1.6).
84 FC2-c086 L06 | In Example 1.5 (S_0 = K = 100, u = 3/2, d = 1/2, R_f = 5/4), what are the replicating shares and the bond position? || Δ = 1/2 share and B = −20 (borrowing 20) (Example 1.5).
85 FC2-c087 L06 | Does the real-world probability of an up move enter the one-period price? || No. Under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.4, the formula in Theorem 1.4(b) uses u, d, R_f and the two payoffs. Two models with the same u, d and R_f but different up probabilities give the same V_0 (Remark 1.7).
86 FC2-c088 L06 | What is the risk-neutral probability of an up move in the one-period binomial model? || q = (R_f − d)/(u − d), with Q(up) = q and Q(down) = 1 − q. Expectations under Q are written E^Q (Definition 2.1).
87 FC2-c089 L06 | Under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.4, how is the one-period price written as an expectation? || V_0 = E^Q[V_1] / R_f, the discounted expectation of the payoff under Q (Theorem 2.2).
88 FC2-c090 L06 | What is a recombining tree? || A tree in which an up move followed by a down move reaches the same node as a down move followed by an up move. Node (n, k) has stock price S(n, k) = S_0 u^k d^{n−k}, so there are n + 1 nodes at time n, not 2^n (Definition 3.1).
89 FC2-c091 L06 | Is the risk-neutral probability q the probability that the stock goes up? || No. In Example 1.5, q = 3/4, while the stock's up factor is u = 3/2. q is the weight that makes the discounted expected payoff equal the replicating cost (Theorem 2.2; Definition 2.1).
90 FC2-c092 L06 | What is backward induction on the recombining tree? || Set V(N, k) = f(S(N, k)) at expiry, and for n < N set V(n, k) = (q V(n+1, k+1) + (1 − q) V(n+1, k)) / R_f, with S(n, k) = S_0 u^k d^{n−k} (Definitions 3.1 and 3.2).
91 FC2-c093 L06 | Under A1, A2, A3, A5, A6 and A6′, what is the multi-period price, and what is the closed form at the root? || The price at node (n, k) is V(n, k) (Theorem 3.3). At the root, V(0,0) = R_f^{−N} Σ_k C(N, k) q^k (1 − q)^{N−k} f(S_0 u^k d^{N−k}).
