  50| 
  51|       A forward contract (Definition 1.1) fixes today the price at which an asset will change hands later. Its price is not a forecast. Under stated conditions, a forward price other than the cost-of-carry price admits an arbitrage. The cost-of-carry price (Definition 2.1) is the spot price, compounded at the rate for the time to expiry. This lesson defines the forward contract and its payoff, states four assumptions, and proves the no-arbitrage forward price by replication, that is, by a portfolio of the asset and a loan with the same payoff. It then values a forward during its life. The lab checks the replication and the mispricing argument on typed-in prices in cents.
  52| 
  53|       1The forward contract and its assumptions
  54| 
  55|       Let \(S\) be the price today, at date 0, of one unit of an asset, and let \(\tau > 0\) be the time to expiry in years. Let \(S_\tau\) be the asset's price at date \(\tau\), which is not known today. The symbol \(\tau\) denotes time to expiry, because \(T\) is reserved in this course for the number of observations in a sample.
  56| 
  57| 
  58|         Definition 1.1 (forward contract)
  59|         A forward contract on the asset, with expiry \(\tau\) and forward price \(F\), is an agreement made at date 0 under which, at date \(\tau\), the long party pays \(F\) and receives one unit of the asset, and the short party delivers one unit and receives \(F\). No payment is made at date 0. The long party's payoff at expiry is \(S_\tau - F\), and the short party's is \(F - S_\tau\).
  60| 
  61| 
  62|       The price \(F\) is fixed when the contract is made. Whether the payoff is a gain or a loss depends on \(S_\tau\), which is not known at date 0. The contract is an exchange at a fixed price, and Definition 1.1 requires no payment at date 0.
  63| 
  64| 
  65|         Assumption A1 (no income)
  66|         The asset pays no income, such as a dividend or a coupon, and bears no storage cost, between dates 0 and \(\tau\).
  67| 
  68| 
  69| 
  70|         Assumption A2 (frictionless trading)
  71|         There are no transaction costs, no bid–ask spreads and no short-sale restrictions. An owner may sell the asset short and may lend or borrow any amount at a rate \(r\), which Assumption A4 of this lesson fixes as constant and continuously compounded, with no default risk.
  72| 
  73| 
  74| 
  75|         Assumption A3 (no arbitrage)
  76|         No portfolio is an arbitrage opportunity in the one-period market of Lesson 1, in the sense of Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1, for the dates indicated: dates 0 and \(\tau\) for Proposition 2.2, and dates \(t\) and \(\tau\) for Proposition 3.1.
  77| 
  78| 
  79| 
  80|         Assumption A4 (constant continuously compounded rate)
  81|         The rate \(r\) is the same at every date between 0 and \(\tau\), and it is continuously compounded. An amount \(x\) at date 0 becomes \(x e^{rt}\) at date \(t\), and the discount factor from date \(\tau\) back to date 0 is \(e^{-r\tau}\).
  82| 
  83| 
  84|       2The no-arbitrage forward price
  85| 
  86| 
  87|         Definition 2.1 (cost-of-carry forward price)
  88|         For an asset with price \(S\) today, a rate \(r\) and an expiry \(\tau\), the cost-of-carry forward price is \(F^\ast = S e^{r\tau}\). It is the amount that the spot price \(S\) reaches at date \(\tau\) when it is invested at rate \(r\).
  89| 
  90| 
  91| 
  92|         Proposition 2.2 (the no-arbitrage forward price)
  93|         Assume A1 to A4. If a forward contract on the asset with expiry \(\tau\) has forward price \(F\), and the market has no arbitrage opportunity, then \(F = F^\ast = S e^{r\tau}\).
  94| 
  95| 
  96| 
  97|         Proof
  98|         Consider two cases. Each one constructs a portfolio whose cost at date 0 is zero and whose payoff at date \(\tau\) is the same in every state.
  99|         Case 1, \(F > S e^{r\tau}\). At date 0, sell the forward at \(F\), which costs nothing by Definition 1.1. Buy one unit of the asset for \(S\), and pay for it with a loan of \(S\) at rate \(r\). The net cash flow at date 0 is \(S - S = 0\). At date \(\tau\), deliver the unit to the forward buyer and receive \(F\), and repay the loan with \(S e^{r\tau}\). The unit is delivered under the contract, so its market price \(S_\tau\) plays no part. The net payoff is \(F - S e^{r\tau}\) in every state, which is strictly positive. This is an arbitrage opportunity, which A3 excludes. So \(F \le F^\ast\).
 100|         Case 2, \(F < S e^{r\tau}\). At date 0, buy the forward at \(F\), which costs nothing. Sell one unit of the asset short for \(S\), and lend the proceeds at rate \(r\). A2 allows both. The net cash flow at date 0 is \(-S + S = 0\). At date \(\tau\), pay \(F\) and receive the unit, which closes the short position, so its market price \(S_\tau\) plays no part. The loan returns \(S e^{r\tau}\). The net payoff is \(S e^{r\tau} - F\) in every state, which is strictly positive. This is an arbitrage opportunity, which A3 excludes. So \(F \ge F^\ast\).
 101|         Together the two cases give \(F = F^\ast\). \(\blacksquare\)
 102| 
 103| 
 104| 
 105|         Remark 2.3 (what the price does not depend on)
 106|         The proof uses no probability of the states and no expectation of \(S_\tau\). The forward price depends only on \(S\), \(r\) and \(\tau\). An observer who expects the asset to rise, or to fall, is offered the forward price \(F^\ast\) all the same, because any other price admits an arbitrage that does not depend on the expectation.
 107| 
 108| 
 109| 
 110|         Example 2.4 (one number)
 111|         An asset priced at 50 dollars today, with \(r = 0.05\) and \(\tau = 1/2\), has \(F^\ast = 50 e^{0.025} = 51.2658\) dollars, which is 51.27 dollars to the cent. Lab 2, Part A, prints the same value in cents, 5126.58, which is 51.2658 dollars.
 112| 
 113| 
 114| 
 115|         Remark 2.5 (a price above spot is not an overpricing)
 116|         Because \(e^{r\tau} > 1\) when \(r > 0\) and \(\tau > 0\), the no-arbitrage forward price exceeds the spot price in Example 2.4. That excess is the cost of carry, which is the cost of financing the asset until expiry under A1 and A4. It is not a mispricing.

 120|         Lab 2 · The forward price, its replication and a mispriced quote
 121|         Predicted before running. Part A prints the fair price 5126.58 cents for a spot price of 5000 cents, rate 0.05 and expiry 0.5 years. Part B finds that the forward payoff equals the replicating payoff in each of four spot outcomes, with a gap below 1e-6 cents. Part C finds that a quote of 5200 cents, which is above the fair price, earns 73.42 cents in every state. Its three date-0 cash legs, a zero forward payment, minus 5000 cents for the asset and plus 5000 cents for the loan, sum to zero by construction. Part D finds that the fair quote earns nothing. Part E values a fair forward after a quarter year at 237.11 cents when the spot is 5300 cents.
 122|         """FC2 lab L02: the no-arbitrage forward price S e^(r tau), its replication, and a mispriced quote.
 123| 
 124| The spot price, rate, expiry, quote and states are typed in for this lab; they describe no market. The conventions
 125| are those of the lesson: the asset pays no income, the rate r is continuously compounded and constant, and
 126| borrowing, lending and short sales are allowed at that rate. Typed-in prices are integer cents; formula prices are
 127| floating-point values in cents. Formula values are computed in floating point and printed to two decimals of a cent,
 128| so a printed cent value is a display rounding of an unrounded value. The replication check is printed as a boolean
 129| against a tolerance of 1e-6 cents.
 130| Standard library only; no network; no randomness.
 131| """
 132| 
 133| from __future__ import annotations
 134| 
 135| import math
 136| 
 137| SPOT_CENTS = 5000
 138| RATE = 0.05
 139| TAU = 0.5
 140| QUOTE_CENTS = 5200
 141| EXPIRY_STATES_CENTS = [3000, 4500, 6000, 7500]
 142| TOLERANCE_CENTS = 1e-6
 143| 
 144| 
 145| def forward_price_cents(spot_cents: int, rate: float, tau: float) -> float:
 146|     """No-arbitrage forward price in cents: the spot price times e to the power rate times tau."""
 147|     return spot_cents * math.exp(rate * tau)
 148| 
 149| 
 150| def replicating_payoff_cents(spot_at_expiry: int, spot_cents: int, rate: float, tau: float) -> float:
 151|     """Payoff at expiry of holding one unit bought at spot_cents and borrowing spot_cents at rate for tau years."""
 152|     return spot_at_expiry - spot_cents * math.exp(rate * tau)
 153| 
 154| 
 155| def long_forward_payoff_cents(spot_at_expiry: int, price_cents: float) -> float:
 156|     """Payoff at expiry of a long forward contract agreed at price_cents."""
 157|     return spot_at_expiry - price_cents
 158| 
 159| 
 160| def forward_value_cents(spot_now: float, price_cents: float, rate: float, years_left: float) -> float:
 161|     """Value today of a long forward agreed at price_cents: spot minus price_cents discounted over years_left."""
 162|     return spot_now - price_cents * math.exp(-rate * years_left)
 163|

 169| 
 170|     print()

 175|         replica = replicating_payoff_cents(s, SPOT_CENTS, RATE, TAU)
 176|         worst = max(worst, abs(forward_leg - replica))
 177|         print(f"spot at expiry {s}: forward payoff {forward_leg:.2f}, replicating portfolio {replica:.2f}")
 178|     print("forward payoff equals replicating payoff in every state, to 1e-6 cents:", worst < TOLERANCE_CENTS)
 179| 
 180|     print()
 181|     print("C. A forward quoted above its fair price: sell the forward, buy the asset with a loan")
 182|     forward_today = 0  # a forward costs nothing at date 0 (Definition 1.1)
 183|     asset_today = -SPOT_CENTS  # the asset is bought at the spot price
 184|     loan_today = SPOT_CENTS  # the loan is taken for the same amount
 185|     print("date-0 cash legs, in cents (forward, asset, loan):", forward_today, asset_today, loan_today)
 186|     print("net cash today, in cents, zero by construction:", forward_today + asset_today + loan_today)
 187|     gains = []
 188|     for s in EXPIRY_STATES_CENTS:
 189|         net = (QUOTE_CENTS - s) + (s - SPOT_CENTS * math.exp(RATE * TAU))
 190|         gains.append(net)
 191|         print(f"spot at expiry {s}: net payoff {net:.2f} cents")

 193|     print("net payoff is the same in every state, to 1e-6 cents:", same)
 194|     print(f"that payoff equals quote minus fair price, in cents: {QUOTE_CENTS - fair:.2f}")
 195| 
 196|     print()
 197|     print("D. Fair quote: the same strategy earns nothing")
 198|     at_fair = max(abs((fair - s) + (s - SPOT_CENTS * math.exp(RATE * TAU))) for s in EXPIRY_STATES_CENTS)
 199|     print("net payoff at the fair quote is zero in every state, to 1e-6 cents:", at_fair < TOLERANCE_CENTS)
 200| 
 201|     print()
 202|     print("E. Value of a fair forward after 0.25 years, for the long side")
 203|     value_now = forward_value_cents(5300, fair, RATE, TAU - 0.25)
 204|     print(f"spot 5300 cents, years left 0.25: value {value_now:.2f} cents")
 205|     print(
 206|         "value at inception of a forward struck at the fair price is zero:",

 241|       3The value of a forward during its life
 242| 
 243|       Define the value of a long forward at date \(t\), with \(0 \le t \le \tau\), as the date-\(t\) cost of a portfolio whose payoff at date \(\tau\) equals the forward's payoff \(S_\tau - F\). Under A3 at date \(t\), every such portfolio has the same cost, by Proposition 2.1 of Lesson 1 applied from date \(t\) to date \(\tau\). Proposition 3.1 computes that cost.
 244| 
 245| 
 246|         Proposition 3.1 (value during life)
 247|         Assume A1 to A4, and assume A3 at each date \(t\) between 0 and \(\tau\). Let a long forward with price \(F\) and expiry \(\tau\) be held at date \(t\), when the asset's price is \(S_t\). Its value at date \(t\) is \(V_t = S_t - F e^{-r(\tau - t)}\).
 248| 
 249| 
 250| 
 251|         Proof
 252|         At date \(t\), buy one unit of the asset for \(S_t\), and borrow \(F e^{-r(\tau - t)}\) at rate \(r\). The net cost at date \(t\) is \(S_t - F e^{-r(\tau - t)} = V_t\). At date \(\tau\) the unit is worth \(S_\tau\), and the loan is repaid as \(F e^{-r(\tau - t)} e^{r(\tau - t)} = F\). The net payoff is \(S_\tau - F\) in every state, which is the payoff of the long forward. Two portfolios with the same payoff at date \(\tau\) have the same value at date \(t\) by Proposition 2.1 of Lesson 1, applied from date \(t\) to date \(\tau\), with A3 at date \(t\). So the forward's value is \(V_t\). \(\blacksquare\)
 253| 
 254| 
 255| 
 256|         Corollary 3.2 (zero value at inception)
 257|         Under the hypotheses of Proposition 3.1 and with \(F = F^\ast\), the value of the forward at date 0 is zero, and its value at any date \(t\) has the sign of \(S_t - F^\ast e^{-r(\tau - t)}\).
 258| 
 259| 
 260| 
 261|         Proof
 262|         At \(t = 0\), Proposition 3.1 gives \(V_0 = S - F^\ast e^{-r\tau} = S - S e^{r\tau} e^{-r\tau} = S - S = 0\). For the second claim, \(V_t = S_t - F^\ast e^{-r(\tau - t)}\) by Proposition 3.1 with \(F = F^\ast\), and the sign statement is the definition of the sign of that difference. \(\blacksquare\)
 263| 
 264| 
 265| 
 266|         Remark 3.3 (zero at inception is not zero later)
 267|         A forward that is fair at inception has zero value at inception and a value that moves with the spot afterwards. Lab 2, Part E, shows one such value. Lesson 3 treats futures contracts, which are settled each day, and does not carry this valuation over to them.
 268| 
 269| 
 270|       4Where the canon breaks
 271| 
 272| 
 273|       The fair price is exact inside A1 to A4, and each assumption is used in the proof of Proposition 2.2.
 274| 
 275|         A1 excludes income and storage. A dividend, a coupon or a storage cost changes the replicating portfolio. Problem P1 derives the price with one known income payment. This lesson does not derive the price for a continuous yield, for storage, or for any income whose size is not known in advance.
 276|         A2 assumes frictionless trading. Transaction costs, bid–ask spreads and limits on short sales are not modelled. Problem R1 shows that a price outside the band \([S e^{r_\ell \tau}, S e^{r_b \tau}]\) admits arbitrage when borrowing and lending rates differ. This lesson does not derive the band for any other friction.
 277|         A4 fixes one rate. The rate is an input, and the lesson does not say how to choose it. The forward price is sensitive to it: the cost of carry grows with \(r\) and \(\tau\) when \(r > 0\) and \(\tau > 0\) (Problem P2).
 278|         No default. A2 assumes no default risk on the loan and deposit at rate \(r\). It does not say that the forward's counterparties perform; a forward with credit risk is not modelled here.

 283|         5Misconceptions
 284| 
 285| 
 286|             “The forward price is the spot price expected at expiry.”
 287|             Proposition 2.2 gives \(F = S e^{r\tau}\), which uses the spot price today and the rate. It does not involve any expectation of \(S_\tau\). Remark 2.3 states that the forward price is the same whatever is expected. Lab 2, Part B, checks one forward price, which Part A prints, against four different outcomes for the spot at expiry.
 288| 
 289| 
 290|             “Entering a forward costs money, so the buyer pays a premium.”
 291|             Definition 1.1 makes no payment at date 0. The buyer pays the forward price \(F\) at expiry, in exchange for the unit. A forward has zero value at inception when it is priced at \(F^\ast\), by Corollary 3.2.
 292| 
 293| 
 294|             “A forward priced above today's spot is overpriced.”
 295|             When \(r > 0\) and \(\tau > 0\), the fair price \(F^\ast = S e^{r\tau}\) is above \(S\), as Remark 2.5 explains. A price above spot is the cost of carry. The mispricing that Proposition 2.2 identifies is a price different from \(F^\ast\) in either direction, and Lab 2, Part C, shows one above it.

 307|             M1 · The fair forward price
 308|             An asset costs 50 dollars today. The rate is 5 percent continuously compounded, and the expiry is six months, with A1 holding. Find the no-arbitrage forward price to the cent.
 309|             Solution
 310|               By Proposition 2.2, \(F^\ast = 50 e^{0.05 \times 0.5} = 50 e^{0.025}\). The exponential is \(e^{0.025} = 1.0253151205\) to ten places, so \(F^\ast = 50 \times 1.0253151205 = 51.265756\) dollars, which is 51.27 dollars to the cent. The displayed factor reproduces the displayed product. Example 2.4 gives the same value, and Lab 2, Part A, prints 5126.58 cents.
 311| 
 312| 
 313| 
 314|             M2 · Profit from a quote
 315|             An asset costs 80 dollars today. The rate is 4 percent continuously compounded, and the expiry is three months. A dealer quotes a forward at 81 dollars. Find the profit per unit to the cent of selling that forward and replicating it, as in Proposition 2.2.
 316|             Solution
 317|               The fair price is \(F^\ast = 80 e^{0.04 \times 0.25} = 80 e^{0.01}\). Since \(e^{0.01} = 1.0100501671\) to ten places, \(F^\ast = 80 \times 1.0100501671 = 80.804013\) dollars, so the quote exceeds it by \(81 - 80.804013 = 0.195987\) dollars. The profit is that difference, which is 0.20 dollars to the cent, in every state at expiry, with no cash needed today.

 321|             M3 · Value after half the life
 322|             A long forward was struck at 100 dollars with one year to expiry, and the rate is 3 percent continuously compounded. After half a year the asset is worth 104 dollars. Find the forward's value to the cent, using Proposition 3.1.
 323|             Solution
 324|               The value is \(V_t = S_t - F e^{-r(\tau - t)}\), here \(104 - 100 e^{-0.03 \times 0.5} = 104 - 100 e^{-0.015}\). Since \(e^{-0.015} = 0.985112\) to six places, \(100 e^{-0.015} = 98.5112\) dollars, and the value is \(104 - 98.5112 = 5.4888\) dollars, which is 5.49 dollars to the cent.

 332|             P1 · One known income payment
 333|             Drop A1 and suppose the asset pays a known income \(I\) at date \(t_1\), with \(0 < t_1 \le \tau\), and nothing else. Keep A2 to A4. Show that the no-arbitrage forward price is \(F = S e^{r\tau} - I e^{r(\tau - t_1)}\), and write it in the form \((S - I e^{-rt_1}) e^{r\tau}\).
 334|             Solution
 335|               Buy the asset for \(S\) with a loan of \(S\). At date \(t_1\) the income \(I\) arrives, and it is lent at rate \(r\) until date \(\tau\), where it is worth \(I e^{r(\tau - t_1)}\). At date \(\tau\) the position holds the unit, worth \(S_\tau\), and the lent income, and owes \(S e^{r\tau}\). Sell the forward short at \(F\), so that the unit is delivered and \(F\) is received. The net payoff is \(F - S e^{r\tau} + I e^{r(\tau - t_1)}\), which is zero in every state when \(F\) equals the stated price. The argument of Proposition 2.2 with this payoff gives \(F = S e^{r\tau} - I e^{r(\tau - t_1)}\). Factoring \(e^{r\tau}\) gives \((S - I e^{-rt_1}) e^{r\tau}\), since \(I e^{r(\tau - t_1)} = I e^{r\tau} e^{-rt_1}\).
 336| 
 337| 
 338| 
 339|             P2 · Monotonicity of the cost of carry
 340|             Show that for \(S > 0\), \(F^\ast\) is strictly increasing in \(r\) and in \(\tau\) when \(\tau > 0\) and \(r > 0\), and that it is strictly increasing in \(S\).
 341|             Solution
 342|               The partial derivative of \(S e^{r\tau}\) with respect to \(r\) is \(S\tau e^{r\tau}\), which is positive when \(S > 0\) and \(\tau > 0\). The partial derivative with respect to \(\tau\) is \(Sr e^{r\tau}\), which is positive when \(r > 0\). The derivative with respect to \(S\) is \(e^{r\tau} > 0\). A function with a positive derivative on an interval is strictly increasing there, so each claim holds on the stated domain.
 343| 
 344| 
 345| 
 346|             P3 · Two forwards with different prices
 347|             Two forward contracts on the same asset have the same expiry \(\tau\), and their prices satisfy \(F_1 < F_2\). Under A3, show that the portfolio that is long the first contract and short the second has a strictly positive payoff in every state, and deduce that the two prices cannot both be in force.
 348|             Solution
 349|               The long first contract pays \(S_\tau - F_1\), and the short second contract pays \(F_2 - S_\tau\). Their sum is \(F_2 - F_1 > 0\) in every state, because the asset's price cancels. Both contracts cost nothing to enter by Definition 1.1, so the portfolio has zero cost and a strictly positive payoff in every state. It is an arbitrage opportunity, which A3 excludes. The two prices cannot both be in force.

 364|             E2 · Reading the 73.42 in Lab 2, Part C
 365|             Lab 2, Part C, prints a net payoff of 73.42 cents in each of four spot outcomes. Explain where the number comes from and why it does not depend on the outcome.
 366|             Solution
 367|               The quote is 5200 cents and the fair price is 5126.58 cents, so the quote exceeds the fair price by 73.42 cents. The sold forward pays the quote minus the spot at expiry. The replicating holding pays the spot at expiry minus the loan repayment of 5126.58 cents. The spot cancels, and the sum is the quote minus the fair price in every state.

 371|             E3 · The value in Lab 2, Part E
 372|             Lab 2, Part E, prints a value of 237.11 cents for a long forward struck at the fair price, after a quarter year, with the spot at 5300 cents. Explain which inputs determine the value and check the printed number from them.
 373|             Solution
 374|               By Proposition 3.1 the value is \(S_t - F e^{-r(\tau - t)}\). Here \(S_t = 5300\), \(r = 0.05\) and \(\tau - t = 0.25\). The fair price is \(F = 5000 e^{0.025} = 5126.5756\) cents, unrounded, which Lab 2, Part A, displays as 5126.58. With \(e^{-0.0125} = 0.98757780\) to eight places, the discounted price is \(F e^{-0.0125} = 5062.8923\) cents, so the value is \(5300 - 5062.8923 = 237.1077\) cents, which is 237.11 to the cent. The value is positive because the spot exceeds the discounted fair price.

 382|             R1 · Borrowing and lending at different rates
 383|             Drop the assumption that borrowing and lending occur at one rate. Let the lending rate be \(r_\ell\) and the borrowing rate \(r_b\), with \(r_\ell \le r_b\), keep A1 and A3, keep from A2 the no-transaction-cost, short-sale, unrestricted lending and borrowing, and no-default parts, and keep A4 with \(r\) replaced by these rates. Show that a forward price is free of arbitrage only if it lies in the interval \([S e^{r_\ell \tau},\ S e^{r_b \tau}]\), and that a price outside the interval gives an arbitrage.
 384|             Solution
 385|               If \(F > S e^{r_b \tau}\), sell the forward and buy the unit with a loan at rate \(r_b\). The net payoff at expiry is \(F - S e^{r_b \tau} > 0\) in every state and the cost today is zero, so this is an arbitrage. If \(F < S e^{r_\ell \tau}\), buy the forward and sell the unit short, lending the proceeds at \(r_\ell\). The net payoff is \(S e^{r_\ell \tau} - F > 0\) in every state, with zero cost, so this is an arbitrage. Hence a price outside the interval gives an arbitrage. The interval is non-empty because \(r_\ell \le r_b\). When \(r_\ell = r_b\) the interval collapses to the single point \(F^\ast\). The lesson does not prove that every price inside the interval is arbitrage-free.

 389|             R2 · What the forward price says about the future
 390|             Show that \(F^\ast\) is a function of \(S\), \(r\) and \(\tau\) alone. Then state what a student can conclude, and what the student cannot conclude, from the observation that the forward price exceeds today's spot.
 391|             Solution
 392|               Proposition 2.2 and Remark 2.3 show that the price is \(S e^{r\tau}\) and that the proof uses no distribution of \(S_\tau\). So the forward price is a function of \(S\), \(r\) and \(\tau\) alone. When \(r > 0\) and \(\tau > 0\), the excess of \(F^\ast\) over \(S\) is the cost of carry, which Remark 2.5 explains. The student can conclude that the excess is consistent with the cost of carry. The student cannot conclude that the market expects the spot to rise, because \(F^\ast\) does not depend on any expectation.

 396|             R3 · Annual compounding
 397|             Suppose the rate is compounded once a year, so that an amount \(x\) becomes \(x(1 + r)^t\) after an integer number of years \(t\). With \(\tau\) an integer, show that the no-arbitrage forward price is \(S(1 + r)^\tau\). Compare it with \(S e^{r\tau}\) for \(S = 1\), \(r = 0.05\) and \(\tau = 2\).
 398|             Solution
 399|               The replication argument of Proposition 2.2 is unchanged except that the loan grows by \((1 + r)^\tau\). Case 1 gives the net payoff \(F - S(1 + r)^\tau\), and Case 2 gives \(S(1 + r)^\tau - F\), so the no-arbitrage price is \(S(1 + r)^\tau\). For \(S = 1\), \(r = 0.05\) and \(\tau = 2\), the annual price is \(1.05^2 = 1.1025\). The continuous price is \(e^{0.1} = 1.105171\) to six places, so the two conventions differ by 0.0027 per unit of the spot. The unrounded difference is \(e^{0.1} - 1.1025 = 0.0026709\), which is 0.0027 to four places. The convention is therefore part of the price, and it must be stated.
