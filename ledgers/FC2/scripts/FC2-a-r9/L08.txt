  51|       A quoted option price can be read as a volatility. Under the Black–Scholes formula of Lesson 7, each volatility gives one price. This lesson shows when a price can be read back as a volatility, and when that volatility is unique, and gives a bisection (Definition 3.1, in Section 3) that finds it to a stated tolerance. A price outside the model-free bounds of Lesson 5 has no volatility under the formula, and the lesson names that condition. The lab checks the inversion on prices the formula itself produces, so it tests the code and the formula, not any quote.
  52| 
  53|       1Definition and setting
  54| 
  55|       Fix the spot \(S > 0\), the strike \(K > 0\), the rate \(r\) and the maturity \(\tau > 0\). Assumptions A1 to A5, A7 and A7′ of Lesson 7 are in force. Write \(C(\sigma)\) for the Black–Scholes closed form of Lesson 7, Definition 1.3, which A7′ of Lesson 7 takes as the call value under A7, as a function of the volatility \(\sigma > 0\), with the other inputs held fixed. The closed form is attributed to Black and Scholes (1973), registry key black1973pricing, and this lesson does not check the paper's text. The lower bound of Lesson 5 for a European call, with the same inputs, is
  56|       \[
  57|         L = \max\big(S - Ke^{-r\tau},\ 0\big).
  58|       \]
  59| 
  60| 
  61|         Definition 1.1 (Implied volatility)
  62|         Let \(C^\ast\) be an observed price of the call. The implied volatility is the number \(\sigma^\ast > 0\), if one exists, with \(C(\sigma^\ast) = C^\ast\). It is defined from the price, the inputs \(S\), \(K\), \(r\), \(\tau\) and the model. It does not use the stock's history.
  63| 
  64| 
  65|       2Monotonicity and the range of prices
  66| 
  67| 
  68|         Lemma 2.1 (Derivative of the price in volatility)
  69|         For \(\sigma > 0\), the derivative \(dC/d\sigma\) equals \(S\,\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{\tau}\), which is positive. Here \(\varphi\) is the standard normal density of Lesson 7.
  70| 
  71|       Proof
  72|         Let \(a = \ln(S/K) + r\tau\) and \(s = \sigma\sqrt{\tau}\). Then \(d_1 = a/s + s/2\) and \(d_2 = a/s - s/2\). Since \(\partial s/\partial\sigma = \sqrt{\tau}\),
  73|         \[
  74|           \frac{\partial d_1}{\partial\sigma} - \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial\sigma}
  75|           = \Big(-\frac{a\sqrt{\tau}}{s^2} + \frac{\sqrt{\tau}}{2}\Big) - \Big(-\frac{a\sqrt{\tau}}{s^2} - \frac{\sqrt{\tau}}{2}\Big) = \sqrt{\tau}.
  76|         \]
  77|         Next, \(S\varphi(d_1) = Ke^{-r\tau}\varphi(d_2)\). Take logarithms of both sides, using \(\varphi(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}\): the difference is \(\ln(S/K) + r\tau - (d_1^2 - d_2^2)/2\). Since \(d_1 - d_2 = s\) and \(d_1 + d_2 = 2a/s\), \((d_1^2 - d_2^2)/2 = a\), so the difference is zero.
  78|         Now, using \(\Phi' = \varphi\),
  79|         \[
  80|           \frac{dC}{d\sigma} = S\varphi(d_1)\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial\sigma} - Ke^{-r\tau}\varphi(d_2)\frac{\partial d_2}{\partial\sigma}
  81|           = S\varphi(d_1)\Big(\frac{\partial d_1}{\partial\sigma} - \frac{\partial d_2}{\partial\sigma}\Big) = S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{\tau}.
  82|         \]
  83|         This is positive because \(S > 0\), \(\tau > 0\) and \(\varphi > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
  84| 
  85| 
  86| 
  87|         Lemma 2.2 (Limits in volatility)
  88|         \(\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma \to \infty} C(\sigma) = S\) and \(\displaystyle\lim_{\sigma \to 0^+} C(\sigma) = L\).
  89| 
  90|       Proof
  91|         As \(\sigma \to \infty\), \(a/s \to 0\), so \(d_1 \to +\infty\) and \(d_2 \to -\infty\). Then \(\Phi(d_1) \to 1\) and \(\Phi(d_2) \to 0\), and \(C(\sigma) \to S\).
  92|         As \(\sigma \to 0^+\), \(s \to 0\), and there are three cases. If \(a > 0\), then \(a/s \to +\infty\), so \(d_1, d_2 \to +\infty\), \(C \to S - Ke^{-r\tau}\), which equals \(L\) because \(a > 0\) means \(S > Ke^{-r\tau}\). If \(a < 0\), then \(d_1, d_2 \to -\infty\), \(C \to 0 = L\). If \(a = 0\), then \(d_1 = s/2 \to 0\) and \(d_2 = -s/2 \to 0\), so \(\Phi(d_1), \Phi(d_2) \to 1/2\), and \(C \to S/2 - Ke^{-r\tau}/2 = 0 = L\), because \(a = 0\) gives \(S = Ke^{-r\tau}\). \(\blacksquare\)
  93| 
  94| 
  95| 
  96|         Theorem 2.3 (Existence and uniqueness of the implied volatility)
  97|         For \(C^\ast\) given, there is exactly one \(\sigma^\ast > 0\) with \(C(\sigma^\ast) = C^\ast\) if \(L < C^\ast < S\), and there is none if \(C^\ast \le L\) or \(C^\ast \ge S\).
  98| 
  99|       Proof
 100|         Let \(\sigma > 0\). Strict monotonicity gives \(C(\sigma/2) < C(\sigma)\), and \(C(\sigma/2^k)\) decreases to \(L\) by Lemma 2.2, so \(L \le C(\sigma/2) < C(\sigma)\). Likewise \(C(\sigma) < C(2\sigma) \le S\), because \(C(2^k\sigma)\) increases to \(S\). So \(L < C(\sigma) < S\), and no \(\sigma > 0\) solves \(C(\sigma) = C^\ast\) when \(C^\ast \le L\) or \(C^\ast \ge S\).
 101|         Let \(L < C^\ast < S\). By Lemma 2.2 there are \(\sigma_1\) with \(C(\sigma_1) < C^\ast\) and \(\sigma_2\) with \(C(\sigma_2) > C^\ast\). Lemma 2.1 makes \(C\) differentiable, hence continuous, on \((0,\infty)\), and \(\sigma_1 < \sigma_2\) because \(C(\sigma_1) < C^\ast < C(\sigma_2)\) and \(C\) is strictly increasing. The intermediate value theorem then gives a root in \([\sigma_1, \sigma_2]\). Strict monotonicity makes it the only root. \(\blacksquare\)
 102| 
 103| 
 104|       Theorem 2.3 is the answer to the question this lesson asks. A price strictly between the lower bound and the spot has one volatility. A price on or outside those bounds has none, so the implied volatility is not defined for it.
 105| 
 106|       3Bisection to a stated tolerance
 107| 
 108| 
 109|         Definition 3.1 (Bisection bracket and step)
 110|         Let \(\sigma_{\text{lo}} < \sigma_{\text{hi}}\) be a bracket with \(C(\sigma_{\text{lo}}) < C^\ast\) and \(C(\sigma_{\text{hi}}) \ge C^\ast\), and let \(\tau_{\text{tol}} > 0\) be a tolerance. While \(\sigma_{\text{hi}} - \sigma_{\text{lo}} > \tau_{\text{tol}}\), repeat: set \(m = (\sigma_{\text{lo}} + \sigma_{\text{hi}})/2\); if \(C(m) < C^\ast\), set \(\sigma_{\text{lo}} = m\), and otherwise set \(\sigma_{\text{hi}} = m\). When the loop ends, report \(\hat\sigma = (\sigma_{\text{lo}} + \sigma_{\text{hi}})/2\). The test is made before each step, so a bracket already within \(\tau_{\text{tol}}\) takes no step. The tolerance is written \(\tau_{\text{tol}}\) to keep \(\tau\) for maturity.
 111| 
 112| 
 113| 
 114|         Proposition 3.2 (Bisection error and step count)
 115|         Let \(\sigma_{\text{lo},0} < \sigma_{\text{hi},0}\) satisfy the bracket condition of Definition 3.1 with \(C^\ast\) in \((L, S)\), and let \(W = \sigma_{\text{hi},0} - \sigma_{\text{lo},0}\). Then the procedure stops after the smallest \(k\) with \(W/2^k \le \tau_{\text{tol}}\), which is \(\lceil \log_2(W/\tau_{\text{tol}}) \rceil\) when \(W > \tau_{\text{tol}}\), and \(|\hat\sigma - \sigma^\ast| \le \tau_{\text{tol}}/2\).
 116| 
 117|       Proof
 118|         By Theorem 2.3, \(\sigma^\ast\) is unique. Because \(C\) is strictly increasing, \(C(\sigma) < C^\ast\) for \(\sigma < \sigma^\ast\) and \(C(\sigma) \ge C^\ast\) for \(\sigma \ge \sigma^\ast\). The invariant \(\sigma_{\text{lo}} < \sigma^\ast \le \sigma_{\text{hi}}\) holds at the start, and each step keeps it: if \(C(m) < C^\ast\) then \(m < \sigma^\ast\), and if \(C(m) \ge C^\ast\) then \(m \ge \sigma^\ast\). Each step halves the width, so after \(k\) steps the width is \(W/2^k\), and the procedure stops at the first \(k\) with \(W/2^k \le \tau_{\text{tol}}\). Then \(\sigma^\ast\) lies in the final bracket, whose width is at most \(\tau_{\text{tol}}\), and the midpoint is within half the width of \(\sigma^\ast\). \(\blacksquare\)

Its code checks the bracket and raises a named error when the bracket does not contain the root. At the upper end this happens when the implied volatility is above 10, which is a price too close to the spot: at strike 100, the price 99.99995 lies above \(C(10) = 99.999944\) (the lab's call function at volatility 10, not printed), so the check rejects it, although a volatility above 10 solves it (Theorem 2.3).  124|         Lab 1 · Implied volatility by bisection, with the outside-the-bounds check
 125|         Stated before running: for spot 100, \(r = 0.05\), \(\tau = 1\), and strikes 80, 100 and 120 with volatilities 0.10, 0.20 and 0.40, the bisection with tolerance \(10^{-10}\) recovers each input volatility to within the tolerance, using 37 halvings in every case. A price of 0, 4.00, 100.00 or 120.00 at strike 100 is rejected, because the lower bound is 4.8771 and the spot is 100. Expected result: each row's recovery error is below \(10^{-10}\), each row uses 37 halvings, and the four prices are rejected with the bounds message. The inputs are model values; no data are read.
 126|         """Lab FC2-L08-1: implied volatility by bisection on the Black-Scholes call price.
 127| 
 128| For a European call with spot 100.00, strike K, r = 0.05 continuously compounded and
 129| tau = 1 year, the Black-Scholes price C(sigma) is strictly increasing in sigma (Lesson 8,
 130| Lemma 2.1), so the volatility that reproduces a price is found by bisection.
 131| The inputs are stated model parameters, not market data. Nothing is random and no file
 132| is read.
 133| 
 134| Prices are model values in currency units. The table prints each price in integer
 135| cents, rounded half up for display only: the inversion runs on the unrounded price, so
 136| the recovered volatility is compared with the volatility that produced the price.
 137| A price outside the no-arbitrage bounds max(S - K e^(-r tau), 0) < price < S has no
 138| volatility, and is rejected with a ValueError that names the condition. The lab uses
 139| the standard library only.
 140| """
 141| 
 142| from __future__ import annotations
 143| 
 144| import math
 145| 
 146| SPOT = 100.0
 147| RATE = 0.05
 148| TAU = 1.0
 149| LOW_SIGMA = 1e-6  # bracket endpoints for the bisection, in volatility units
 150| HIGH_SIGMA = 10.0
 151| TOL = 1e-10  # stop when the bracket is at most TOL wide
 152| CASES = (
 153|     (80.0, 0.10),
 154|     (80.0, 0.20),
 155|     (80.0, 0.40),
 156|     (100.0, 0.10),
 157|     (100.0, 0.20),
 158|     (100.0, 0.40),
 159|     (120.0, 0.10),
 160|     (120.0, 0.20),
 161|     (120.0, 0.40),
 162| )
 163| 
 164| 
 165| def cents(amount: float) -> int:
 166|     """Integer cents of a currency amount, rounded half up (away from zero for negatives)."""
 167|     sign = -1 if amount < 0 else 1
 168|     return sign * math.floor(abs(amount) * 100 + 0.5)
 169| 
 170| 
 171| def norm_cdf(x: float) -> float:
 172|     """Standard normal distribution function, (1 + erf(x / sqrt 2)) / 2."""
 173|     return 0.5 * (1.0 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2.0)))
 174| 
 175| 
 176| def bs_call(spot: float, strike: float, rate: float, tau: float, sigma: float) -> float:
 177|     """Black-Scholes European call value, in currency units: S Phi(d1) - K exp(-r tau) Phi(d2)."""
 178|     root = sigma * math.sqrt(tau)
 179|     d1 = (math.log(spot / strike) + (rate + sigma * sigma / 2.0) * tau) / root
 180|     d2 = d1 - root
 181|     return spot * norm_cdf(d1) - strike * math.exp(-rate * tau) * norm_cdf(d2)
 182| 
 183| 
 184| def lower_bound(spot: float, strike: float, rate: float, tau: float) -> float:
 185|     """The model-free lower bound on a European call, max(S - K exp(-r tau), 0)."""
 186|     return max(spot - strike * math.exp(-rate * tau), 0.0)
 187| 
 188| 
 189| def implied_volatility(price: float, strike: float) -> tuple[float, int]:
 190|     """Volatility sigma with bs_call(sigma) = price, by bisection, and the number of halvings used.
 191| 
 192|     Raises ValueError when the price is not strictly between the lower bound and the spot price.
 193|     """
 194|     bound = lower_bound(SPOT, strike, RATE, TAU)
 195|     if not bound < price < SPOT:
 196|         raise ValueError(
 197|             "price outside the no-arbitrage bounds: a European call needs max(S - K e^(-r tau), 0) < price < S"
 198|         )
 199| 
 200|     def gap(sigma: float) -> float:
 201|         return bs_call(SPOT, strike, RATE, TAU, sigma) - price
 202| 
 203|     if not gap(LOW_SIGMA) < 0 <= gap(HIGH_SIGMA):
 204|         raise ValueError(
 205|             f"the volatility bracket [{LOW_SIGMA}, {HIGH_SIGMA}] does not contain the root: "
 206|             "the implied volatility is above the upper end (the price is too close to the spot), "
 207|             "or below the lower end"
 208|         )
 209|     low, high = LOW_SIGMA, HIGH_SIGMA
 210|     halvings = 0
 211|     while high - low > TOL:
 212|         middle = (low + high) / 2
 213|         if gap(middle) < 0:
 214|             low = middle
 215|         else:
 216|             high = middle
 217|         halvings += 1
 218|     return (low + high) / 2, halvings
 219| 
 220| 
 221| def main() -> None:
 222|     """Invert the price of each case, check the recovered volatility, and reject prices outside the bounds."""
 223|     print(f"spot={SPOT} r={RATE} tau={TAU} tolerance={TOL} bracket=[{LOW_SIGMA}, {HIGH_SIGMA}]")
 224|     print(f"lower bound for strike 100 = {lower_bound(SPOT, 100.0, RATE, TAU):.4f} currency units")
 225|     print("strike  sigma_true  price_cents  sigma_recovered  halvings  abs_error")
 226|     worst = 0.0
 227|     for strike, sigma_true in CASES:
 228|         price = bs_call(SPOT, strike, RATE, TAU, sigma_true)
 229|         sigma_hat, halvings = implied_volatility(price, strike)
 230|         error = abs(sigma_hat - sigma_true)
 231|         worst = max(worst, error)
 232|         print(
 233|             f"{strike:>6.0f}  {sigma_true:>10.2f}  {cents(price):>11}  {sigma_hat:>15.8f}  {halvings:>8}  {error:>9.2e}"
 234|         )
 235|     print(f"largest absolute error over {len(CASES)} cases: {worst:.2e}; within the tolerance: {worst <= TOL}")
 236| 
 237|     print("prices with no volatility (strike 100)")
 238|     for price in (0.0, 4.0, 100.0, 120.0):
 239|         try:
 240|             implied_volatility(price, 100.0)
 241|         except ValueError as exc:
 242|             print(f"  price {price:.2f}: rejected: {exc}")
 243|         else:
 244|             print(f"  price {price:.2f}: accepted")
 245| 
 246|     assert worst <= TOL
 247|     assert len(CASES) == 9

 273|         4Where the canon breaks
 274|         In this lesson the implied volatility is defined relative to the formula \(C(\sigma)\) under A7 and A7′, which takes one volatility for every strike. Suppose two quotes with the same spot, rate and maturity, at strikes \(K_1\) and \(K_2\), give implied volatilities \(\sigma_1 \ne \sigma_2\) by Definition 1.1. Each implied volatility is unique by Theorem 2.3. If both quotes were prices of the A7 formula at one common volatility, each would invert to that volatility. So the two quotes are not both values of the formula at one common volatility. The lesson does not say which assumption fails, because the failure can be in A1 to A5 (for example A1, income on the stock, or A4, the rate), in A7, in A7′, or in the quote itself.
 275|         The bisection is a numerical procedure. Proposition 3.2 gives its error and step count, and the lesson does not teach the general theory of root-finding methods. Lab 1 uses only the bisection. Proposition 3.2 proves its error bound for the model's price function, and its proof uses the strict monotonicity of Lemma 2.1 and the bracket condition.
 276|         A price on a model's bounds has no implied volatility, and Theorem 2.3 says so for the A7 formula only. An American call, or a call on a stock that pays income, is outside A5 or A1 respectively, so Theorem 2.3 does not cover it. The proof of Lemma 2.1 is for the formula of this lesson.

 280|         5Misconceptions
 281| 
 282|           “Implied volatility is the stock's historical volatility.”
 283|             Definition 1.1 uses the observed price \(C^\ast\), the spot, the strike, the rate, the maturity and the model. The stock's past prices do not enter, so the implied volatility can differ from a historical volatility estimate computed from the same stock.
 284|           “Every option price has an implied volatility.”
 285|             Theorem 2.3 gives an implied volatility only for a price strictly between \(L\) and \(S\). Lab 1 rejects the prices 0.00, 4.00, 100.00 and 120.00 at strike 100. The price 4.00 is below the lower bound of 4.8771, and 100.00 is the spot, so neither has a volatility.
 286|           “A successful inversion check shows that a quoted price is right.”
 287|             The inversion check in Lab 1 starts from the formula's own price and returns the volatility that produced it. It checks the inversion code and the formula. It does not test any quote, because no quote enters the lab.

 292|         6Problem set
 293|         Mechanics
 294|           M1 · The lower bound
 295|             For \(S = K = 100\), \(r = 0.05\) and \(\tau = 1\), find \(L\).
 296|             Solution
 297|               \(Ke^{-r\tau} = 100e^{-0.05} = 95.1229\), so \(L = 100 - 95.1229 = 4.8771\), and the unrounded value is 4.877058.
 298|           M2 · Which prices have a volatility
 299|             For the inputs of M1, which of the prices 4.00, 4.90 and 100.00 has an implied volatility?
 300|             Solution
 301|               By Theorem 2.3 the prices must lie strictly between \(L = 4.8771\) and \(S = 100\). The price 4.00 is below \(L\), and 100.00 equals \(S\), so neither has an implied volatility. The price 4.90 lies strictly between the two bounds, so it has exactly one.
 302|           M3 · Halvings for a tolerance
 303|             For the bracket \([10^{-6}, 10]\) and tolerance \(10^{-10}\), how many halvings does the procedure of Proposition 3.2 use?
 304|             Solution
 305|               The width is \(W = 10 - 10^{-6}\), so \(W/\tau_{\text{tol}} \approx 10^{11}\), and \(\log_2(10^{11}) = 36.54\). The procedure stops at the first \(k\) with \(W/2^k \le 10^{-10}\), which is \(k = 37\). At \(k = 36\) the width is \(10/2^{36} = 1.46 \times 10^{-10}\), which is still above the tolerance. Lab 1 prints 37 halvings in every row.
 306| 
 307|         Proof
 308|           P1 · The identity in the proof of Lemma 2.1
 309|             Show \(S\varphi(d_1) = Ke^{-r\tau}\varphi(d_2)\) without the logarithm step, by writing \(d_1^2 - d_2^2\) in terms of \(a\) and \(s\).
 310|             Solution
 311|               \(d_1^2 - d_2^2 = (d_1 - d_2)(d_1 + d_2) = s \cdot 2a/s = 2a\). Then \(\varphi(d_1)/\varphi(d_2) = e^{-(d_1^2 - d_2^2)/2} = e^{-a}\), and \(e^{-a} = e^{-\ln(S/K) - r\tau} = K e^{-r\tau}/S\). Hence \(\varphi(d_1)/\varphi(d_2) = Ke^{-r\tau}/S\), which is the identity.
 312|           P2 · The upper limit
 313|             Show that \(C(\sigma) \to S\) as \(\sigma \to \infty\), using only that \(\Phi\) is increasing with limits 0 and 1.
 314|             Solution
 315|               As \(\sigma \to \infty\), \(a/s \to 0\), so \(d_1 = a/s + s/2 \to \infty\), and \(d_2 = a/s - s/2 \to -\infty\). Since \(\Phi\) has limits 1 at \(+\infty\) and 0 at \(-\infty\), \(\Phi(d_1) \to 1\) and \(\Phi(d_2) \to 0\). Then \(C = S\Phi(d_1) - Ke^{-r\tau}\Phi(d_2) \to S\).
 316|           P3 · The bracket invariant
 317|         Show that the bisection of Definition 3.1 keeps \(\sigma_{\text{lo}} < \sigma^\ast \le \sigma_{\text{hi}}\) after each step.
 318|             Solution
 319|               If \(C(m) < C^\ast\), then \(m < \sigma^\ast\), because \(C\) is increasing and \(C(\sigma) \ge C^\ast\) for \(\sigma \ge \sigma^\ast\). Setting \(\sigma_{\text{lo}} = m\) keeps \(\sigma_{\text{lo}} < \sigma^\ast\), and \(\sigma_{\text{hi}}\) is unchanged. If \(C(m) \ge C^\ast\), then \(m \ge \sigma^\ast\), and setting \(\sigma_{\text{hi}} = m\) keeps \(\sigma^\ast \le \sigma_{\text{hi}}\). Either way the invariant holds.

 321|         Empirical
 322|           E1 · The largest recovery error
 323|             From Lab 1's table, what is the largest recovery error, and is it within the stated tolerance?
 324|             Solution
 325|               The largest absolute error printed is \(1.74 \times 10^{-11}\), in the rows with volatility 0.40, and the rows with volatility 0.10 print \(1.73 \times 10^{-11}\). The tolerance is \(10^{-10}\), so every row is within it. The largest error is 0.174 of the tolerance, that is \(1.74 \times 10^{-11}\) divided by \(10^{-10}\), using the printed error.
 326|           E2 · Why the lower bound is zero at strike 120
 327|             Explain why the table's row for strike 120 gives a price of 46 cents at volatility 0.10, and why the bound \(L\) for that strike is zero.
 328|             Solution
 329|               \(120e^{-0.05} = 114.15\), which exceeds the spot of 100, so \(S - Ke^{-r\tau} < 0\) and \(L = \max(\cdot, 0) = 0\). The call is then worth something positive for every positive volatility: \(C(\sigma)\) is strictly increasing (Lemma 2.1) and tends to \(L = 0\) as \(\sigma \to 0^+\) (Lemma 2.2). At volatility 0.10 the printed price is 46 cents, from the unrounded value 46.2497 cents.
 330|           E3 · Predict the rejection
 331|             Predict whether a price of 0.50 at strike 120 is accepted or rejected, and say which condition decides.
 332|             Solution
 333|               At strike 120 the lower bound is \(L = 0\), so 0.50 is strictly above it and strictly below the spot of 100. The bounds check passes, and the bracket test decides the rest. The bracket has a sign change, because \(C\) is near 0 at the lower end and above 0.50 at the upper end. The price is accepted. Running the lab's inversion on this price gives a volatility of 0.102077 after 37 halvings, although Lab 1 does not print this case. The bounds message appears only for a price at or below 0 or at or above 100 at this strike.

 336|           R1 · What monotonicity needs
 337|             State the property of a price function that Theorem 2.3 needs, and say which of the lessons of FC2 provides it for the formula of this lesson.
 338|             Solution
 339|               The argument needs a price function of volatility that is continuous and strictly increasing, with the limits of Lemma 2.2. Lemma 2.1 proves the derivative is positive for the formula of Lesson 7, and that is the only price function this lesson covers. A different pricing model needs its own proof of the same property.
 340|           R2 · A one-cent error
 341|             At spot 100, strike 100, \(r = 0.05\), \(\tau = 1\), and volatility 0.20, the derivative \(dC/d\sigma\) is 37.52 currency units per unit of volatility, to two decimals (Lemma 2.1: \(100\varphi(0.35) = 37.5240\)). Use the first-order approximation \(\Delta C \approx \dfrac{dC}{d\sigma}\,\Delta\sigma\) to estimate the change in implied volatility from a one-cent change in the quoted price.
 342|             Solution
 343|               The change in price is \(0.01\) currency units. Using the displayed value 37.52, \(\Delta\sigma \approx 0.01/37.52 = 0.000267\), or 0.0267 volatility points (one volatility point is 0.01 of volatility), which is 0.027 to three decimals. (The unrounded value 37.5240 gives 0.000266.) This is a first-order estimate of a local change, not a theorem. At these inputs (spot and strike 100, \(\tau = 1\), \(\sigma = 0.20\)), a one-cent quote error moves the implied volatility by about 0.0266 volatility points (0.000266) to first order.
 344|           R3 · The evidence a fit needs
 345|             State what evidence would show whether a single volatility under A7 fits the quoted prices of one expiry across several strikes. Name one input it would need. Make no claim about any market.
 346|             Solution
 347|               The test needs quoted call prices at several strikes for one expiry, the spot and the rate on each quote date, and the dividends over the life of the option, because A1 excludes income. From these the implied volatility of each quote follows by Definition 1.1, and the test asks whether they agree. The lesson cites no source for the inputs and makes no claim about any quote.
