  51|       A call option is worth what a portfolio of the stock and a riskless bond costs when the portfolio pays the same amount as the option. In a one-period binomial model, the stock can end at one of two prices, and that replication fixes the price today without any forecast of the stock's expected return. The same step, applied at each node, prices a tree of many periods. Section 2 names the probabilities that appear in the price, and Section 3 states the backward induction. Lab 1 computes a one-period and a two-period price in exact fractions and checks each against the replication. Lesson 7 takes the number of periods to a limit.
  52| 
  53|       1The one-period model and its replicating portfolio
  54| 
  55|       The lesson uses these assumptions from the FC2 numbering. A1: no dividends, storage costs or other income on the stock over the period. A2: no transaction costs, no bid–ask spread, no short-sale constraint, and lending and borrowing at the riskless return \(R_f\) of Definition 1.1, without limit. A3: no arbitrage opportunity, as defined in Lesson 1. Equivalently, a portfolio whose cost today is at most zero and whose payoff is non-negative in every state has cost zero and payoff zero in every state. A5: the claim, whose payoff Definition 1.2 gives, is paid at the end of the period only. A6: the binomial condition, stated in Definition 1.2 of this lesson. A3 is a hypothesis about the model, not a fact about markets. The term arbitrage opportunity is defined in Lesson 1.
  56| 
  57| 
  58|         Definition 1.1 (Gross riskless return)
  59|         Let \(r_1\) be the riskless rate over one period. The gross riskless return is \(R_f = 1 + r_1\). A sure payoff of 1 at the end of the period is worth \(1/R_f\) today. This lesson uses \(R_f\) for the gross return and \(r_1\) for the one-period rate. The letter \(r\) without a subscript is the continuously compounded rate of A4 in Lessons 4 and 5, and this lesson does not use it.
  60| 
  61| 
  62| 
  63|         Definition 1.2 (One-period binomial model)
  64|         Binomial option pricing values a claim by replication when the stock moves between two values over each period. The stock is worth \(S_0 > 0\) today and is worth \(uS_0\) or \(dS_0\) at the end of the period, where \(u\) and \(d\) are constants. Assumption A6 is \(0 < d < R_f < u\). A claim pays \(V_1(u) = \max(uS_0 - K, 0)\) if the stock goes up and \(V_1(d) = \max(dS_0 - K, 0)\) if it goes down. This is the call payoff of Lesson 4, with strike \(K\) and expiry at the end of the period.
  65| 
  66| 
  67| 
  68|         Definition 1.3 (Replicating portfolio, binomial)
  69|         A replicating portfolio holds \(\Delta\) shares and a bond position \(B\), where \(B > 0\) is cash lent and \(B < 0\) is cash borrowed, repaid with the gross return \(R_f\). It satisfies
  70|         \[
  71|           \Delta\, uS_0 + R_f B = V_1(u), \qquad \Delta\, dS_0 + R_f B = V_1(d).
  72|         \]
  73|         Its cost today is \(V_0 = \Delta S_0 + B\).
  74| 
  75| 
  76| 
  77|         Theorem 1.4 (One-period pricing)
  78|         Under A1, A2, A3, A5 and A6, with \(q = (R_f - d)/(u - d)\):
  79|         (a) there is exactly one replicating portfolio; (b) its cost is \(V_0 = \big(q\,V_1(u) + (1 - q)\,V_1(d)\big)/R_f\), and \(0 < q < 1\); (c) given A2, if the claim trades at a price \(\pi \ne V_0\) today, then a zero-cost portfolio with a strictly positive payoff in every state exists (sell the claim and hold the replicating portfolio, or buy the claim and sell the replicating portfolio, and invest the surplus at the riskless rate). A3 excludes such a portfolio, so under A3 the price is \(V_0\).
  80| 
  81|       Proof
  82|         (a) Subtract the two equations of Definition 1.3: \(\Delta S_0 (u - d) = V_1(u) - V_1(d)\). Since \(u \ne d\) and \(S_0 > 0\), \(\Delta = (V_1(u) - V_1(d))/((u - d)S_0)\) is unique. Then \(R_f B = V_1(u) - u\Delta S_0\) is unique too. Both equations hold for these values, by construction.
  83|         (b) Write \(\Delta S_0 = (V_1(u) - V_1(d))/(u - d)\) and \(R_f B = V_1(u) - u\Delta S_0 = (uV_1(d) - dV_1(u))/(u - d)\). Then
  84|         \[
  85|           V_0 = \frac{V_1(u) - V_1(d)}{u - d} + \frac{uV_1(d) - dV_1(u)}{(u - d)R_f}
  86|           = \frac{(R_f - d)\,V_1(u) + (u - R_f)\,V_1(d)}{(u - d)R_f}.
  87|         \]
  88|         Because \(u - d > 0\), \(q = (R_f - d)/(u - d) > 0\) since \(R_f > d\), and \(1 - q = (u - R_f)/(u - d) > 0\) since \(u > R_f\). Hence \(0 < q < 1\) and \(V_0 = (qV_1(u) + (1 - q)V_1(d))/R_f\).
  89|         (c) Suppose \(\pi > V_0\). Sell the claim for \(\pi\), buy the replicating portfolio for \(V_0\), and lend the surplus \(\pi - V_0\). The cash flow today is zero. At the end of the period the claim is paid, so the position receives \(-V_1\) from the sold claim, the replicating portfolio pays \(V_1\) by Definition 1.3, and the lent surplus pays \((\pi - V_0)R_f\). The total is \((\pi - V_0)R_f > 0\) in both states. If \(\pi < V_0\), reverse every position, and the total is \((V_0 - \pi)R_f > 0\) in both states. Either way a zero-cost portfolio has a strictly positive payoff, which A3 excludes. \(\blacksquare\)
  90| 
  91| 
  92| 
  93|         Example 1.5 (One-period call, exact numbers)
  94|         Let \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 100\), \(u = 3/2\), \(d = 1/2\) and \(R_f = 5/4\). Then \(S_1 \in \{150, 50\}\), so \(V_1(u) = 50\) and \(V_1(d) = 0\). The replicating shares are \(\Delta = 50/(1 \cdot 100) = 1/2\), and the bond position is \(B = (u \cdot 0 - d \cdot 50)/((u - d)R_f) = -25/(5/4) = -20\). The cost is \(V_0 = \Delta S_0 + B = 100/2 - 20 = 30\). Also \(q = (5/4 - 1/2)/(3/2 - 1/2) = 3/4\), and \((3/4 \cdot 50)/(5/4) = 30\), as Theorem 1.4 (b) requires. The value is 3000 cents. The riskless return \(R_f = 5/4\) is a model input chosen for exact arithmetic, a return of 25 percent per period, and it is not a market rate.
  95| 
  96| 
  97| 
  98|         Example 1.6 (A mispriced call in the model of Example 1.5)
  99|         Let the call trade at \(\pi = 35\), above \(V_0 = 30\). Sell the call for 35, buy \(\Delta = 1/2\) share for 50, and borrow 20. The cash flow today is \(35 - 50 + 20 = 5\), which is lent at \(R_f\), so it pays \(5 \cdot 5/4 = 25/4\) at the end of the period. At the end of the period, the sold call costs \(-50\) if the stock goes up and 0 if it goes down. The shares and the loan give \(\tfrac12 \cdot 150 - 25 = 50\) in the up state and \(\tfrac12 \cdot 50 - 25 = 0\) in the down state. The total is \(25/4\) in both states. This is the portfolio that Theorem 1.4 (c) describes.
 100| 
 101| 
 102| 
 103|         Remark 1.7 (The real-world probability is not in the price)
 104|         The formula in Theorem 1.4 (b) uses \(u\), \(d\), \(R_f\) and the two payoffs. It does not use the real-world probability, meaning the probability that the stock goes up. Two models with the same \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\) and different probabilities of an up move give the same \(V_0\), because the replication of Theorem 1.4 (a) uses no probability at all.
 105| 
 106| 
 107|       2The risk-neutral probability
 108| 
 109| 
 110|         Definition 2.1 (Risk-neutral probability, binomial)
 111|         The risk-neutral probability of an up move in the one-period binomial model is \(q = (R_f - d)/(u - d)\), and \(\mathbb{Q}(\text{up}) = q\), \(\mathbb{Q}(\text{down}) = 1 - q\). Expectations under \(\mathbb{Q}\) are written \(\E^{\mathbb{Q}}\).
 112| 
 113| 
 114|       Remark. In the binomial model, \(q\) is the weight under which the discounted stock has the same expected value next period as today. The stock's expected next-period value is \(\E^{\mathbb{Q}}[S_1] = q\,uS_0 + (1 - q)\,dS_0\), and this equals \(R_f S_0\) exactly when \(q = (R_f - d)/(u - d)\). This is an identity of the formula, not a theorem about markets. The weight \(q\) is not the real-world probability that the stock rises: in Example 1.5, \(q = 3/4\), and the model contains no real-world probability at all. A different measure, for a general model, is outside this unit, and this lesson does not use it.
 115| 
 116| 
 117|         Theorem 2.2 (Discounted expectation)
 118|         Under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.4, \(V_0 = \E^{\mathbb{Q}}[V_1]/R_f\).
 119| 
 120|       Proof
 121|         By definition \(\E^{\mathbb{Q}}[V_1] = q\,V_1(u) + (1 - q)\,V_1(d)\). Theorem 1.4 (b) states \(V_0 = \E^{\mathbb{Q}}[V_1]/R_f\). \(\blacksquare\)

 123| 
 124|       3Multi-period pricing by backward induction
 125| 
 126| 
 127|         Definition 3.1 (Recombining tree)
 128|         Let \(N \ge 1\) be the number of periods. The node \((n, k)\) is the stock price after \(n\) periods and \(k\) up moves, with \(S(n,k) = S_0 u^k d^{n - k}\). An up move then a down move and a down move then an up move reach the same node, so the tree recombines: it has \(n + 1\) nodes at time \(n\), not \(2^n\). Assumption A6 applies at every node \(n < N\) with the same \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\). This is assumption A6′.
 129| 
 130| 
 131| 
 132|         Definition 3.2 (Backward induction)
 133|         Let \(f\) be the payoff at expiry, so the claim pays \(f(S(N,k))\) at node \((N, k)\). Define \(V(N,k) = f(S(N,k))\) and, for \(n < N\),
 134|         \[
 135|           V(n,k) = \frac{q\,V(n+1,\,k+1) + (1 - q)\,V(n+1,\,k)}{R_f}.
 136|         \]
 137| 
 138| 
 139| 
 140|         Theorem 3.3 (Multi-period pricing)
 141|         Under A1, A2, A3, A5, A6 and A6′, for \(0 \le n \le N\) the arbitrage-free price of the claim at node \((n,k)\) is \(V(n,k)\). In particular
 142|         \[
 143|           V(0,0) = R_f^{-N} \sum_{k=0}^{N} \binom{N}{k}\, q^{k} (1 - q)^{N - k}\, f\!\left(S_0 u^k d^{N-k}\right).
 144|         \]
 145| 
 146|       Proof
 147|         Price first. Induct backward on \(n\). At \(n = N\), the claim is paid and has no later cash flow (A5), so its price at \((N,k)\) is \(f(S(N,k)) = V(N,k)\). Suppose the price at each node \((n+1, j)\) is \(V(n+1, j)\). The one-step subtree from \((n,k)\) reaches \((n+1,k)\) and \((n+1,k+1)\), and A6 holds on it. Theorem 1.4 applied at \((n,k)\) with \(V_1(u) = V(n+1,k+1)\) and \(V_1(d) = V(n+1,k)\) gives the replicating cost \(V(n,k)\), and any other price admits the arbitrage of Theorem 1.4 (c). So the price at \((n,k)\) is \(V(n,k)\).
 148|         Closed form. Claim: for \(0 \le n \le N\),
 149|         \[
 150|           V(n,k) = R_f^{-(N-n)} \sum_{i=0}^{N-n} \binom{N-n}{i}\, q^{i} (1 - q)^{N-n-i}\, f\!\left(S(n,k)\,u^{i} d^{N-n-i}\right).
 151|         \]
 152|         At \(n = N\) both sides equal \(f(S(N,k))\). Assume the claim at \(n+1\), write \(m = N - n - 1\), and note \(S(n+1,k+1) = uS(n,k)\) and \(S(n+1,k) = dS(n,k)\). Substituting into Definition 3.2, the up term has the factor \(q\) and the index \(j = i - 1\), and the down term has the factor \(1 - q\) and the index \(j = i\). Their coefficients on \(q^i(1-q)^{m+1-i} f(S(n,k)u^i d^{m+1-i})\) are \(\binom{m}{i-1}\) and \(\binom{m}{i}\). Pascal's rule \(\binom{m}{i-1} + \binom{m}{i} = \binom{m+1}{i}\) gives the sum, and the factor \(R_f^{-1} R_f^{-m} = R_f^{-(m+1)}\) gives the claim at \(n\). Setting \(n = 0\) and \(k = 0\) gives the displayed formula. \(\blacksquare\)
 153| 
 154| 
 155| 
 156|         Example 3.4 (Two periods, the parameters of Example 1.5)
 157|         Use \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 100\), \(u = 3/2\), \(d = 1/2\), \(R_f = 5/4\) and a call. The expiry prices are \(S(2,2) = 225\), \(S(2,1) = 75\) and \(S(2,0) = 25\), with payoffs 125, 0 and 0. At \((1,1)\), \(S = 150\), and \(V(1,1) = (\tfrac34 \cdot 125)/\tfrac54 = 75\). At \((1,0)\), \(S = 50\), and \(V(1,0) = 0\). Then \(V(0,0) = (\tfrac34 \cdot 75)/\tfrac54 = 45\). The closed form gives \(R_f^{-2}\,\tfrac{9}{16}\cdot 125 = \tfrac{16}{25}\cdot\tfrac{9}{16}\cdot 125 = 45\). The price is 4500 cents.
 158| 
 159| 
 160| 
 161|         Definition 3.5 (Self-financing strategy)
 162|         A self-financing strategy trades the stock and the bond at each node reached. At each node, the new holdings cost exactly the wealth carried to that node, so no cash is added or removed between dates. The wealth carried to the next node is the value of those holdings at that node's stock price and bond return.
 163| 
 164| 
 165| 
 166|         Lab 1 · Replication, risk-neutral pricing and a two-period tree in exact arithmetic
 167|         Stated before running: with spot 100, strike 100, up factor 3/2, down factor 1/2 and gross return 5/4 per period, the one-period price by replication equals the discounted risk-neutral expectation, 30 (3000 cents). The two-period price by backward induction equals the closed-form path sum, 45 (4500 cents). A self-financing strategy constructed from the one-period replications reproduces each node value and each payoff at expiry. Expected result: the output shows these equalities; the one-period check, the four path checks and the self-financing check print True. All inputs are model values; no data are read.
 168|         """Lab FC2-L06-1: one-period replication, risk-neutral pricing and a two-step binomial tree.
 169| 
 170| Every number is exact. Shares, bond positions, stock prices, payoffs and values are Fraction
 171| values. Stock prices, payoffs and values after one move are printed in currency units; shares
 172| and bond positions are printed as exact fractions, because they are positions. Only the time-0
 173| values are also printed in integer cents: the one-period cost V0, the one-period discounted
 174| expectation, and the two-period V0 by backward induction and in closed form (half up, which changes
 175| nothing here because each is already a whole number of cents). The parameters are model inputs
 176| chosen for exact arithmetic, not market data and not a forecast: spot 100 currency units, strike 100,
 177| up factor 3/2, down factor 1/2, and gross riskless return 5/4 per period. The claim is a European call paid at
 178| expiry, with payoff max(S - K, 0). No input is random and no data file is read.
 179| """
 180| 
 181| from __future__ import annotations
 182| 
 183| import math
 184| from fractions import Fraction
 185| 
 186| SPOT = Fraction(100)
 187| STRIKE = Fraction(100)
 188| UP = Fraction(3, 2)  # gross move on an up step
 189| DOWN = Fraction(1, 2)  # gross move on a down step
 190| RETURN = Fraction(5, 4)  # gross riskless return per period, R_f = 1 + r_1 (r_1 is the one-period rate)
 191| 
 192| 
 193| def cents(amount: Fraction) -> int:
 194|     """Integer cents of a currency amount, rounded half up (0.5 cent goes up)."""
 195|     return math.floor(amount * 100 + Fraction(1, 2))
 196| 
 197| 
 198| def payoff(price: Fraction) -> Fraction:
 199|     """Payoff at expiry of the European call with the strike above, in currency units."""
 200|     return max(price - STRIKE, Fraction(0))
 201| 
 202| 
 203| def replicate(
 204|     up_value: Fraction, down_value: Fraction, spot: Fraction, up: Fraction, down: Fraction
 205| ) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
 206|     """Shares and bond position that give the two next-date values, from one node.
 207| 
 208|     The bond position is the cash lent (positive) or borrowed (negative) at the node, repaid at
 209|     RETURN per period. The two equations are solved exactly; up differs from down, so the solution is unique.
 210|     """
 211|     shares = (up_value - down_value) / ((up - down) * spot)
 212|     bond = (up * down_value - down * up_value) / ((up - down) * RETURN)
 213|     return shares, bond
 214| 
 215| 
 216| def main() -> None:
 217|     """Price one period two ways, price two periods by backward induction, and check the replication."""
 218|     q = (RETURN - DOWN) / (UP - DOWN)
 219|     print(f"parameters: spot={SPOT} strike={STRIKE} up={UP} down={DOWN} gross return per period={RETURN}")
 220|     print(f"risk-neutral up probability q=(R-d)/(u-d) = {q}")
 221| 
 222|     print("one period")
 223|     up_value, down_value = payoff(SPOT * UP), payoff(SPOT * DOWN)
 224|     shares, bond = replicate(up_value, down_value, SPOT, UP, DOWN)
 225|     price = shares * SPOT + bond
 226|     print(f"  payoff after an up move = {up_value}; after a down move = {down_value}")
 227|     print(f"  replicating shares = {shares}; bond position = {bond}")
 228|     print(f"  replicating cost V0 = {price} = {cents(price)} cents")
 229|     expected_value = (q * up_value + (1 - q) * down_value) / RETURN
 230|     print(f"  discounted risk-neutral expectation = {expected_value} = {cents(expected_value)} cents")
 231|     replicated_up = shares * SPOT * UP + bond * RETURN
 232|     replicated_down = shares * SPOT * DOWN + bond * RETURN
 233|     one_period_ok = replicated_up == up_value and replicated_down == down_value and price == expected_value
 234|     print(f"  portfolio matches the payoff in both states and equals the expectation: {one_period_ok}")
 235| 
 236|     print("two periods")
 237|     uu, ud, dd = SPOT * UP * UP, SPOT * UP * DOWN, SPOT * DOWN * DOWN
 238|     terminal = {"uu": payoff(uu), "ud": payoff(ud), "dd": payoff(dd)}
 239|     print(f"  stock at expiry: uu={uu} ud={ud} dd={dd}")
 240|     print(f"  payoff at expiry: uu={terminal['uu']} ud={terminal['ud']} dd={terminal['dd']}")
 241|     # Backward induction: each node's value is the risk-neutral discounted expectation of its two successors.
 242|     value_after_up = (q * terminal["uu"] + (1 - q) * terminal["ud"]) / RETURN
 243|     value_after_down = (q * terminal["ud"] + (1 - q) * terminal["dd"]) / RETURN
 244|     value_today = (q * value_after_up + (1 - q) * value_after_down) / RETURN
 245|     print(f"  backward induction: value after an up move = {value_after_up}; after a down move = {value_after_down}")
 246|     print(f"  backward induction: V0 = {value_today} = {cents(value_today)} cents")
 247| 
 248|     # Closed form: discount the q-weighted sum over the paths of the tree.
 249|     closed = (
 250|         sum(math.comb(2, k) * q**k * (1 - q) ** (2 - k) * payoff(SPOT * UP**k * DOWN ** (2 - k)) for k in range(3))
 251|         / RETURN**2
 252|     )
 253|     print(f"  closed form over paths: V0 = {closed} = {cents(closed)} cents")
 254| 
 255|     # Direct replication: hold the time-0 portfolio, rebalance at the node reached, and check the payoff.
 256|     shares0, bond0 = replicate(value_after_up, value_after_down, SPOT, UP, DOWN)
 257|     wealth_up = shares0 * SPOT * UP + bond0 * RETURN
 258|     wealth_down = shares0 * SPOT * DOWN + bond0 * RETURN
 259|     print(f"  time-0 holdings: shares {shares0}, bond {bond0}, cost {shares0 * SPOT + bond0}")
 260|     print(
 261|         f"  wealth after an up move = {wealth_up} (value_after_up {value_after_up}); after a down move = {wealth_down}"
 262|     )
 263|     shares_u, bond_u = replicate(terminal["uu"], terminal["ud"], SPOT * UP, UP, DOWN)
 264|     shares_d, bond_d = replicate(terminal["ud"], terminal["dd"], SPOT * DOWN, UP, DOWN)
 265|     print(f"  holdings after an up move: shares {shares_u}, bond {bond_u}")
 266|     print(f"  holdings after a down move: shares {shares_d}, bond {bond_d}")
 267|     # (path, terminal wealth of the strategy, payoff of the claim on that path)
 268|     paths = [
 269|         ("uu", shares_u * uu + bond_u * RETURN, terminal["uu"]),
 270|         ("ud", shares_u * ud + bond_u * RETURN, terminal["ud"]),
 271|         ("du", shares_d * ud + bond_d * RETURN, terminal["ud"]),
 272|         ("dd", shares_d * dd + bond_d * RETURN, terminal["dd"]),
 273|     ]
 274|     for label, wealth_end, target in paths:
 275|         print(f"  path {label}: terminal wealth {wealth_end} = payoff {target}: {wealth_end == target}")
 276|     # Self-financing: the holdings chosen at each node cost exactly the wealth carried to that node.
 277|     rebalance_up_ok = shares_u * SPOT * UP + bond_u == wealth_up
 278|     rebalance_down_ok = shares_d * SPOT * DOWN + bond_d == wealth_down
 279|     self_financing = (
 280|         all(wealth_end == target for _, wealth_end, target in paths) and rebalance_up_ok and rebalance_down_ok
 281|     )
 282|     matches_induction = (
 283|         wealth_up == value_after_up and wealth_down == value_after_down and shares0 * SPOT + bond0 == value_today
 284|     )
 285|     reproduced = self_financing and matches_induction
 286|     print(f"  self-financing strategy reproduces every node value and every payoff: {reproduced}")
 287| 
 288|     assert one_period_ok
 289|     assert value_today == closed == Fraction(45)
 290|     assert self_financing and matches_induction
 291| 
 292| 
 293| main()
 294|         parameters: spot=100 strike=100 up=3/2 down=1/2 gross return per period=5/4
 295| risk-neutral up probability q=(R-d)/(u-d) = 3/4
 296| one period
 297|   payoff after an up move = 50; after a down move = 0
 298|   replicating shares = 1/2; bond position = -20
 299|   replicating cost V0 = 30 = 3000 cents
 300|   discounted risk-neutral expectation = 30 = 3000 cents
 301|   portfolio matches the payoff in both states and equals the expectation: True
 302| two periods
 303|   stock at expiry: uu=225 ud=75 dd=25
 304|   payoff at expiry: uu=125 ud=0 dd=0
 305|   backward induction: value after an up move = 75; after a down move = 0
 306|   backward induction: V0 = 45 = 4500 cents
 307|   closed form over paths: V0 = 45 = 4500 cents
 308|   time-0 holdings: shares 3/4, bond -30, cost 45
 309|   wealth after an up move = 75 (value_after_up 75); after a down move = 0
 310|   holdings after an up move: shares 5/6, bond -50
 311|   holdings after a down move: shares 0, bond 0
 312|   path uu: terminal wealth 125 = payoff 125: True
 313|   path ud: terminal wealth 0 = payoff 0: True
 314|   path du: terminal wealth 0 = payoff 0: True
 315|   path dd: terminal wealth 0 = payoff 0: True
 316|   self-financing strategy reproduces every node value and every payoff: True
 317|         The checks test the algebra of Theorems 1.4, 2.2 and 3.3 with these inputs, and the code that implements them. They say nothing about any market. Shares are printed as exact fractions of a share, and the bond position as an exact amount in currency units; a negative bond position is borrowing. The lab keeps u, d and R_f fixed at the values of Example 1.5, so it does not test other choices of them. The self-financing path check is the direct replication: it shows that the portfolio constructed from the time-0 holdings and the rebalanced holdings pays the claim on each of the four paths, which the backward induction predicts.

 321|         4Where the canon breaks
 322|         The model has two possible next-date prices. With three possible next prices and only a stock and a bond to trade, two unknowns \(\Delta\) and \(B\) must satisfy three equations. A payoff that is not reproduced by the stock and the bond then has no replicating portfolio. For example, let the next prices be 150, 100 and 50, with payoffs 1, 0 and 0. The last two equations give \(\Delta \cdot 50 = 0\), so \(\Delta = 0\) and \(R_f B = 0\), and the first equation then reads \(0 = 1\). Theorem 1.4 does not apply to such a claim, and this lesson states no pricing rule for it.
 323|         Assumption A6′ holds \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\) fixed across nodes. The lesson takes these three numbers as given and states no rule for choosing them from data. Assumption A2 excludes transaction costs and short-sale constraints. Theorem 1.4 uses A2 for the bond position in (a) and (b) and for the arbitrage argument in (c). The lesson states nothing about prices when A2 fails.
 324|         The claims here are paid at expiry only (A5). An option that may be exercised before expiry is not valued in this lesson. Early exercise in a tree is a separate question, and Lesson 4 defines the terms for it.

 328|         5Misconceptions
 329| 
 330|           “A higher probability of an up move raises the price of the call.”
 331|             Theorem 1.4 (b) gives \(V_0\) from \(u\), \(d\), \(R_f\) and the two payoffs only, and Remark 1.7 states that the real-world probability does not enter. Changing the probability of an up move changes no input of the formula, so the price in Example 1.5 stays at 30 whatever that probability is.
 332|           “The risk-neutral probability \(q\) is the probability that the stock goes up.”
 333|             In Example 1.5, \(q = 3/4\), and the stock's up factor is \(u = 3/2\). The weight \(q\) is the number that makes the discounted expected payoff equal the replicating cost (Theorem 2.2). It is a weight on the two payoffs, and the model contains no statement of how likely an up move is in the world.
 334|           “The price of a call on a tree is the call's payoff at the expected final stock price, discounted.”
 335|             In Example 3.4, the expected final price under the path weights \(q^2\), \(2q(1 - q)\) and \((1 - q)^2\) is \(\E^{\mathbb{Q}}[S(2,\cdot)] = 156.25 = R_f^2 S_0\). This is arithmetic on the three expiry prices of Example 3.4, and the discounted expected final price is \(S_0 = 100\). The payoff at that expected price is \(\max(156.25 - 100, 0) = 56.25\), and discounting it gives \(56.25 \cdot 16/25 = 36\). The correct price is 45, which is the discounted expectation of the payoff (Theorem 3.3). Before discounting, the expected payoff under these three weights is 70.3125 USD (\(1125/16\)), and the payoff at the expected price is 56.25 USD. The comparison is a computation on these three prices; it is not a general claim.

 341|         Mechanics
 342|           M1 · One period with \(u = 2\)
 343|             Let \(S_0 = 100\), \(K = 100\), \(u = 2\), \(d = 1/2\) and \(R_f = 5/4\). Find \(q\) and the cost \(V_0\) of the call.
 344|             Solution
 345|               \(q = (5/4 - 1/2)/(2 - 1/2) = (3/4)/(3/2) = 1/2\). The payoffs are \(V_1(u) = 200 - 100 = 100\) and \(V_1(d) = 0\). Then \(V_0 = (\tfrac12 \cdot 100)/(5/4) = 40\), which is 4000 cents.
 346|           M2 · The replicating portfolio for M1
 347|             For the call in M1, give \(\Delta\) and \(B\), and check that \(\Delta S_0 + B = 40\).
 348|             Solution
 349|               \(\Delta = (100 - 0)/((2 - 1/2) \cdot 100) = 100/150 = 2/3\). \(B = (2 \cdot 0 - \tfrac12 \cdot 100)/((2 - \tfrac12)\cdot\tfrac54) = -50/(15/8) = -80/3\). Then \(\Delta S_0 + B = 200/3 - 80/3 = 40\), as Theorem 1.4 (b) requires.
 350|           M3 · Two periods with the M1 parameters
 351|             Use the parameters of M1 for a two-period tree and a call with strike 100. Give the expiry prices, the payoffs, the value after one up move and the price \(V(0,0)\).
 352|             Solution
 353|               Expiry prices are \(S(2,2) = 400\), \(S(2,1) = 100\) and \(S(2,0) = 25\), with payoffs 300, 0 and 0. With \(q = 1/2\), \(V(1,1) = (\tfrac12 \cdot 300)/(5/4) = 120\), and \(V(1,0) = 0\). Then \(V(0,0) = (\tfrac12 \cdot 120)/(5/4) = 48\), which is 4800 cents.
 354| 
 355|         Proof
 356|           P1 · The risk-neutral weight is a probability
 357|             Under A6, show that \(0 < q < 1\) and that \(q\) and \(1 - q\) sum to 1.
 358|             Solution
 359|               By definition \(q + (1 - q) = 1\). Since \(d < R_f\), \(R_f - d > 0\), so \(q > 0\). Since \(R_f < u\), \(u - R_f > 0\), and \(1 - q = (u - R_f)/(u - d) > 0\) because \(u - d > 0\). So \(0 < q < 1\).
 360|           P2 · The replicating portfolio is unique
 361|             Show that Theorem 1.4 (a) holds without using the values of \(V_1(u)\) and \(V_1(d)\).
 362|             Solution
 363|               Subtracting the two equations of Definition 1.3 eliminates \(B\) and gives \(\Delta S_0 (u - d) = V_1(u) - V_1(d)\), a linear equation in \(\Delta\) with coefficient \(S_0(u - d)\). This coefficient is nonzero because \(S_0 > 0\) and \(u > d\), so \(\Delta\) is unique. The first equation then fixes \(R_f B\), and since \(R_f > 0\), \(B\) is unique.
 364|           P3 · An arbitrage at a higher price
 365|             Write the arbitrage of Theorem 1.4 (c) for a claim that trades at \(\pi > V_0\), and show that its payoff is \((\pi - V_0)R_f\) in both states.
 366|             Solution
 367|               Sell the claim for \(\pi\), buy \(\Delta\) shares and \(B\) bonds at cost \(V_0\), and lend \(\pi - V_0\). Today's cash is \(\pi - V_0 - (\pi - V_0) = 0\). At expiry the sold claim costs \(-V_1\), the replicating portfolio pays \(V_1\) in each state by Definition 1.3, and the lent amount pays \((\pi - V_0)R_f\). The sum is \((\pi - V_0)R_f\) in both states, which is positive.

 374|           E2 · Predict the scaling
 375|             Predict the one-period price if the spot and the strike are both doubled to 200, with \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\) unchanged. Then check the prediction in the lab's one-period formula by hand.
 376|             Solution
 377|               The payoffs double, because \(\max(uS_0 - K, 0)\) is positive homogeneous in \(S_0\) and \(K\). Theorem 1.4 (b) is linear in the payoffs, so \(V_0\) doubles to 60, or 6000 cents. By hand: \(q = 3/4\), \(V_1(u) = \max(300 - 200, 0) = 100\), and \((\tfrac34 \cdot 100)/(\tfrac54) = 60\).
 378|           E3 · Settle a quoted price
 379|             A dealer quotes the one-period call of Example 1.5 at 28. Using Theorem 1.4, say whether the quote admits an arbitrage, and describe the portfolio that exploits it.
 380|             Solution
 381|               The quote is below \(V_0 = 30\), so Theorem 1.4 (c) gives an arbitrage. Buy the call for 28. Short the replicating portfolio: sell \(1/2\) share for 50, and lend 20, which reverses the borrowing of Example 1.5. The short sale brings in \(50 - 20 = 30\). Together with the purchase, the cash flow today is \(-28 + 30 = 2\), and the surplus 2 is lent. Today's net cash is \(-28 + 30 - 2 = 0\). At expiry the call pays 50 in the up state and 0 in the down state. The short shares cost \(-\tfrac12 \cdot 150 = -75\) or \(-\tfrac12 \cdot 50 = -25\). The lent 20 returns \(20 \cdot 5/4 = 25\), and the lent 2 returns \(2 \cdot 5/4 = 5/2\). The totals are \(50 - 75 + 25 + 5/2 = 5/2\) in the up state and \(0 - 25 + 25 + 5/2 = 5/2\) in the down state, a strictly positive payoff at zero cost.

 383|         Research
 384|           R1 · A payoff with no replication
 385|             In the three-price example of Section 4, find a claim whose payoff the stock and the bond do replicate, and state what the example shows about the replication condition.
 386|             Solution
 387|               Take the claim with payoffs 50, 0 and \(-50\) at the next prices 150, 100 and 50. At \(R_f = 5/4\), hold \(\Delta = 1\) share and \(R_f B = -100\), so the bond position is \(B = -80\). The stock pays 150, 100 and 50 at the three prices, and the bond repays \(-100\) in every state, so the portfolio pays \(50\), \(0\) and \(-50\), exactly the claim. The claim is therefore replicated. The payoff (1, 0, 0) of Section 4 is not replicated: a combination \(a(150, 100, 50) + b(1, 1, 1)\) of the stock and the bond payoffs equals \((1, 0, 0)\) only if \(a = 0\) and \(b = 0\) from the last two entries, which then give \(0 = 1\) in the first. Replication is a property of the payoff vector, not of the number of states alone.
 388|           R2 · A zero rate
 389|             Take the parameters of Example 1.5 with \(R_f = 1\). Compute \(q\) and \(V_0\), and say what the zero-rate case removes from the replicating portfolio.
 390|             Solution
 391|               \(q = (1 - 1/2)/(3/2 - 1/2) = 1/2\). Then \(V_0 = (\tfrac12 \cdot 50)/1 = 25\). With \(R_f = 1\), the bond position is \(B = (u \cdot 0 - d \cdot 50)/((u - d) \cdot 1) = -25\), so 25 is borrowed and repaid with no interest. The zero rate removes the discounting from the bond position, and the replication of Theorem 1.4 (a) is unchanged.
 392|           R3 · The evidence a tree needs
 393|             State what evidence would show whether a one-period tree with fixed \(u\), \(d\) and \(R_f\) matches the prices of a traded option. Name one input that the model does not have. Make no claim about any market.
 394|             Solution
 395|               The test needs traded option prices at several strikes and expiries, the stock prices they refer to, and the riskless return over each period. It also needs a rule for choosing \(u\) and \(d\) from data. The model has no such rule, and it has one pair \(u\), \(d\) for the whole period. A tree that matches one option's price is not thereby shown to match another. This lesson cites no source for these inputs, so it makes no claim about any market.
