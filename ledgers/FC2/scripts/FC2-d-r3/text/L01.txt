 A derivative price is a claim that prices are consistent with one another. The test is arbitrage: a portfolio that costs nothing or less today and cannot lose later. This lesson defines an arbitrage opportunity in a finite one-period market, states the no-arbitrage principle as a hypothesis, and proves two consequences: the law of one price, and the rule that, under no arbitrage, a portfolio which pays at least as much in every state costs at least as much today. It then gives a criterion, positive state prices (Definition 3.1), that rules arbitrage out. The lab finds arbitrage in a mispriced two-asset market and finds none in a consistent one. 
 1 Setting and the definition 
 Fix two dates, 0 (today) and 1. At date 1 exactly one of \(m\) states occurs, and no other. There are \(n\) traded assets. Asset \(j\) costs \(p_j\) at date 0, and at date 1 it pays \(X_{sj}\) in state \(s\). The vector \(p\) of prices and the matrix \(X\) with rows indexed by states and columns by assets describe the market. A portfolio is a vector \(\theta\) of holdings, one entry per asset. A negative entry is a short position : the owner has sold an asset it does not hold. The portfolio costs \(p \cdot \theta = \sum_j p_j \theta_j\) at date 0 and pays \((X\theta)_s = \sum_j X_{sj}\theta_j\) in state \(s\) at date 1. Prices and payoffs are in the units of the model, and this lesson reports no money amount in cents. 
 Definition 1.1 (arbitrage opportunity) 
 A portfolio \(\theta\) is an arbitrage opportunity when all three hold: its cost is at most zero, \(p \cdot \theta \le 0\); its payoff is at least zero in every state, \((X\theta)_s \ge 0\) for every \(s\); and it is not free of profit, meaning that either \(p \cdot \theta 0\) for some state \(s\). 
 The second and third conditions say that the portfolio never loses, and that it gains either today or in some state. A zero-cost portfolio with zero payoff in every state is not an arbitrage opportunity, because it gains nothing. A portfolio that pays money today and has a zero payoff is an arbitrage opportunity, because its cost is strictly negative. 
 Assumption A3 (no arbitrage) 
 No portfolio is an arbitrage opportunity in the market under study. Assumptions A1 and A2, which concern income and frictions, are first used in Lesson 2. This lesson uses A3 alone. 
 A3 is a no-arbitrage principle : a hypothesis about a market, not a fact that this lesson establishes. A price set can violate it, and Lab 1, Part B, constructs one. The proofs below state where they assume A3. 
 2 Dominance and the law of one price 
 Proposition 2.1 (law of one price) 
 Assume A3. If portfolios \(a\) and \(b\) have the same payoff in every state, \(X a = X b\), then they have the same cost, \(p \cdot a = p \cdot b\). 
 Proof 
 Let \(\theta = a - b\). Then \(X\theta = Xa - Xb = 0\), so the payoff of \(\theta\) is at least zero in every state. Suppose \(p \cdot \theta 
 Proposition 2.2 (payoff dominance) 
 Assume A3. If \((Xa)_s \ge (Xb)_s\) for every state \(s\), then \(p \cdot a \ge p \cdot b\). If in addition \((Xa)_s > (Xb)_s\) for every state \(s\), then \(p \cdot a > p \cdot b\). 
 Proof 
 Let \(\theta = a - b\), so \((X\theta)_s = (Xa)_s - (Xb)_s \ge 0\) for every \(s\). Suppose \(p \cdot \theta 0\), so the third condition of Definition 1.1 holds, and \(\theta\) is again an arbitrage opportunity. Hence \(p \cdot \theta > 0\). \(\blacksquare\) 
 Remark 2.3 (what the two results use) 
 Both results use A3 and nothing else: no probabilities, no preferences and no particular model of prices. The number of states is finite, and each proof is a single argument that holds for every pair of portfolios in the market. Proposition 2.2 says nothing about portfolios whose payoffs are ordered in some states but not in others. For such pairs the cost order is not determined by these results. 
 Example 2.4 (a portfolio that pays more and costs more) 
 Let state \(u\) pay the bond 1 and the stock 3, and state \(d\) pay the bond 1 and the stock 1, with prices 9/10 for the bond and 1 for the stock. The portfolio \(a = (0, 2)\), two stocks, costs 2 and pays \((6, 2)\). The portfolio \(b = (1, 1)\), one bond and one stock, costs \(19/10\) and pays \((4, 2)\). Here \(a\) pays at least as much as \(b\) in both states, so Proposition 2.2 requires \(p \cdot a \ge p \cdot b\), and \(2 \ge 19/10\) holds. The example checks the statement; it does not prove it, because the proof is the argument above. 
 3 Positive state prices exclude arbitrage 
 Definition 3.1 (strictly positive state prices) 
 A vector \(\psi = (\psi_1, \dots, \psi_m)\) with \(\psi_s > 0\) for every state \(s\) is a set of state prices for the market when \(p_j = \sum_{s} \psi_s X_{sj}\) for every asset \(j\). In matrix form, \(p = X^\top \psi\). 
 Proposition 3.2 (positive state prices exclude arbitrage) 
 If the market has a set of strictly positive state prices, then it has no arbitrage opportunity. A3 is not assumed. 
 Proof 
 Let \(\theta\) be a portfolio with \(p \cdot \theta \le 0\) and \((X\theta)_s \ge 0\) for every \(s\). Using \(p_j = \sum_s \psi_s X_{sj}\), 
 \[ p \cdot \theta = \sum_j \theta_j \sum_s \psi_s X_{sj} = \sum_s \psi_s (X\theta)_s. \] 
 Each term \(\psi_s (X\theta)_s\) is at least zero, because \(\psi_s > 0\) and \((X\theta)_s \ge 0\). So \(p \cdot \theta \ge 0\), and the assumption \(p \cdot \theta \le 0\) forces \(p \cdot \theta = 0\). Then every term is zero, and because \(\psi_s > 0\), \((X\theta)_s = 0\) for every \(s\). The portfolio has zero cost and zero payoff in every state, so it fails the third condition of Definition 1.1. It is not an arbitrage opportunity. \(\blacksquare\) 
 Remark 3.3 (the converse is not claimed) 
 Proposition 3.2 runs one way. This lesson does not claim that a market without arbitrage has strictly positive state prices. In the market of Example 3.4 they are unique, because the two state-price equations have a nonzero determinant, \(-2\). The lesson does not state uniqueness for other markets, and the lab checks state prices only for the two markets it prints. 
 Example 3.4 (a consistent market) 
 Take the market of Example 2.4 with the stock priced at \(q\) and the bond at 9/10. The state-price equations are \(\psi_u + \psi_d = 9/10\) for the bond and \(3\psi_u + \psi_d = q\) for the stock. Subtracting the first from the second gives \(2\psi_u = q - 9/10\), so \(\psi_u = (q - 9/10)/2\), and then \(\psi_d = 9/10 - \psi_u = (27/10 - q)/2\). At \(q = 1\) this gives \(\psi_u = 1/20\) and \(\psi_d = 17/20\). Both are strictly positive, so by Proposition 3.2 this market has no arbitrage opportunity, for any holdings whatever. Lab 1, Part A, prints the same two state prices.

 Remark 3.6 (the boundaries are arbitrage) 
 At \(q = 9/10\) and at \(q = 27/10\) the market admits arbitrage, although the state-price formulas give zero at those points. The boundary portfolios of the proof have zero cost, so they are arbitrage only because their payoff is strictly positive in one state. Definition 1.1 includes that case, and the open interval in Proposition 3.5 follows from it.

 """FC2 lab L01: arbitrage in a two-date, two-state price set, in exact arithmetic.
The two price sets are typed in for this lab. Holdings are integers searched over a finite box, so a count of zero
means zero portfolios in that box; the algebra in the lesson, not the box, decides the general claims. Prices and
payoffs are fractions.Fraction, so every printed value is exact. Standard library only; no network; no randomness.
Conventions: a portfolio is a pair (bond units, stock units). Its cost is the sum of holdings times prices at date 0.
Its payoff in a state is the sum of holdings times that asset's payoff in that state, at date 1. An arbitrage
opportunity is a portfolio whose cost is at most zero, whose payoff is at least zero in both states, and which is
not free of profit: its cost is strictly negative or its payoff is strictly positive in some state.
"""
from __future__ import annotations
from fractions import Fraction
from itertools import product
BOX = range(-10, 11)
SMALL = range(-2, 3)
Holding = tuple[int, int]
def payoff_of(holding: Holding, payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[Fraction]:
 """Payoff at date 1 in each state: payoffs[state][asset] times holding[asset], summed over assets."""
 return [sum((holding[j] * row[j] for j in range(len(row))), Fraction(0)) for row in payoffs]
def cost_of(holding: Holding, prices: list[Fraction]) -> Fraction:
 """Cost at date 0: prices[asset] times holding[asset], summed over assets."""
 return sum((holding[j] * prices[j] for j in range(len(prices))), Fraction(0))
def is_arbitrage(holding: Holding, prices: list[Fraction], payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> bool:
 """True when the cost is at most zero, the payoff is at least zero in every state, and the portfolio is not
 free of profit: its cost is strictly negative or its payoff is strictly positive in some state."""
 cost = cost_of(holding, prices)
 pay = payoff_of(holding, payoffs)
 if cost > 0 or any(x < 0 for x in pay):
 return False
 return cost < 0 or any(x > 0 for x in pay)
def arbitrages_in_box(prices: list[Fraction], payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> list[Holding]:
 """Every arbitrage portfolio with holdings in BOX, in lexicographic order."""
 return [h for h in product(BOX, BOX) if is_arbitrage(h, prices, payoffs)]
def state_prices(prices: list[Fraction], payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
 """Solve psi_u * X[u][j] + psi_d * X[d][j] = price_j for j = 0, 1 (Cramer's rule on the 2 by 2 system)."""
 a, b = payoffs[0][0], payoffs[1][0]
 c, d = payoffs[0][1], payoffs[1][1]
 det = a * d - b * c
 psi_u = (prices[0] * d - b * prices[1]) / det
 psi_d = (a * prices[1] - c * prices[0]) / det
 return psi_u, psi_d
def dominance_violations(prices: list[Fraction], payoffs: list[list[Fraction]]) -> tuple[int, int]:
 """Over holdings in SMALL: count pairs (a, b) with payoff(a) >= payoff(b) in both states, and the number of
 those pairs for which cost(a) < cost(b)."""
 holdings = list(product(SMALL, SMALL))
 dominating = 0
 violating = 0
 for a in holdings:
 pa = payoff_of(a, payoffs)
 for b in holdings:
 pb = payoff_of(b, payoffs)
 if all(x >= y for x, y in zip(pa, pb, strict=True)):
 dominating += 1
 if cost_of(a, prices) < cost_of(b, prices):
 violating += 1
 return dominating, violating

 4 Where the canon breaks 
 The results are exact inside their hypotheses. Four places where the reader must act: 
 A3 is a hypothesis. Definition 1.1 has no transaction costs, no borrowing limit and no short-sale limit. A price set that shows an arbitrage under the definition may not yield a profit once those frictions are priced. Lesson 2 adds A2, which removes the frictions by assumption, and states what it removes. 
 Positive state prices are sufficient, not shown necessary. Proposition 3.2 is one direction. Remark 3.3 says that the converse is not claimed here. 
 One period. The market has one trading date and one payoff date. The definition does not cover trades at intermediate dates, so a multi-period claim needs its own proof. 
 Equal payoffs, not similar ones. Proposition 2.1 needs equal payoffs state by state, so it does not apply to assets whose payoffs differ, even slightly. Proposition 2.2 applies only when one payoff is at least the other in every state. 
 5 Misconceptions 
 “Any trade that makes money in some state is an arbitrage.” 
 Definition 1.1 requires the cost to be at most zero and the payoff to be at least zero in every state. Take the stock at 1 and the bond at 9/10 from Example 3.4 and hold one stock while shorting two bonds. The cost is \(1 - 2 \cdot 9/10 = -4/5\), so money comes in today, and the payoff is \((3 - 2, 1 - 2) = (1, -1)\). The portfolio gains in state \(u\) and loses 1 in state \(d\). It is not an arbitrage opportunity, because it can lose. 
 “State prices are probabilities, so they add up to one.” 
 In the market of Example 3.4 at stock price 1, the state prices are \(1/20\) and \(17/20\), which add to \(9/10\), the price of the bond that pays 1 in every state. They are not probabilities: they sum to 9/10, not to one, and this lesson assigns no probabilities to the states. Problem P3 makes the sum explicit. 
 “No arbitrage is a fact that real markets obey.” 
 A3 is a hypothesis. Lab 1, Part B, prices the stock at 1/2 with the same payoffs as Part A. That price set has arbitrage portfolios in the search box, including a zero-cost portfolio that pays 22 and 4 in the two states. A market that has such prices is not consistent with A3.

 Mechanics 
 M1 · Testing a portfolio against Definition 1.1 
 In the market of Example 3.4, with the stock at 1, test the portfolio of 2 bonds and minus 1 stock against Definition 1.1. 
 Solution 
 The cost is \(2 \cdot 9/10 - 1 = 4/5\), which is positive, so the first condition fails. The payoff is \((2 - 3, 2 - 1) = (-1, 1)\), which is negative in state \(u\), so the second condition also fails. The portfolio is not an arbitrage opportunity on either count. 
 M2 · State prices for a cheaper bond 
 Find the state prices of the market with the bond at 4/5 and the stock at 1, with the payoffs of Example 3.4. Say whether Proposition 3.2 rules out arbitrage. 
 Solution 
 The equations are \(\psi_u + \psi_d = 4/5\) and \(3\psi_u + \psi_d = 1\). Subtracting gives \(2\psi_u = 1/5\), so \(\psi_u = 1/10\), and then \(\psi_d = 4/5 - 1/10 = 7/10\). Both are strictly positive, so Proposition 3.2 excludes arbitrage in this market.

 In the market with the bond at 9/10 and the stock at 1/2, find every holding \((x, y)\) of bonds and stocks with zero cost, and say for which of them the payoff is strictly positive in both states.

Their payoff is \(t(22, 4)\), which is strictly positive in both states exactly when \(t > 0\). Lab 1, Part B, prints the case \(t = 1\).

 P3 · State prices are not probabilities 
 In the market of Example 3.4 with \(q = 1\), show that the state prices sum to the bond's price, and that they are therefore not probabilities in the sense of summing to one. 
 Solution 
 The state prices are \(\psi_u = 1/20\) and \(\psi_d = 17/20\). Their sum is \(18/20 = 9/10\), which is the price of the bond. The bond pays 1 in every state, so its price is the sum of the state prices, and that sum is 9/10, not 1. A probability would sum to one. Here the sum is the price of the bond, which pays a sure dollar at date 1, and that price is 9/10.

 E2 · Reading the arbitrage count in Lab 1, Part B

 No. Scaling an arbitrage opportunity by any positive real number keeps the cost at most zero, the payoff at least zero, and the profit strict, so the market has infinitely many arbitrage portfolios. The count 30 counts only the integer holdings from minus 10 to 10 of the two assets. It is a fact about that box.

 In the zero-cost portfolio of the market with bond 9/10 and stock 1/2, what is the holding of the bond, in the form (bond, stock) = (x, 9)? 
 Answer 
 Zero cost requires \(9x/10 + 9/2 = 0\), so \(x = -5\). The portfolio is \((-5, 9)\). 
 Under what condition do strictly positive state prices exist and exclude arbitrage, in the market of Example 3.4? 
 Options 
 Only when the stock price equals 1 
 When the stock price lies strictly between 9/10 and 27/10 
 When the stock price is at least 27/10 
 Proposition 3.5: the state prices are \((q - 9/10)/2\) and \((27/10 - q)/2\), both positive exactly in that open interval. 
 In the market of Example 3.4, at what stock price does the upper end of the no-arbitrage range fall? 
 Answer 
 The no-arbitrage range is open at \(27/10 = 2.7\). At that price the portfolio \((3, -1)\) costs zero and pays \((0, 2)\), so it is an arbitrage opportunity, by Proposition 3.5. 
 Why does the zero count in Lab 1, Part A, not by itself prove that the market has no arbitrage? 
 Options 
 Because the state prices are negative 
 Because the search covers finitely many holdings 
 Because the search uses fractions 
 The box contains finitely many holdings. Proposition 3.2 gives the all-holdings result, from the positive state prices that Part A prints. 
 In the market of Example 3.4 at stock price 1, what is the cost of one bond and minus two stocks, in the model's units? 
 Answer 
 The cost is \(9/10 - 2 \cdot 1 = -11/10 = -1.1\). The cost is negative, so the portfolio brings in money today. 
 Is the no-arbitrage principle a fact that every real market obeys? 
 Options 
 Yes, by the definition of a price 
 No, it is a hypothesis, and Lab 1, Part B, violates it 
 Yes, for every payoff matrix 
 A3 is stated as a hypothesis in Section 1. The mispriced market of Lab 1, Part B, has arbitrage portfolios in the box. 
 Under Definition 1.1, is a portfolio that costs \(-1\) and pays 0 in every state an arbitrage opportunity? 
 Options 
 Yes, because its cost is strictly negative 
 No, because its payoff is zero 
 No, because it costs money 
 The definition has three conditions: cost at most zero, payoff at least zero, and either strictly negative cost or a strictly positive payoff. The strictly negative cost meets the last one.
