Partial Derivatives
Uses: the derivative of a function of one variable (Lesson L04), the product and chain rules (Lesson L05), the central difference and its error (Lesson L13), and the open sets, limits and continuity of Lesson L15
Introduces: partial derivative

Dlore
Xrefuted
A sensitivity asks how a value changes when one input moves and the others stay put. Holding the other inputs fixed is the partial derivative. It is a one-variable derivative, so the rules of Lessons L04 and L05 apply to it directly. The partial derivative also has a catch: it describes motion along one coordinate line at a time. This lesson defines it, computes it, shows that the central difference of Lesson L13 gives it with the same error law, and shows with an example that existing partial derivatives do not make a function continuous.
1Definition and first computations
        Definition 1.1 (partial derivative)
Let \(f\) be a real function on an open set \(U\) in \(\R^n\), let \(a \in U\), and let \(e_i\) be the \(i\)th standard basis vector. The partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to \(x_i\) at \(a\) is
\[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}(a) = \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{f(a + t e_i) - f(a)}{t},\]
when this limit exists. The other coordinates of \(a\) are held fixed.
        Remark 1.2 (a partial is a one-variable derivative)
Let \(\varphi(t) = f(a + t e_i)\), a function of one real variable defined for \(t\) near \(0\), because \(U\) is open. The quotient in Definition 1.1 is the difference quotient of \(\varphi\) at \(0\). So \(\partial f/\partial x_i(a)\) exists exactly when \(\varphi\) is differentiable at \(0\), and then it equals \(\varphi'(0)\). Every rule of Lesson L05 for \(\varphi\) therefore applies to the partial, with the other variables treated as constants.
Assumption 1. The derivative of \(\exp\) is \(\exp\). This page assumes it and does not prove it. Example 1.3, Lab 1, Problems M2, E1 and E3 and the key-results row for Example 1.3 use it, and each says so.
        Example 1.3 (the function of Lab 1)
Let \(F(x, y) = x^2 y + e^{xy}\). Under Assumption 1, holding \(y\) fixed and differentiating in \(x\) with the product and chain rules gives \(F_x(x, y) = 2xy + y\,e^{xy}\), since \(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}e^{xy} = y\,e^{xy}\). Holding \(x\) fixed gives \(F_y(x, y) = x^2 + x\,e^{xy}\). At \((1, 2)\), \(F_x = 4 + 2e^2\) and \(F_y = 1 + e^2\). With \(e^2 = 7.389056099\), these are \(18.778112198\) and \(8.389056099\). Lab 1 computes the same two numbers by central difference.
2A central difference in one variable
Since a partial is a one-variable derivative by Remark 1.2, the central difference of Lesson L13 applies to it. The next result gives its error with the variables other than \(x_i\) held at their values.
        Proposition 2.1 (central difference of a partial)
Let \(f\) be defined on an open set \(U\) in \(\R^2\), let \(a = (a_1, a_2) \in U\), and let \(h > 0\) be such that the closed segment from \((a_1 - h, a_2)\) to \((a_1 + h, a_2)\) lies in \(U\). Assume that the function \(t \mapsto f(t, a_2)\) has a continuous third derivative on \([a_1 - h, a_1 + h]\). Then the central difference \(C_h^{(1)} f(a) = \bigl(f(a_1 + h, a_2) - f(a_1 - h, a_2)\bigr)/(2h)\) satisfies
\[C_h^{(1)} f(a) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a) = \frac{h^2}{6}\, \frac{\partial^3 f}{\partial x^3}(\xi, a_2)\]
for some \(\xi\) with \(a_1 - h < \xi < a_1 + h\).
        Proof
Let \(\varphi(t) = f(t, a_2)\). By Remark 1.2 and Definition 1.1, \(\varphi'(a_1) = \partial f/\partial x(a)\), and \(C_h^{(1)} f(a)\) is the central difference \(C_h \varphi(a_1)\) of Lesson L13. The hypothesis on \(\varphi\) is the hypothesis of Theorem 2.2 of Lesson L13, applied at the point \(a_1\). That theorem gives \(\xi\) with \(a_1 - h < \xi < a_1 + h\) and \(C_h\varphi(a_1) - \varphi'(a_1) = \tfrac{h^2}{6}\varphi'''(\xi)\). Since \(\varphi'''(\xi) = \partial^3 f/\partial x^3(\xi, a_2)\), the claim follows. \(\blacksquare\)
Proposition 2.1 is the one-variable result applied to \(\varphi\), and it inherits the rounding model of Lesson L13. The lab below takes the step \(h = 10^{-5}\). For the function \(F\) at \((2, 1)\), Problem E1 gives a truncation part of order \(10^{-10}\) and a rounding part of order \(10^{-10}\), so neither part dominates there.
3Existing partials do not give continuity
Partial derivatives look along the two coordinate lines through a point in \(\R^2\). A function can have both of them at a point and still behave badly along other paths. Example 3.4 of Lesson L15 is the function \(g(x, y) = xy/(x^2 + y^2)\) with \(g(0, 0) = 0\).
        Proposition 3.1 (both partials exist at the origin, and \(g\) is not continuous there)
Let \(g(x, y) = xy/(x^2 + y^2)\) for \((x, y) \ne (0, 0)\) and \(g(0, 0) = 0\). Then \(\partial g/\partial x(0, 0)\) and \(\partial g/\partial y(0, 0)\) both exist and equal \(0\), while \(g\) is not continuous at \((0, 0)\).
        Proof
For \(t \ne 0\), \(g(t, 0) = 0\) and \(g(0, t) = 0\). So \((g(0 + t, 0) - g(0, 0))/t = 0\) and \((g(0, 0 + t) - g(0, 0))/t = 0\) for every \(t \ne 0\). Both quotients are the constant \(0\), so both limits exist and equal \(0\). Non-continuity is Example 3.4 of Lesson L15, which uses the sequence \((1/k, 1/k)\) with \(g = 1/2\) and Corollary 3.2 of that lesson. \(\blacksquare\)
Proposition 3.1 shows that the existence of all partial derivatives at a point does not imply continuity there. Lesson L18 (Theorem 4.1) adds the hypothesis that makes the partials control the function: the partials exist on a ball about the point, and each partial is continuous at the point.

"""M2 lab L16: partial derivatives by central difference, against their closed forms.
Standard library only. The function is F(x, y) = x^2 y + exp(x y). Its partial derivatives are
F_x = 2 x y + y exp(x y) and F_y = x^2 + x exp(x y). For each partial the lab takes a central
difference in that variable alone, with step h = 10^-5, at 12 grid points (x in {0.5, 1, 1.5, 2},
y in {-1, 0, 1}), and reports whether the largest absolute error is below 1e-6. The lab also tests the axis
quotients of g(x, y) = x y / (x^2 + y^2) at the origin, where g(0, 0) = 0. Nothing is random.
"""
from __future__ import annotations
import math
from collections.abc import Callable
H = 1e-5
def big_f(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """F(x, y) = x^2 y + exp(x y)."""
    return x * x * y + math.exp(x * y)
def fx_closed(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """The closed-form partial derivative of F with respect to x: 2 x y + y exp(x y)."""
    return 2.0 * x * y + y * math.exp(x * y)
def fy_closed(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """The closed-form partial derivative of F with respect to y: x^2 + x exp(x y)."""
    return x * x + x * math.exp(x * y)
def partial_x(fn: Callable[[float, float], float], x: float, y: float, h: float) -> float:
    """Central difference in x with y held fixed: (fn(x + h, y) - fn(x - h, y)) / (2 h)."""
    return (fn(x + h, y) - fn(x - h, y)) / (2.0 * h)
def partial_y(fn: Callable[[float, float], float], x: float, y: float, h: float) -> float:
    """Central difference in y with x held fixed: (fn(x, y + h) - fn(x, y - h)) / (2 h)."""
    return (fn(x, y + h) - fn(x, y - h)) / (2.0 * h)
def g(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """g(x, y) = x y / (x^2 + y^2) for (x, y) != (0, 0), and g(0, 0) = 0."""
    if x == 0.0 and y == 0.0:
        return 0.0
    return x * y / (x * x + y * y)
def grid() -> list[tuple[float, float]]:
    """The 12 grid points with x in {0.5, 1, 1.5, 2} and y in {-1, 0, 1}."""
    return [(x, y) for x in (0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0) for y in (-1.0, 0.0, 1.0)]
def main() -> None:
    worst_x = 0.0
    worst_y = 0.0
    for x, y in grid():
        worst_x = max(worst_x, abs(partial_x(big_f, x, y, H) - fx_closed(x, y)))
        worst_y = max(worst_y, abs(partial_y(big_f, x, y, H) - fy_closed(x, y)))
    print(f"grid points: {len(grid())}")
    print(f"max error in F_x by central difference below 1e-6: {worst_x < 1e-6}")
    print(f"max error in F_y by central difference below 1e-6: {worst_y < 1e-6}")
    x0, y0 = 1.0, 2.0
    print(f"F_x(1, 2) by central difference: {partial_x(big_f, x0, y0, H):.6f}")
    print(f"F_x(1, 2) closed form: {fx_closed(x0, y0):.6f}")
    print(f"F_y(1, 2) by central difference: {partial_y(big_f, x0, y0, H):.6f}")
    print(f"F_y(1, 2) closed form: {fy_closed(x0, y0):.6f}")
    for t in (1e-3, 1e-6):
        along_x = (g(t, 0.0) - g(0.0, 0.0)) / t
        along_y = (g(0.0, t) - g(0.0, 0.0)) / t
        print(f"g quotients at the origin, t = {t:.0e}: along x {along_x:.1f}, along y {along_y:.1f}")
main()

4Where the canon breaks
A partial derivative is a precise object with a precise convention, and four points separate it from the everyday phrase "the effect of x on f".
The partial depends on the other variables held fixed. Take \(f(x, y) = y\) with coordinates \((x, y)\). Then \(\partial f/\partial x = 0\). Change the coordinates to \((x, s)\) with \(s = x + y\). Then \(y = s - x\), so \(f = s - x\), and holding \(s\) fixed gives \(\partial f/\partial x = -1\). The same name \(\partial f/\partial x\) denotes two different numbers, one for each choice of the held variable.
The partial is not the derivative along a path. The next misconception shows that \(\partial f/\partial x\) and the derivative of \(f(x, x)\) differ, because the second moves \(y\) as well.
Existence of partials is weaker than differentiability. Proposition 3.1 gives a point where both partials exist and the function is not continuous. Lesson L18 requires more, and Lesson L17 defines the directional derivative, which uses lines in every direction.
The step of a numerical partial is a choice. The lab uses \(h = 10^{-5}\), and Lesson L13 shows that a different step gives a different error. A printed partial is therefore an estimate with an error set by the step and the rounding, not the partial itself.
5Misconceptions
“If both partial derivatives exist at a point, the function is continuous there.”
Proposition 3.1 gives a function with both partials equal to \(0\) at the origin and no continuity there. The partials use the two axes, and the function's values along the diagonal are \(1/2\) at every \((1/k, 1/k)\).
“The partial \(\partial f/\partial x\) is the derivative of \(f(x, x)\) with \(y\) treated as a constant.”
Take \(f(x, y) = xy\). Then \(\partial f/\partial x = y\). The function \(\varphi(x) = f(x, x) = x^2\) has derivative \(\varphi'(1) = 2\), while \(\partial f/\partial x(1, 1) = 1\). The partial holds \(y\) fixed, and moving along the diagonal also changes \(y\).
“A partial derivative at a point depends on the values of \(f\) across the whole plane.”
Definition 1.1 uses the values on the line through \(a\) in direction \(e_i\), at points \(a + t e_i\) for small \(t\). Values far from that line do not enter the limit, though they can change the function's behaviour elsewhere.

Mechanics
            M1 · A polynomial partial
For \(f(x, y) = x^3 + 2xy\), find \(\partial f/\partial x\) at \((1, 1)\).
            Solution
Holding \(y\) fixed, \(\partial f/\partial x = 3x^2 + 2y\). At \((1, 1)\) this is \(3 + 2 = 5\).
            M2 · A partial with an exponential
For \(f(x, y) = e^{x} y^2\), find \(\partial f/\partial y\) at \((0, 1)\).
            Solution
Under Assumption 1 (the derivative of \(\exp\) is \(\exp\)), holding \(x\) fixed, \(\partial f/\partial y = 2 e^{x} y\). At \((0, 1)\) this is \(2 e^0 \cdot 1 = 2\).
            M3 · A quotient partial
For \(f(x, y) = x/y\) with \(y \ne 0\), find \(\partial f/\partial x\) at \((2, 4)\) and \(\partial f/\partial y\) at \((2, 4)\).
            Solution
Holding \(y\) fixed, \(\partial f/\partial x = 1/y = 1/4\). Holding \(x\) fixed, \(\partial f/\partial y = -x/y^2 = -2/16 = -1/8\).
Proof
            P1 · The central difference of a quadratic in x is exact
Let \(f(x, y) = x^2 y\). Show that the central difference in \(x\) of Proposition 2.1 equals \(\partial f/\partial x\) exactly, for every \(h > 0\), and say which term of the error formula vanishes.
            Solution
With \(y = a_2\) fixed, \(\varphi(t) = t^2 a_2\). The central difference is \((\varphi(a_1 + h) - \varphi(a_1 - h))/(2h) = a_2\bigl((a_1 + h)^2 - (a_1 - h)^2\bigr)/(2h) = a_2 \cdot 4a_1 h/(2h) = 2a_1 a_2\). The partial is \(\partial f/\partial x = 2xy\), which at \(a\) is \(2a_1 a_2\). The difference is zero. In Proposition 2.1 the third derivative \(\varphi'''\) is zero, so the error term \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}\varphi'''(\xi)\) vanishes.
            P2 · The axis quotients for \(g\)
Prove that \(\partial g/\partial y(0, 0) = 0\) for \(g\) of Proposition 3.1, using only Definition 1.1.
            Solution
By Definition 1.1, \(\partial g/\partial y(0, 0) = \lim_{t \to 0} (g(0, t) - g(0, 0))/t\). For \(t \ne 0\), \(g(0, t) = 0 \cdot t/(0 + t^2) = 0\), and \(g(0, 0) = 0\). So each quotient is \(0/t = 0\), and the limit is \(0\).
            P3 · A partial that does not exist
Let \(k(x, y) = |x|\). Show that \(\partial k/\partial x(0, 0)\) does not exist, and that \(\partial k/\partial y(0, 0)\) exists and equals \(0\).
            Solution
The quotient \((k(t, 0) - k(0, 0))/t = |t|/t\) equals \(1\) for \(t > 0\) and \(-1\) for \(t < 0\). It has different one-sided limits, so \(\lim_{t \to 0}\) does not exist and the partial in \(x\) does not exist. For \(y\), \(k(0, t) = |0| = 0\) for every \(t\), so the quotient is \(0\) for every \(t \ne 0\), and the partial in \(y\) is \(0\).
Empirical
            E1 · The central difference at a grid point (Lab 1)
Lab 1 reports that the largest central-difference error in \(F_x\) on the 12 grid points is below \(10^{-6}\). Using Proposition 2.1 and the third \(x\)-derivative of \(F\), bound the truncation part at \(x = 2\), \(y = 1\), and say whether the reported bound is consistent with it.
            Solution
Under Assumption 1, compute \(F_{xx} = 2y + y^2 e^{xy}\) and \(F_{xxx} = y^3 e^{xy}\). At \((x, y) = (2, 1)\) on the segment \(x \in [2 - h, 2 + h]\) with \(h = 10^{-5}\), \(F_{xxx} = e^{xy} \le e^{2.00001}\), about \(7.39\). The truncation part is \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}F_{xxx}(\xi, 1)\), at most \(\tfrac{10^{-10}}{6} \cdot 7.39 \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-10}\). This is far below \(10^{-6}\), so the reported bound is consistent with the truncation part. The rounding part of Proposition 3.2 of Lesson L13 is \(\delta/h\), where \(\delta\) is half the spacing of doubles near \(F(2, 1) = 4 + e^2 \approx 11.39\), which is about \(8.9 \times 10^{-16}\). So the rounding part is about \(8.9 \times 10^{-11}\), also below \(10^{-6}\).
            E2 · What the axis lines show (Lab 1)
Lab 1 prints \(0.0\) for the axis quotients of \(g\) at \(t = 10^{-3}\) and \(t = 10^{-6}\). State what these two printed values establish, and what they do not.
            Solution
They establish that the quotients are zero at those two steps, which Proposition 3.1 proves for every \(t \ne 0\). They do not establish the limit at the origin by themselves: a limit needs the quotient for every \(t\) near \(0\), and the lab checks two values of \(t\). The printed values also say nothing about continuity, which Proposition 3.1 handles through Lesson L15.
            E3 · Mixed second partials of \(F\) (Lab 1 closed forms)
Compute \(F_{xy}\) and \(F_{yx}\) for \(F(x, y) = x^2 y + e^{xy}\) from the formulas of Example 1.3, and check that they agree at \((1, 2)\).
            Solution
Under Assumption 1, differentiate \(F_x = 2xy + y e^{xy}\) in \(y\): \(F_{xy} = 2x + e^{xy} + xy\,e^{xy}\). Differentiate \(F_y = x^2 + x e^{xy}\) in \(x\): \(F_{yx} = 2x + e^{xy} + xy\,e^{xy}\). They are the same expression. At \((1, 2)\) both are \(2 + e^2 + 2e^2 = 2 + 3e^2 = 24.167168\). The equality is checked here for this \(F\); the general condition is taken up in Lesson L19 (Theorem 2.1 there, on the symmetry of mixed partials).
Research
            R1 · Partials and a diagonal direction
For \(g\) of Proposition 3.1 and the unit vector \(u = (1, 1)/\sqrt2\), show that the limit \(\lim_{t \to 0}(g(tu) - g(0))/t\) does not exist, although both partial derivatives at the origin exist.
            Solution
On the diagonal \(tu = (t/\sqrt2, t/\sqrt2)\) with \(t \ne 0\), \(g = (t^2/2)/(t^2) = 1/2\). So \((g(tu) - g(0))/t = (1/2)/t\), which is unbounded as \(t \to 0\), and the limit does not exist. The partials exist because they use the axes, where \(g\) is zero. This shows that a directional derivative is a further condition, which Lesson L17 treats.
            R2 · Choosing the held variable
Let \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) and consider the coordinates \((u, v)\) with \(x = u + v\) and \(y = u - v\). Compute \(\partial f/\partial u\) at \((u, v) = (1, 0)\) in the new coordinates, and explain why it differs from \(\partial f/\partial x\) at \((x, y) = (1, 1)\).
            Solution
In the new coordinates \(f = (u + v)^2 + (u - v)^2 = 2u^2 + 2v^2\), so \(\partial f/\partial u = 4u\), which is \(4\) at \(u = 1\). In the old coordinates, at \((1, 1)\), \(\partial f/\partial x = 2x = 2\), since holding \(y = 1\) fixed. The two partials are of different functions in different coordinate systems, and the held variable differs, so they need not agree. The number is a property of the coordinates as well as of \(f\).
            R3 · The error budget of a partial in a program
A program computes \(\partial f/\partial x\) by central difference with \(h = 10^{-5}\), where its stored values of \(f\) have evaluation error at most \(\delta = 10^{-15}\) and \(\max|\partial^3 f/\partial x^3| \le 20\) on the step. Bound the total error using Proposition 2.1 of this lesson and Proposition 3.2 of Lesson L13, and say which hypothesis of Proposition 3.2 of Lesson L13 the rounding of the points can violate.
            Solution
The truncation part is at most \(\tfrac{h^2}{6}\cdot 20 = \tfrac{10^{-10}}{6}\cdot 20 = 3.33 \times 10^{-10}\). The rounding part of the central quotient is at most \(\delta/h = 10^{-15}/10^{-5} = 10^{-10}\), by Proposition 3.2 of Lesson L13. The total bound is \(4.33 \times 10^{-10}\). Proposition 3.2 of Lesson L13 assumes that the arithmetic after the evaluations is exact, and that the three evaluations are taken at the exact points \(a_1 - h\), \(a_1\) and \(a_1 + h\); the third hypothesis, the exactness of the evaluation points, is the one that the rounding of \(a_1 \pm h\) can violate. A point \(a_1 \pm h\) is in general not stored exactly, and that rounding changes the step, as Section 5 of Lesson L13 explains.

               \(x\)
               \(y\)
               \(x + y\)
Holding \(y\) fixed, \(xy\) is linear in \(x\) with slope \(y\).
For \(f(x, y) = x/y\) at \((2, 4)\), what is \(\partial f/\partial x\)?
            Answer 
Holding \(y = 4\) fixed, \(\partial f/\partial x = 1/y = 1/4\) (Problem M3).
Why is the central difference in \(x\) of Proposition 2.1 exact for \(f = x^2 y\)?
            Options
               Because \(f\) is zero at the origin
               Because the third \(x\)-derivative of \(f\) is zero, so the error term vanishes
               Because the step is zero
Problem P1 shows the difference quotient equals \(2xy\) exactly for this function.
In Lab 1, what do the axis quotients of \(g\) at the origin print, and why?
            Options
               \(0.5\), because of the diagonal
               \(0.0\), because \(g\) is zero on both axes
               A value that depends on the step, because of rounding
Proposition 3.1: for \(t \ne 0\), \(g(t, 0) = g(0, t) = 0\), so both quotients are zero.
For \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) in the coordinates \(x = u + v\), \(y = u - v\), what is \(\partial f/\partial u\) at \(u = 1\), \(v = 0\)? Use the coordinate form \(f = 2u^2 + 2v^2\).
            Answer 
\(\partial f/\partial u = 4u = 4\) at \(u = 1\) (Problem R2).
Which statement about \(\partial f/\partial x\) and the derivative of \(f(x, x)\) is correct?
            Options
               They are always equal
               They differ in general, because the derivative along the diagonal also moves \(y\)
               The partial is the derivative of \(f(x, x)\)
Misconception 2 gives \(f = xy\): the partial is \(y\), while the diagonal derivative is \(2x\).
