Where numerical sensitivities fail
Uses: finite-difference derivative approximation (L13), derivative (L04), Taylor's theorem with remainder (L08)
Introduces: none in the concept index (cancellation, defined in Section 4, and the error hypothesis, Definition 4.1, are prose definitions with no index entry)

Bpublished, not settled

Dlore
Xrefuted
A sensitivity computed by a finite difference depends on the step it uses. A step that is too large misses the curvature, a step that is too small loses digits to subtraction, and a function with a kink (a corner where the left and right slopes differ) gives a finite difference that looks stable but has no derivative behind it. This lesson bounds the truncation error (the gap between a quotient and the derivative that a finite step leaves) of the forward and central quotients, derives the step that balances it against round-off, the evaluation error of Section 4, and shows a kink where the quotients return values with no derivative behind them.
1Difference quotients and the definition of the derivative
For a function \(f\) of one variable, a point \(x\), and a step \(h > 0\), write the forward quotient \(D^+_h f(x) = (f(x + h) - f(x))/h\), the backward quotient \(D^-_h f(x) = (f(x) - f(x - h))/h\), and the central quotient \(D^0_h f(x) = (f(x + h) - f(x - h))/(2h)\). These are the finite-difference approximations of Lesson L13. The derivative of Lesson L04 is the limit of the difference quotient over both signs of \(h\), when that limit exists. The forward quotient can have a limit as \(h \to 0^+\) where the derivative does not exist (Theorem 2.1 below). Whenever the derivative exists, the central quotient converges to it too, because \(D^0_h f(x)\) is the average of \(D^+_h f(x)\) and \(D^-_h f(x)\), and both tend to \(f'(x)\) as \(h \to 0^+\). The converse fails: Theorem 2.1 gives a central quotient of \(|x|\) at \(0\) equal to \(0\) at every step, so that limit exists while the derivative does not.
2A kink returns values with no derivative behind them
Theorem 2.1 (the quotients of \(|x|\) at its kink)
Let \(f(x) = |x|\). For every step \(h > 0\), \(D^+_h f(0) = 1\), \(D^-_h f(0) = -1\) and \(D^0_h f(0) = 0\). The function \(f\) has no derivative at \(0\).
Proof
For \(h > 0\), \(D^+_h f(0) = (|h| - 0)/h = 1\), and \(D^-_h f(0) = (0 - |{-h}|)/h = -1\). Their average gives \(D^0_h f(0) = (|h| - |{-h}|)/(2h) = 0\). Since the forward quotient is \(1\) for every \(h > 0\) and the backward quotient is \(-1\) for every \(h > 0\), the limit of the difference quotient as \(h \to 0\) does not exist, because the two one-sided limits differ. Therefore \(f\) has no derivative at \(0\) by the definition of Lesson L04. \(\blacksquare\)
The point of Theorem 2.1 is that a finite-difference value at a fixed step is a number, and a number from a finite step does not show that a limit exists. Here the forward quotient is \(1\) at every step, which looks stable, and the derivative does not exist. Lab 1 Part A prints these values at six steps.
3Truncation error
Proposition 3.1 (truncation of the forward quotient)
Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), let \(f\) be twice differentiable on an open interval containing \([x, x + h]\), with \(f''\) bounded on \([x, x + h]\) by \(M_2\). Then \(|D^+_h f(x) - f'(x)| \le \tfrac12 M_2\,h\).
Proof
The Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder of order one (Lesson L08) gives \(\xi \in (x, x + h)\) with \(f(x + h) = f(x) + h f'(x) + \tfrac12 h^2 f''(\xi)\). Dividing the difference \(f(x+h) - f(x)\) by \(h\) gives \(D^+_h f(x) - f'(x) = \tfrac12 h f''(\xi)\), and \(|f''(\xi)| \le M_2\) gives the bound. \(\blacksquare\)
Proposition 3.2 (truncation of the central quotient)
Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), let \(f\) be three times differentiable on an open interval containing \([x - h, x + h]\), with \(f'''\) bounded on \([x - h, x + h]\) by \(M_3\). Then \(|D^0_h f(x) - f'(x)| \le \tfrac16 M_3\,h^2\).
Proof
The Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder of order two gives \(\xi_1 \in (x, x+h)\) and \(\xi_2 \in (x-h, x)\) with
\[f(x + h) = f(x) + hf'(x) + \tfrac12 h^2 f''(x) + \tfrac16 h^3 f'''(\xi_1),\]
\[f(x - h) = f(x) - hf'(x) + \tfrac12 h^2 f''(x) - \tfrac16 h^3 f'''(\xi_2).\]
Subtracting, \(f(x+h) - f(x-h) = 2hf'(x) + \tfrac16 h^3\bigl(f'''(\xi_1) + f'''(\xi_2)\bigr)\). Dividing by \(2h\), \(D^0_h f(x) - f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{12}h^2\bigl(f'''(\xi_1) + f'''(\xi_2)\bigr)\), whose absolute value is at most \(\tfrac16 M_3 h^2\). \(\blacksquare\)
4Round-off and the step that balances the errors
Round-off is not in the mathematics of Sections 2 and 3, because those proofs use exact values of \(f\). A computer evaluates \(f\) with an error. Subtracting two nearly equal computed values loses digits; this loss is called cancellation. The model below states the evaluation error as a hypothesis.
Definition 4.1 (the error hypothesis)
Let \(\tilde f\) denote the computed value of \(f\). The hypothesis is that \(|\tilde f(t) - f(t)| \le \delta\) for every \(t\) at which the quotient is evaluated, for a stated \(\delta > 0\). The model counts only the error in evaluating \(f\). It ignores the rounding of the subtraction and the division, and it is a hypothesis about the arithmetic, not a measurement.
Proposition 4.2 (the computed forward quotient, under Definition 4.1)
Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), under the hypotheses of Proposition 3.1 and Definition 4.1, with \(M_2 > 0\), \(|\tilde D^+_h f(x) - f'(x)| \le \tfrac12 M_2 h + 2\delta/h\). The bound is minimized at \(h^* = 2\sqrt{\delta/M_2}\), where it equals \(2\sqrt{M_2\,\delta}\).
Proof
The computed quotient is \((\tilde f(x+h) - \tilde f(x))/h\). Its distance from \(D^+_h f(x)\) is at most \((\delta + \delta)/h = 2\delta/h\), and Proposition 3.1 bounds the distance of \(D^+_h f(x)\) from \(f'(x)\) by \(\tfrac12 M_2 h\). The triangle inequality gives the bound. For \(g(h) = Ah + B/h\) with \(A, B > 0\), \(g'(h) = A - B/h^2\) vanishes at \(h = \sqrt{B/A}\), where \(g\) is minimal, and \(g = 2\sqrt{AB}\) there. With \(A = M_2/2\) and \(B = 2\delta\), \(h^* = \sqrt{4\delta/M_2} = 2\sqrt{\delta/M_2}\) and \(g(h^*) = 2\sqrt{M_2\,\delta}\). \(\blacksquare\)
Proposition 4.3 (the computed central quotient, under Definition 4.1)
Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), under the hypotheses of Proposition 3.2 and Definition 4.1, with \(M_3 > 0\), \(|\tilde D^0_h f(x) - f'(x)| \le \tfrac16 M_3 h^2 + \delta/h\). The bound is minimized at \(h^* = (3\delta/M_3)^{1/3}\).
Proof
The computed quotient differs from \(D^0_h f(x)\) by at most \((\delta + \delta)/(2h) = \delta/h\). Proposition 3.2 gives the truncation bound. Put \(g(h) = Ch^2 + B/h\) with \(C = M_3/6\) and \(B = \delta\). Then \(g'(h) = 2Ch - B/h^2\) vanishes when \(h^3 = B/(2C) = 3\delta/M_3\), which is the stated \(h^*\). \(\blacksquare\)
These are bounds on the error. The step they name minimizes the bound, not the error itself, so the actual minimizer of the error can differ from it. Remark 4.4 shows what that difference can look like.
Remark 4.4 (a bound is not the error)
Proposition 4.2 gives an upper bound, and the true error at \(h^*\) is only guaranteed to be at most the bound. The truncation and round-off errors can have opposite signs, so the true error at a given step can be smaller than the bound. Lab 1 Part B prints the true errors on a grid of decade steps, and it does not measure \(\delta\) or \(M_2\).
Assumption 4.1 (derivative of the exponential). For every real \(x\), \(\tfrac{d}{dx}e^x = e^x\). This page takes the fact from the prerequisite calculus and does not prove it. The lab's predicted derivative at \(x_0 = 1\), the value \(e\), is stated under this assumption.

5Where the canon breaks
The error statements in this lesson are bounds or models, and each is conditional on its stated hypotheses. The smooth-function results need the stated differentiability, and the round-off results need Definition 4.1.
A stable finite difference does not certify a derivative. Theorem 2.1 gives the same forward quotient at every step for \(|x|\), and no derivative exists. A check on a function must test the function's smoothness at the point, not only the stability of its quotients.
The optimal step depends on quantities the user does not know. Proposition 4.2 needs \(M_2\), and Proposition 4.3 needs \(M_3\). They are bounds on higher derivatives over an interval. The page's prediction takes \(M_2 \approx e\) from the known function \(e^x\) (Lab 1 computes no \(M_2\)), and Lab 1 Part B finds the grid minimum by a sweep over decades; the page gives no method for estimating \(M_2\) or \(M_3\) from data.
Subtraction of close numbers is the cancellation. The round-off term \(2\delta/h\) is the cancellation in \(\tilde f(x+h) - \tilde f(x)\). An algebraic rewrite can remove the subtraction, as Problem P3 shows for \(\sqrt{1+x} - 1\).
6Misconceptions
“A smaller step always gives a better finite-difference derivative.”
Proposition 4.2 bounds the error by \(\tfrac12 M_2 h + 2\delta/h\), which increases as \(h\) falls below \(h^*\). Lab 1 Part B shows the forward error rising from about \(6.6 \times 10^{-9}\) at \(10^{-8}\) to \(2.15 \times 10^{-7}\) at \(10^{-9}\).
“The central quotient gives the derivative of \(|x|\) at \(0\).”
Theorem 2.1 shows \(D^0_h f(0) = 0\) for every \(h\), while the forward quotient is \(1\) and the backward quotient is \(-1\). The central quotient averages two different one-sided slopes and returns their mean, and that value is not a derivative of \(|x|\) at \(0\), which does not exist.
“A finite-difference value that does not change with the step proves the function is differentiable.”
The forward quotient of \(|x|\) at \(0\) is \(1\) at every step from \(10^{-1}\) to \(10^{-6}\) (Lab 1 Part A), and \(|x|\) has no derivative at \(0\) by Theorem 2.1. Stability across steps is a property of the quotients, not evidence of differentiability.

Mechanics
M1 · Three quotients for \(x^2\)
For \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(x = 1\), compute \(D^+_h f(1)\), \(D^-_h f(1)\) and \(D^0_h f(1)\) exactly, and state the error of each.
Solution
\(D^+_h f(1) = ((1+h)^2 - 1)/h = 2 + h\), so the error is \(h\). \(D^-_h f(1) = (1 - (1-h)^2)/h = 2 - h\), with error \(-h\). \(D^0_h f(1) = ((1+h)^2 - (1-h)^2)/(2h) = 4h/(2h) = 2\), with error zero. The central quotient is exact for a quadratic.
M2 · The central quotient of \(|x|\)
For \(f(x) = |x|\) at \(x = 0\) and \(h = 0.5\), compute the central quotient and the forward quotient.
Solution
The central quotient is \((|0.5| - |{-0.5}|)/(2 \cdot 0.5) = 0\). The forward quotient is \((|0.5| - 0)/0.5 = 1\). Both agree with Theorem 2.1.
M3 · A forward quotient of a cubic
For \(f(x) = x^3\) at \(x = 2\) and \(h = 0.1\), compute \(D^+_h f(2)\) and its error against \(f'(2) = 12\).
Solution
\(D^+_h f(2) = (2.1^3 - 2^3)/0.1 = (9.261 - 8)/0.1 = 12.61\), so the error is \(0.61\). Proposition 3.1 gives \(\tfrac12 M_2 h\) with \(M_2 = \sup |6x|\) on \([2, 2.1]\), which is \(12.6\), so \(\tfrac12 \cdot 12.6 \cdot 0.1 = 0.63\), and the actual error \(0.61\) is below it.

P2 · Truncation of the forward quotient
Prove Proposition 3.1 from the Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder of order one.
Solution
Taylor's theorem gives \(f(x+h) = f(x) + hf'(x) + \tfrac12 h^2 f''(\xi)\) for some \(\xi\) between \(x\) and \(x+h\). Subtracting \(f(x)\) and dividing by \(h\) gives \(D^+_h f(x) = f'(x) + \tfrac12 h f''(\xi)\). Taking absolute values and bounding \(|f''(\xi)|\) by \(M_2\) gives the claim.
P3 · The rewrite that removes cancellation
Show that \(\sqrt{1+x} - 1 = x/(\sqrt{1+x} + 1)\) for \(x > -1\), and explain why the right side avoids the cancellation of the left side when \(x\) is small.
Solution
Multiplying the left side by \((\sqrt{1+x}+1)/(\sqrt{1+x}+1)\) gives \((1 + x - 1)/(\sqrt{1+x}+1) = x/(\sqrt{1+x}+1)\), where the denominator is positive for \(x > -1\). For small \(x\), the left side subtracts two nearly equal numbers, losing digits, while the right side divides \(x\) by a number near \(2\), with no subtraction of nearly equal values.
Empirical
E1 · The constant quotient (Lab 1)
Lab 1 Part A prints \(+1\) for the forward quotient at every step. Explain from Theorem 2.1 why this is the expected output, and say what the printed constancy does not show.
Solution
For \(h > 0\), \(D^+_h|x|\) at \(0\) equals \(|h|/h = 1\) exactly, so it is constant in \(h\) by the algebra of the absolute value, not by a limit. The printed constancy does not show differentiability, because the derivative is a limit that does not exist here.
E2 · The rise at small steps (Lab 1)
Lab 1 prints a forward error of \(3.263 \times 10^{-5}\) at \(h = 10^{-11}\) and \(3.903 \times 10^{-1}\) at \(h = 10^{-15}\). Explain which error term produces the rise, using Proposition 4.2.
Solution
The truncation term \(\tfrac12 M_2 h\) decreases as \(h\) falls, so it cannot produce the rise. The term \(2\delta/h\) grows as \(h\) falls and is the round-off term, which dominates at these steps. At \(h = 10^{-15}\), the difference \(\tilde f(x+h) - \tilde f(x)\) is a difference of values that agree in almost all digits, so the quotient's error is bounded by \(2\delta/h\). With the assumed \(\delta = 2.22 \times 10^{-16}\) of the Lab 1 discussion, that bound is \(0.444\) at \(h = 10^{-15}\), and the printed error \(0.390\) is consistent with it.
E3 · The central minimum on a grid (Lab 1)
Lab 1 prints the same central error, \(5.859 \times 10^{-11}\), at \(h = 10^{-5}\) and \(h = 10^{-7}\). State what this means for the grid result and where the predicted minimizer \(6.3 \times 10^{-6}\) lies.
Solution
The printed digits are the same for the two decades. The unrounded errors, which the lab does not print and which are obtained by rerunning its central quotient at these two steps without the printed rounding, are \(5.858691 \times 10^{-11}\) at \(10^{-5}\) and \(5.858736 \times 10^{-11}\) at \(10^{-7}\), which differ by about \(4.4 \times 10^{-16}\), one unit in the last place of values near \(e\). The grid's choice of \(10^{-5}\) is therefore a rounding-level decision, not a measured minimum. The predicted minimizer \(6.3 \times 10^{-6}\) lies between \(10^{-5}\) and \(10^{-6}\), closer in logarithm to \(10^{-5}\), and the grid answer \(10^{-5}\) is consistent with it. A finer grid would be needed to decide the minimum, and the lab does not run one.
Research
R1 · The forward step when \(M_2\) is overestimated
Suppose only a bound \(M_2\) is known and the round-off hypothesis holds with \(\delta\). State the step the bound recommends and the size of the bound there, and say what happens to that step if \(M_2\) is overestimated by a factor of 100.
Solution
Proposition 4.2 recommends \(h^* = 2\sqrt{\delta/M_2}\), with bound \(2\sqrt{M_2\delta}\) there. If \(M_2\) is overestimated by a factor of 100, the recommended step \(2\sqrt{\delta/(100M_2)}\) is one tenth of the correct \(h^*\). At that smaller step the round-off term \(2\delta/h\) equals \(10\sqrt{M_2\delta}\), since \(2\delta/h^* = \sqrt{M_2\delta}\), so it is ten times the round-off term at \(h^*\), which is \(\sqrt{M_2\delta}\). The error is pushed toward the round-off-dominated regime.
R2 · A bounded-slope condition bounds every quotient
Let \(|f(s) - f(t)| \le L|s - t|\) for all \(s, t\) (the Lipschitz condition with constant \(L\)). Show that every forward and central quotient satisfies \(|D^+_h f(x)| \le L\) and \(|D^0_h f(x)| \le L\), and explain why this bound holds even at a kink.
Solution
\(|D^+_h f(x)| = |f(x+h) - f(x)|/h \le L h/h = L\), and \(|D^0_h f(x)| = |f(x+h) - f(x-h)|/(2h) \le L \cdot 2h/(2h) = L\). The bound uses only the Lipschitz inequality and not a derivative, so it holds at the kink of \(|x|\), where \(L = 1\) and every quotient in Theorem 2.1 has absolute value at most \(1\).
R3 · Automatic differentiation without a step
Explain why the step-balancing argument of Proposition 4.2 does not apply to a method that computes the derivative without a difference quotient, and name the lesson of this unit that treats such a method.
Solution
The argument needs a step \(h\) and a difference quotient, and it trades truncation against round-off through \(h\). A method that computes the derivative without a quotient has no step to choose, so the trade-off in Proposition 4.2 does not arise for it. The method is treated in Lesson L14, where forward-mode automatic differentiation is defined. This page makes no claim about its accuracy.
