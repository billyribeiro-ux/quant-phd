Constrained optimization and the envelope theorem

A firm, a household or a portfolio chooses a quantity to make an objective as large as possible, and the best achievable value depends on a parameter such as a price, a rate or a budget. The question "how much does the best value change when the parameter moves?" is answered by solving for the optimal choice and differentiating. The envelope theorem of Definition 1.1 gives the derivative of the best value as the partial derivative of the objective at the optimal choice, without differentiating that choice. This lesson proves it for one choice variable, shows how a constraint is handled by substitution, and shows the corner where the result fails.
1The value of a maximization and its derivative
Let \(J\) and \(I\) be open intervals, with \(x\) in \(J\) the choice and \(a\) in \(I\) the parameter. Let \(f\) be a real function on \(J \times I\). For each \(a\), the value function is \(V(a) = \max_{x \in J} f(x, a)\) whenever the maximum exists, and a maximizer is a point \(\hat{x}(a) \in J\) with \(f(\hat{x}(a), a) = V(a)\).
Definition 1.1 (the envelope theorem)
The envelope theorem is the statement that, under stated hypotheses, the derivative of the value function \(V\) at \(a\) equals the partial derivative \(f_a\) of the objective at the optimal choice \(\hat{x}(a)\), with the choice held at its optimum and not differentiated.
Lemma 1.2 (interior maximum, first-order condition)
Let \(\varphi\) be differentiable on an open interval \(J\), and let \(\hat{x} \in J\) be a maximizer of \(\varphi\) on \(J\). Then \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) = 0\).
Proof
If \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) > 0\), then \(\bigl(\varphi(\hat{x} + t) - \varphi(\hat{x})\bigr)/t \to \varphi'(\hat{x}) > 0\) as \(t \to 0\), so for small \(t > 0\) the quotient is positive and \(\varphi(\hat{x} + t) > \varphi(\hat{x})\). Theorem 1.3 (envelope theorem, one choice variable)
Assume the following. (H1) \(f\) is continuously differentiable on \(J \times I\). (H2) For each \(a \in I\), \(\hat{x}(a) \in J\) is a maximizer of \(f(\cdot, a)\) on \(J\). (H3) The function \(\hat{x}\) is differentiable on \(I\). Then \(V(a) = f(\hat{x}(a), a)\) is differentiable on \(I\), and
\[V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a).\]
Proof
For fixed \(a\), the function \(\varphi(x) = f(x, a)\) is differentiable on \(J\), and \(\hat{x}(a)\) maximizes it by (H2). By Lemma 1.2, \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\) for every \(a \in I\).
The map \(a \mapsto (\hat{x}(a), a)\) is differentiable by (H3), and \(f\) is differentiable by (H1). The chain rule therefore applies to \(V(a) = f(\hat{x}(a), a)\), giving
\[V'(a) = f_x(\hat{x}(a), a)\,\hat{x}^{\prime}(a) + f_a(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0 \cdot \hat{x}^{\prime}(a) + f_a(\hat{x}(a), a).\]
The first term vanishes because \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\), so \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\). \(\blacksquare\)
Remark 1.4 (where (H3) comes from)
Suppose \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable, \(\hat{x}(a_0)\) is a point where \(f_x = 0\) and \(f_{xx} \ne 0\), and \(\hat{x}\) is continuous at \(a_0\). Apply Theorem 1.3 of Lesson L21, assuming its Assumption 1.2 (the intermediate value theorem) and Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06 (the extreme value theorem), to \(G(a, x) = f_x(x, a)\), with \(a\) as the independent variable and \(x\) as the dependent one, at \((a_0, \hat{x}(a_0))\), where the partial derivative of \(G\) in \(x\) is \(f_{xx} \ne 0\). The theorem gives a differentiable function \(\tilde{x}\) on an interval \(I\) around \(a_0\), with values in an interval \(J\) around \(\hat{x}(a_0)\), and \(f_x(\tilde{x}(a), a) = 0\). For \(a\) near \(a_0\), \(\hat{x}(a)\) lies in \(J\) by continuity, and \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\) by Lemma 1.2. The zero of \(f_x(\cdot, a)\) in \(J\) is unique, so \(\hat{x}(a) = \tilde x(a)\) there, which gives (H3) locally.

2Constraints absorbed into the parameter
When the constraint depends on the parameter, the naive partial derivative can be wrong, as Example 2.2 shows. The fix is to solve the constraint for one choice variable and substitute, which leaves an unconstrained problem.
Proposition 2.1 (a constraint solved for one variable)
Let \(J\) and \(I\) be open intervals as in Theorem 1.3. Let \(g\) be continuously differentiable on an open set \(U \subseteq \R^3\) that contains \(\{(x, c(x, a), a) : (x, a) \in J \times I\}\), and let \(c\) be a real function on \(J \times I\) that is continuously differentiable there. The constraint is \(y = c(x, a)\). Put \(F(x, a) = g(x, c(x, a), a)\). Assume that \(F\) satisfies (H1) to (H3) of Theorem 1.3 on \(J \times I\), with maximizer \(\hat{x}(a)\), and put \(y^*(a) = c(\hat{x}(a), a)\). Then the problem of maximizing \(g(x, y, a)\) over \(x \in J\) subject to \(y = c(x, a)\) has value \(V(a) = F(\hat{x}(a), a)\), and
\[V'(a) = g_y\bigl(\hat{x}(a), y^*(a), a\bigr)\,c_a\bigl(\hat{x}(a), a\bigr) + g_a\bigl(\hat{x}(a), y^*(a), a\bigr).\]
Proof
Along feasible points \(y = c(x, a)\), so the value is \(\max_x F(x, a)\), and Theorem 1.3 applies to \(F\), giving \(V'(a) = F_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\). The chain rule gives \(F_a(x, a) = g_y(x, c(x,a), a)\,c_a(x,a) + g_a(x, c(x,a), a)\), and substituting \(x = \hat{x}(a)\) gives the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

The objective is \(g(x, y, a) = xy\), and the constraint is \(y = c(x, a) = a - x\), so \(c_a = 1\). Then \(F(x, a) = x(a - x)\) and \(F_x = a - 2x\), which vanishes at \(\hat{x}(a) = a/2\), with \(F_{xx} = -2

Let \(f(x, a) = ax\) on \(x \in [0, 1]\), so \(V(a) = \max_{x \in [0,1]} ax\). For \(a > 0\) the unique maximizer is \(\hat{x} = 1\), an endpoint, and \(V(a) = a\). For \(a
The naive partial ignores the constraint. In Example 2.2 the constraint moves \(y\) with \(a\), and a derivative that ignores this gives zero where the answer is \(a/2\).
Theorem 1.3 needs a differentiable selection of maximizers on an open interval. Hypotheses (H2) and (H3) require \(\hat{x}(a)\) to maximize \(f(\cdot, a)\) and to be differentiable, and \(J\) is open, so the maximizer is interior. Example 2.3 has a closed domain \([0, 1]\), so the theorem does not apply there. At \(a > 0\) the formula still gives \(f_a(1, a) = 1\), which equals \(V'(a) = 1\). At \(a = 0\) the maximizer is not unique, and the formula gives \(f_a(1, 0) = 1\) on one side and \(f_a(0, 0) = 0\) on the other, matching the two one-sided derivatives.
The value function can have a kink. In Example 2.3 the kink occurs at \(a = 0\), the one parameter value where the maximizer is not unique. The theorem does not claim differentiability of \(V\) at such a point.
3Misconceptions
“When a constraint depends on \(a\), the derivative of the value is still the partial of the objective in \(a\).”
Example 2.2 has objective \(xy\), with partial \(0\) in \(a\), and the true derivative is \(a/2\). Proposition 2.1 shows the missing term \(g_y c_a\), and Lab 1 Part B prints the zero naive partial next to the correct central difference.
“The envelope theorem needs no assumption about the optimum.”
Theorem 1.3 uses (H2), that \(\hat{x}(a)\) maximizes \(f(\cdot,a)\). Example 2.3 shows that at \(a = 0\), where the maximizer is not unique, the value function has no derivative, so the formula cannot hold there.
“A small error in the optimal choice ruins the derivative of the value.”
Lab 1 prints \(\hat{x}\) with an error of about \(10^{-8}\), and the central difference of \(V\) still matches \(\ln a\) to the printed digits. At an interior maximum, where the first derivative in \(x\) is zero, a location error \(e\) changes the objective by about \(\tfrac12 |f_{xx}|\,e^2\) to leading order. At \(a = 1.5\), \(\hat{x} = 0.5\) and \(f_{xx} = -a/(1+\hat{x})^2 = -0.666667\) (Example 1.5), so a location error of \(10^{-8}\) changes the objective by about \(0.5 \times 0.666667 \times 10^{-16} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-17}\) (Section 1, interpretation of Lab 1).

P1 · The chain-rule step
Using only the chain rule and Lemma 1.2, prove that \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\) under (H1) to (H3).
Solution
Lemma 1.2 applied to \(f(\cdot, a)\) for each \(a\) gives \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\). The chain rule applied to \(a \mapsto f(\hat{x}(a), a)\) gives \(f_x \cdot \hat{x}^{\prime} + f_a\), and the first term is zero, so \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\).
P2 · The corner value function
For Example 2.3, show that \(V(a) = \max(a, 0)\) and that \(V\) has one-sided derivatives \(1\) and \(0\) at \(a = 0\).
Solution
For \(a > 0\) the largest value of \(ax\) on \([0,1]\) is at \(x = 1\), giving \(a\). For \(a  0\), giving right derivative \(1\), and \(V(-h) - V(0) = 0\) for \(h > 0\), giving left derivative \(0\).
P3 · Why the constrained formula has two terms
For a constraint \(y = c(x, a)\), explain in one sentence why \(g_a\) alone is not the derivative of the value, and identify the term that the substitution adds.
Solution
Along the constraint, \(y\) moves with \(a\) through \(c_a\), so the objective changes through \(y\) as well as through its explicit dependence on \(a\). The added term is \(g_y c_a\), which accounts for the change in \(y\) forced by the constraint.
Empirical
E1 · The location error of the maximizer (Lab 1)
Lab 1 prints \(\hat{x} = 0.50000001\) for \(a = 1.5\), while the exact maximizer is \(0.5\). Explain why the search is off by about \(10^{-8}\) and why the value of the maximum is much more accurate.
Solution
Near a maximum, \(f(\hat{x} + t, a) - f(\hat{x}, a)\) is of order \(t^2\), so differences between nearby points are at the level of machine precision once \(t\) is about the square root of that precision, roughly \(10^{-8}\). The search therefore resolves the location only to that scale. The value at a point within \(10^{-8}\) of the maximum differs from the maximum by about \(10^{-16}\), which is why the value is accurate.
E2 · The naive partial (Lab 1)
Lab 1 Part B prints \(0\) for the naive partial of \(xy\) in \(a\), and the central difference of \(V\) equals \(a/2\). State which quantity the naive partial computes and which the central difference computes.
Solution
The naive partial holds \(x\) and \(y\) fixed, and \(xy\) has no \(a\), so it is zero. The central difference differentiates \(V(a) = a^2/4\), in which the constraint moves \(x\) and \(y\) with \(a\). Their values differ by \(a/2\), which is the term \(g_y c_a\) of Proposition 2.1.
E3 · Comparing the two columns (Lab 1)
At \(a = 1.5\) Lab 1 prints a central difference of \(V\) and an \(f_a\) value that differ in the last digit. Explain which of these depends on the printed \(\hat{x}\), and why.
Solution
The \(f_a\) value is \(\ln(1 + \hat{x})\), which depends on \(\hat{x}\) directly, so the error in \(\hat{x}\) of about \(10^{-8}\) passes into it. The central difference of \(V\) uses only the computed values of \(V\), which are insensitive to the location error to first order. That is why the two columns differ at the last digit.
Research
R1 · Where the implicit-function theorem enters
Identify the single hypothesis of Theorem 1.3 that needs the implicit-function theorem, and state the extra condition on \(f_{xx}\) under which that result gives it.
Solution
Hypothesis (H3), differentiability of \(\hat{x}\), is the one that needs the implicit-function theorem. By Remark 1.4, the condition \(f_{xx}(\hat{x}(a_0), a_0) \ne 0\) at a point of continuity of \(\hat{x}\) gives it, using Theorem 1.3 of Lesson L21 under its Assumption 1.2 and Assumption 2.1 of Lesson L06. Without that condition, the implicit-function argument does not apply, and \(\hat{x}\) may fail to be differentiable.
R2 · Several choice variables
State what the envelope theorem says when the choice \(x\) is a vector in \(\R^2\), using the gradient (Lesson L17) with respect to \(x\), and name the matrix of second derivatives that the differentiability of \(\hat{x}\) needs. Lesson L19 defines that matrix.
Solution
Assuming the vector form of Theorem 1.3, which this page does not prove, with the choice \(x\) in \(\R^2\) and the parameter \(a\) in \(\R^k\), the formula becomes \(\nabla_a V(a) = \nabla_a f(\hat{x}(a), a)\), the vector of partial derivatives of \(f\) with respect to the components of \(a\), and the first-order condition is \(\nabla_x f(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\). In the vector case the differentiability of \(\hat{x}\) would need the Hessian matrix in \(x\) to be invertible, the vector form of the condition \(f_{xx} \ne 0\) in Remark 1.4; this page does not prove the vector case. The Hessian is the matrix defined in Lesson L19.
R3 · Sensitivity without solving
Explain why the envelope theorem lets an analyst compute the sensitivity of an optimal value without computing \(\hat{x}(a)\), and state what the analyst must still be able to do for the formula to be useful.
Solution
The formula \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\) needs only the optimal choice at the point of interest, not its derivative, so a numerical optimizer that returns \(\hat{x}\) is enough. The analyst must still find the maximizer at each point and know that the hypotheses hold there, in particular that \(\hat{x}(a)\) is a differentiable maximizer there (H2 and H3). Example 2.3 shows that \(V\) can fail to be differentiable at a parameter where the maximizer is not unique.
