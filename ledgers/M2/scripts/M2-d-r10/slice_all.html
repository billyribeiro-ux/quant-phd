A first-order approximation of a function near a point is a plane, and it cannot see curvature. Risk and sensitivity work asks how an output bends as an input moves, and with two or more inputs the bending has a pure part for each input and a mixed part for each pair. This lesson collects the second partial derivatives into one array (Definition 1.1), proves when its mixed entries can be swapped, and proves the second-order Taylor expansion with a remainder that is small against the square of the step.

1Second partial derivatives and the Hessian

Let \(f\) be a real function of two real variables on an open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2\). Its partial derivatives \(f_x\) and \(f_y\), defined in Lesson L16, are functions on \(U\). Writing \(f_{xy}\) means \(\partial_y(\partial_x f)\), the derivative first in \(x\) and then in \(y\), and \(f_{yx}\) means \(\partial_x(\partial_y f)\). Each partial derivative of a partial derivative is a second partial derivative.

Definition 1.1 (Hessian matrix)

Let \(f\) be twice differentiable at a point \(p\) of \(U\). Its Hessian matrix at \(p\) is the array of second partial derivatives

\[H(p) = \begin{pmatrix} f_{xx}(p) & f_{xy}(p) \\ f_{yx}(p) & f_{yy}(p) \end{pmatrix},\]

where \(f_{xx} = \partial_x(\partial_x f)\) and \(f_{yy} = \partial_y(\partial_y f)\). The Hessian is used here only through its entries and through the quadratic form \(h^{\top} H h\) written out in Theorem 3.1; no other property of arrays is used.

Example 1.2 (a Hessian by hand)

Let \(f(x, y) = x^3 + 2xy^2 - y\). Then \(f_x = 3x^2 + 2y^2\) and \(f_y = 4xy - 1\). Differentiating again, \(f_{xx} = 6x\), \(f_{xy} = \partial_y(3x^2 + 2y^2) = 4y\), \(f_{yx} = \partial_x(4xy - 1) = 4y\), and \(f_{yy} = 4x\). At \(p = (1, 2)\) the Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 8 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\). The two mixed entries agree here, as Theorem 2.1 says they must under its hypotheses.

2When the mixed partials agree

Theorem 2.1 (symmetry of the mixed partials)

Let \(f\) be a real function on an open disc \(N\) centred at \(p = (a, b)\). Assume that \(f_x\), \(f_y\) and \(f_{xy}\) exist at every point of \(N\), and that \(f_{xy}\) is continuous at \(p\). Then \(f_{yx}(p)\) exists and \(f_{yx}(p) = f_{xy}(p)\).

Proof

For \(h, k\) small enough that \((a + h, b + k) \in N\), put \(\Delta(h, k) = f(a+h, b+k) - f(a+h, b) - f(a, b+k) + f(a, b)\).

Step 1 (a double mean value argument). Let \(\varphi(x) = f(x, b+k) - f(x, b)\). Its derivative at \(x\) is \(f_x(x, b+k) - f_x(x, b)\), which exists, so \(\varphi\) is differentiable on the interval between \(a\) and \(a + h\), and \(\Delta = \varphi(a+h) - \varphi(a)\). The mean value theorem gives \(\xi\) between \(a\) and \(a+h\) with \(\Delta = h\,[f_x(\xi, b+k) - f_x(\xi, b)]\). Let \(\psi(y) = f_x(\xi, y)\). Its derivative \(\psi'(y) = f_{xy}(\xi, y)\) exists on \(N\), so the mean value theorem gives \(\eta\) between \(b\) and \(b+k\) with \(\psi(b+k) - \psi(b) = k\,f_{xy}(\xi, \eta)\). Hence

\[\Delta(h, k) = hk\, f_{xy}(\xi, \eta), \qquad |\xi - a| \le |h|,\; |\eta - b| \le |k|.\]

Step 2 (the bound). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). By continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at \(p\) there is \(\delta > 0\), with the disc of radius \(\delta\) inside \(N\), such that \(|f_{xy}(q) - f_{xy}(p)| < \varepsilon\) for \(|q - p| < \delta\). If \(0 < |h| < \delta/2\) and \(0 < |k| < \delta/2\), then \((\xi, \eta)\) lies within distance \(\delta\) of \(p\), so \(\left|\Delta(h,k)/(hk) - f_{xy}(p)\right| < \varepsilon\).

Step 3 (the limit in \(k\) at fixed \(h\)). Fix \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta/2\). Since \(f_y\) exists at \((a+h, b)\) and at \((a, b)\), \(\Delta(h, k)/k = [f(a+h, b+k) - f(a+h, b)]/k - [f(a, b+k) - f(a, b)]/k\) tends to \(f_y(a+h, b) - f_y(a, b)\) as \(k \to 0\). Letting \(k \to 0\) in the bound of Step 2 gives \(\left|\bigl(f_y(a+h, b) - f_y(a, b)\bigr)/h - f_{xy}(p)\right| \le \varepsilon\) for every \(0 < |h| < \delta/2\).

The left side is the difference quotient that defines \(f_{yx}(p) = \partial_x(\partial_y f)(p)\). Since \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary, that limit exists and equals \(f_{xy}(p)\). \(\blacksquare\)

Example 2.2 (the hypothesis cannot be dropped)

Let \(f(x, y) = xy(x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)\) for \((x, y) \ne (0, 0)\), and \(f(0, 0) = 0\). Off the origin the quotient is a rational function with a non-vanishing denominator, so \(f_x\), \(f_y\), \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) exist there. At the origin, \(f(0, y) = 0\) for \(y \ne 0\), and for \(y \ne 0\) the expansion \(f(h, y) = hy(h^2 - y^2)/(h^2 + y^2)\) gives \(\lim_{h \to 0} [f(h, y) - f(0, y)]/h = -y\). The same limit is \(-y\) at \(y = 0\), so \(f_x(0, y) = -y\) for every \(y\). Likewise \(f(x, 0) = 0\) and \(f(x, k)/k \to x\) as \(k \to 0\) for \(x \ne 0\), with \(f_y(x, 0) = x\) for every \(x\). Therefore \(f_{xy}(0, 0) = \partial_y(f_x)(0, 0) = -1\) and \(f_{yx}(0, 0) = \partial_x(f_y)(0, 0) = 1\). The mixed partials differ at the origin.

The first partials \(f_x\) and \(f_y\), and the mixed partial \(f_{xy}\), exist on a disc around the origin. Since the conclusion of Theorem 2.1 fails at the origin, its hypotheses must fail there too, and the only one that can fail is the continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at the origin. So \(f_{xy}\) is discontinuous at the origin, and the example shows that the continuity hypothesis is needed.

3The second-order Taylor expansion

Let \(f\) be twice differentiable on an open disc \(N\) centred at \(p\), with gradient \(\nabla f = (f_x, f_y)\) and Hessian \(H\). For a vector \(h = (h_1, h_2)\), write \(\nabla f(p) \cdot h = f_x(p) h_1 + f_y(p) h_2\) and \(h^{\top} H(p) h = \sum_{i,j} h_i H_{ij}(p) h_j\). Below, \(o(s^k)\) denotes a quantity whose quotient by \(s^k\) tends to \(0\) as \(s \to 0\).

Theorem 3.1 (second-order expansion with a remainder)

Let \(f\) be twice differentiable on an open disc \(N\) centred at \(p\), and assume the Hessian \(H\) is continuous at \(p\). For every \(h\) with \(p + h \in N\),

\[f(p + h) = f(p) + \nabla f(p) \cdot h + \tfrac12 h^{\top} H(p) h + R(h), \qquad |R(h)| \le 2\,|h|^2\,\omega(|h|),\]

where \(\omega(r) = \sup\{\max_{i,j} |H_{ij}(p+q) - H_{ij}(p)| : |q| \le r\}\). Since \(\omega(r) \to 0\) as \(r \to 0\), \(R(h)/|h|^2 \to 0\).

Proof

Fix \(h\) and put \(g(t) = f(p + th)\) for \(t\) in an interval containing \([0, 1]\). By the chain rule \(g'(t) = \nabla f(p+th) \cdot h\) and \(g''(t) = h^{\top} H(p+th)\, h\). Put \(\varphi(t) = g(t) - g(0) - t\,g'(0) - \tfrac12 t^2 g''(0)\). Then \(\varphi(0) = 0\), \(\varphi'(0) = 0\), and \(\varphi''(t) = g''(t) - g''(0) = h^{\top}\bigl(H(p+th) - H(p)\bigr) h\).

The mean value theorem applied to \(\varphi\) on \([0, 1]\), and then to \(\varphi'\) on \([0, s_1]\), gives \(s_1 \in (0, 1)\) and \(s_2 \in (0, s_1)\) with \(\varphi(1) = \varphi'(s_1) = s_1\,\varphi''(s_2)\). So \(|\varphi(1)| \le \sup_{0 \le t \le 1} |\varphi''(t)|\).

Each entry of \(H(p+th) - H(p)\) is at most \(\omega(|h|)\) in absolute value when \(|th| \le |h|\), and \(|h^{\top} M h| \le (|h_1| + |h_2|)^2 \max_{ij}|M_{ij}| \le 2|h|^2 \max_{ij}|M_{ij}|\). Hence \(|\varphi''(t)| \le 2|h|^2\omega(|h|)\). Since \(g(1) = f(p+h)\) and \(g'(0) = \nabla f(p)\cdot h\), \(\varphi(1) = R(h)\) and the bound follows. Continuity of \(H\) at \(p\) gives \(\omega(r) \to 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.2 (the expansion along one direction)

Fix a unit vector \(d\) and let \(g(s) = f(p + sd)\). Theorem 3.1 with \(h = sd\) gives \(g(s) = g(0) + g'(0)s + \tfrac12 g''(0)s^2 + o(s^2)\), with \(g'(0) = \nabla f(p) \cdot d\) and \(g''(0) = d^{\top} H(p) d\). The coefficient of \(s^2\) is \(\tfrac12 g''(0) = \tfrac12 d^{\top} H(p)\, d = \tfrac12\bigl(d_1^2 f_{xx}(p) + d_1 d_2\,(f_{xy}(p) + f_{yx}(p)) + d_2^2 f_{yy}(p)\bigr)\). The two mixed partials enter through the term in \(d_1 d_2\); when they agree, as Theorem 2.1 gives under its hypotheses, this is \(d_1 d_2 \cdot 2 f_{xy}(p)\).

Proposition 3.3 (the next term is cubic)

Let \(g\) be three times differentiable on an interval containing \([0, s]\), with \(g'''\) continuous at \(0\), and put \(E(s) = g(s) - g(0) - g'(0)s - \tfrac12 g''(0)s^2\). Then \(E(s) = \tfrac16 g'''(0)s^3 + o(s^3)\). If \(g'''(0) \ne 0\), then \(E(s)/E(s/2) \to 8\) as \(s \to 0\).

Proof

By the Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder (Lesson L08), \(E(s) = \tfrac16 g'''(\theta s)s^3\) for some \(\theta \in (0, 1)\). Continuity of \(g'''\) at \(0\) gives \(g'''(\theta s) \to g'''(0)\) as \(s \to 0\), which is the first claim. If \(g'''(0) \ne 0\), then \(E(s)/E(s/2) = \dfrac{\tfrac16 g'''(0)s^3 + o(s^3)}{\tfrac1{48} g'''(0)s^3 + o(s^3)} \to 8\). \(\blacksquare\)

Lab 1 · The gradient, the Hessian and the expansion errors of a SIMULATED function

The function is \(f(x, y) = e^x y + x y^2\), a SIMULATED test function, not market data. A central difference of a function \(u(x, y)\) in the \(x\)-direction, with step \(h > 0\), is \((u(x + h, y) - u(x - h, y))/(2h)\), and the \(y\)-direction is defined in the same way. Its closed-form gradient and Hessian are \((e^x y + y^2,\; e^x + 2xy)\) and \(\begin{pmatrix} e^x y & e^x + 2y \\ e^x + 2y & 2x \end{pmatrix}\). Predicted before running: at the base point \((0.5, 0.25)\) the Hessian entries are \(f_{xx} = e^{0.5}/4 \approx 0.41218\), \(f_{xy} = e^{0.5} + 0.5 \approx 2.14872\) and \(f_{yy} = 1\). Central differences of the closed gradient at step \(10^{-5}\) should reproduce the Hessian to \(10^{-10}\) or better, and the two mixed differences should agree to the same order, because \(f\) is smooth. Along the unit direction \(d = (0.6, -0.8)\), the first-order error should fall by a factor near 4 when the step halves. The second-order error should fall by a factor near 8 if the third derivative \(g'''(0)\) of \(g(s) = f(p + sd)\) is non-zero, by Proposition 3.3. The lab does not compute \(g'''(0)\), so the printed ratios bear on that point only indirectly.

"""M2 lab L19: the gradient, the Hessian and the second-order Taylor expansion of a two-variable function.

SIMULATED: the function f(x, y) = exp(x) * y + x * y**2 is a test function chosen for this lab. It is not
market data and it models no security.
Part A checks the closed-form gradient and Hessian of f against central differences, and reports the
symmetry of the finite-difference Hessian. Part B measures the error of the first-order and the second-order
Taylor expansions of f along one fixed unit direction, at step sizes s, s/2, s/4 and s/8, and prints the ratio
of successive errors. Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math

Point = tuple[float, float]
Matrix = tuple[tuple[float, float], tuple[float, float]]

BASE: Point = (0.5, 0.25)
DIRECTION: Point = (0.6, -0.8)  # a unit vector: 0.6 * 0.6 + 0.8 * 0.8 = 1
STEPS: tuple[float, ...] = (0.1, 0.05, 0.025, 0.0125)


def f(p: Point) -> float:
    """The test function f(x, y) = exp(x) * y + x * y**2."""
    x, y = p
    return math.exp(x) * y + x * y * y


def gradient_closed(p: Point) -> Point:
    """The closed-form gradient (f_x, f_y) at p."""
    x, y = p
    return (math.exp(x) * y + y * y, math.exp(x) + 2.0 * x * y)


def hessian_closed(p: Point) -> Matrix:
    """The closed-form Hessian [[f_xx, f_xy], [f_yx, f_yy]] at p."""
    x, y = p
    e = math.exp(x)
    return ((e * y, e + 2.0 * y), (e + 2.0 * y, 2.0 * x))


def fd_hessian(p: Point, h: float) -> Matrix:
    """Hessian by central differences of the closed gradient: entry (i, j) differentiates gradient component i along j.

    The step h must be positive; a step of zero or less raises ValueError.
    """
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    rows: list[list[float]] = []
    for i in range(2):
        row: list[float] = []
        for j in range(2):
            up = [p[0], p[1]]
            down = [p[0], p[1]]
            up[j] += h
            down[j] -= h
            plus = gradient_closed((up[0], up[1]))[i]
            minus = gradient_closed((down[0], down[1]))[i]
            row.append((plus - minus) / (2.0 * h))
        rows.append(row)
    return ((rows[0][0], rows[0][1]), (rows[1][0], rows[1][1]))


def along(p: Point, d: Point, s: float) -> Point:
    """The point p + s * d."""
    return (p[0] + s * d[0], p[1] + s * d[1])


def first_order_error(p: Point, d: Point, s: float) -> float:
    """f(p + s d) minus its first-order Taylor polynomial f(p) + s * grad f(p) . d."""
    g = gradient_closed(p)
    return f(along(p, d, s)) - (f(p) + s * (g[0] * d[0] + g[1] * d[1]))


def second_order_error(p: Point, d: Point, s: float) -> float:
    """f(p + s d) minus its second-order Taylor polynomial f(p) + s grad f(p) . d + (s**2 / 2) * (d H d)."""
    g = gradient_closed(p)
    hess = hessian_closed(p)
    quad = d[0] * (hess[0][0] * d[0] + hess[0][1] * d[1]) + d[1] * (hess[1][0] * d[0] + hess[1][1] * d[1])
    return f(along(p, d, s)) - (f(p) + s * (g[0] * d[0] + g[1] * d[1]) + 0.5 * s * s * quad)


def main() -> None:
    g = gradient_closed(BASE)
    print(f"base point (x, y) = {BASE}")
    print(f"closed-form gradient: f_x = {g[0]:.6f}, f_y = {g[1]:.6f}")

    hess = hessian_closed(BASE)
    print(f"closed-form Hessian: f_xx = {hess[0][0]:.6f}, f_xy = {hess[0][1]:.6f}, f_yy = {hess[1][1]:.6f}")

    h = 1e-5
    fd = fd_hessian(BASE, h)
    hess = hessian_closed(BASE)
    worst = max(abs(fd[i][j] - hess[i][j]) for i in range(2) for j in range(2))
    print(f"central-difference Hessian at step {h:g}: largest error against closed form {worst:.2e}")
    asymmetry = abs(fd[0][1] - fd[1][0])
    print(f"central-difference Hessian asymmetry |f_xy - f_yx| at the same step: {asymmetry:.2e}")

    print(f"unit direction d = {DIRECTION}, norm check {DIRECTION[0] ** 2 + DIRECTION[1] ** 2:.6f}")
    previous_first = previous_second = 0.0
    for k, s in enumerate(STEPS):
        e1 = first_order_error(BASE, DIRECTION, s)
        e2 = second_order_error(BASE, DIRECTION, s)
        line = f"step s = {s:<7g} first-order error {e1: .6e}  second-order error {e2: .6e}"
        if k > 0:
            line += f"  ratios {previous_first / e1:.4f} {previous_second / e2:.4f}"
        print(line)
        previous_first, previous_second = e1, e2

4Where the canon breaks

The statement of Theorem 2.1 has two hypotheses: the partials \(f_x\), \(f_y\) and \(f_{xy}\) exist at every point of the disc \(N\), and \(f_{xy}\) is continuous at \(p\). Theorem 2.1 uses a hypothesis on \(f_{xy}\) at one point, not a hypothesis that \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable on the whole disc. Example 2.2 shows the failure that the hypothesis prevents.

  • Symmetry needs a hypothesis. Example 2.2 has both mixed partials exist near the origin and differ at it, so no symmetry can be assumed for an arbitrary function whose second partials exist. A proof of symmetry must check the continuity hypothesis at the point of interest.
  • A finite-difference table is not a proof of symmetry. Lab 1 prints a mixed-difference asymmetry of 1.94e-11. That number is a computed size at one step, and it cannot show symmetry at a point where the hypothesis fails. Lesson L13 treats the error of finite differences in general.
  • The remainder is little-o, not a constant times a cube, under Theorem 3.1. Theorem 3.1 gives \(R(h)/|h|^2 \to 0\) under twice differentiability on the disc together with continuity of \(H\) at \(p\). The cubic rate of Proposition 3.3 needs a third derivative in one direction, which Theorem 3.1 does not assume.

5Misconceptions

  1. “The mixed partials \(f_{xy}\) and \(f_{yx}\) are always equal.”

    Example 2.2 computes \(f_{xy}(0,0) = -1\) and \(f_{yx}(0,0) = 1\) for \(f(x, y) = xy(x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)\). Theorem 2.1 gives equality under continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at the point, and the example shows that the hypothesis is needed.

  2. “The second-order term has no cross term, because it is only the second derivative times \(h^2\).”

    The quadratic form in Theorem 3.1 is \(\tfrac12 h^{\top} H h = \tfrac12(f_{xx} h_1^2 + 2 f_{xy} h_1 h_2 + f_{yy} h_2^2)\), where the two mixed entries are equal for this smooth \(f\) by Theorem 2.1, and the cross term \(f_{xy} h_1 h_2\) is present whenever \(f_{xy}(p) \ne 0\). Lab 1 uses \(f_{xy}(0.5, 0.25) = e^{0.5} + 0.5 \approx 2.149\), so the cross term is not zero, and dropping it would change the second-order errors the lab prints.

  3. “A small asymmetry in a numerical Hessian shows that Theorem 2.1 fails.”

    Lab 1 prints an asymmetry of 1.94e-11 for a smooth function at step 1e-5. The mixed partials of that function are equal by Theorem 2.1, and the asymmetry is a finite-difference error of the computed quotients. A counterexample needs a point where the hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 fails, as in Example 2.2.

Mechanics

M1 · The Hessian of a cubic at a point

Let \(f(x, y) = x^3 + 2xy^2 - y\). Compute \(\nabla f\) and the Hessian at \((1, 2)\).

Solution

\(f_x = 3x^2 + 2y^2\), which is \(3 + 8 = 11\) at \((1, 2)\), and \(f_y = 4xy - 1\), which is \(8 - 1 = 7\). Then \(f_{xx} = 6x = 6\), \(f_{xy} = 4y = 8\), \(f_{yx} = 4y = 8\) and \(f_{yy} = 4x = 4\). The Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix} 6 & 8 \\ 8 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\), and the mixed entries agree as Example 1.2 requires.

M2 · The expansion of a quadratic is exact

Let \(f(x, y) = x^2 + xy + y^2\). Show that the second-order expansion at \((0, 0)\) has remainder zero for every \(h\).

Solution

The Hessian is \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) everywhere, and \(\nabla f(0,0) = 0\). Then \(\tfrac12 h^{\top} H h = \tfrac12(2h_1^2 + 2h_1h_2 + 2h_2^2) = h_1^2 + h_1 h_2 + h_2^2 = f(h)\), so \(R(h) = 0\) for every \(h\), which is consistent with Theorem 3.1.

M3 · A second derivative along a direction

For \(f(x, y) = x^2 y\) at \((1, 1)\) and the direction \(d = (1, 0)\), compute \(g''(0)\) for \(g(s) = f((1,1) + s d)\), and check it against \(f_{xx}\).

Solution

Here \(g(s) = (1 + s)^2 \cdot 1\), so \(g''(s) = 2\) and \(g''(0) = 2\). Remark 3.2 gives \(g''(0) = d^{\top} H d = f_{xx}(1,1)\), and \(f_{xx} = 2y = 2\) at \((1,1)\), which agrees.

P1 · Symmetry for a polynomial

Show directly that \(f_{xy} = f_{yx}\) everywhere for \(f(x, y) = x^2 y^3\), without citing Theorem 2.1.

Solution

\(f_x = 2xy^3\), so \(f_{xy} = \partial_y(2xy^3) = 6xy^2\). Also \(f_y = 3x^2y^2\), so \(f_{yx} = \partial_x(3x^2y^2) = 6xy^2\). The two mixed partials are equal at every point, which is the claim for this polynomial.

P2 · The remainder of a one-variable quadratic

Let \(g(t) = a + bt + ct^2\). Show that \(\varphi(t) = g(t) - g(0) - t g'(0) - \tfrac12 t^2 g''(0)\) is identically zero, and explain what this says about Theorem 3.1 in one variable.

Solution

Here \(g(0) = a\), \(g'(0) = b\) and \(g''(0) = 2c\). Then \(\varphi(t) = a + bt + ct^2 - a - bt - \tfrac12 t^2 \cdot 2c = 0\). A quadratic equals its second-order expansion, so the remainder of Theorem 3.1 vanishes when \(f\) is a quadratic, as in Problem M2.

P3 · The Hessian of a product of exponential and linear terms

For \(f(x, y) = e^x y\), compute every entry of \(H\) and verify that it is symmetric, with a proof that uses only differentiation.

Solution

\(f_x = e^x y\) and \(f_y = e^x\). Then \(f_{xx} = e^x y\), \(f_{xy} = \partial_y(e^x y) = e^x\), \(f_{yx} = \partial_x(e^x) = e^x\) and \(f_{yy} = \partial_y(e^x) = 0\). The mixed entries agree, so \(H = \begin{pmatrix} e^x y & e^x \\ e^x & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) is symmetric at every point.

Empirical

E1 · Why the printed asymmetry is not a counterexample

Lab 1 prints an asymmetry of 1.94e-11 between the two mixed finite differences at step 1e-5. Explain why this does not contradict Theorem 2.1, and give the size of the rounding error that a difference at this step carries.

Solution

The lab's mixed entries are finite-difference quotients, not the analytic mixed partials, so they carry truncation and rounding errors. Theorem 2.1 says the analytic partials are equal for this smooth \(f\), and the quotients need not be equal to the same accuracy. A rounding error of order \(\varepsilon/h\), with \(\varepsilon \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}\) and \(h = 10^{-5}\), is of order \(2.2 \times 10^{-11}\), the same order as the printed asymmetry \(1.94 \times 10^{-11}\).

E2 · The ratio of first-order errors (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints first-order errors whose successive ratios approach 4. Explain from Taylor's theorem why halving the step divides the first-order error by about 4, and say which term of the expansion produces that error.

Solution

The first-order error is \(R_1(s) = f(p+sd) - f(p) - s\nabla f(p) \cdot d = \tfrac12 g''(0)s^2 + o(s^2)\), a multiple of \(s^2\). Halving \(s\) divides \(s^2\) by 4, so the ratio tends to 4. The printed ratios 3.9509, 3.9750 and 3.9874 rise toward 4, consistent with this.

E3 · The second-order ratios (Lab 1)

Lab 1 prints second-order ratios 7.8836, 7.9429 and 7.9717. Which term of the expansion makes the error, and what limit do the ratios approach?

Solution

After the second-order term is removed, the error is \(\tfrac16 g'''(0)s^3\) plus smaller terms, so the cubic term produces it. Proposition 3.3 gives a limit of 8 when \(g'''(0) \ne 0\). The printed ratios increase toward 8 at the four step sizes shown, and the lab does not establish the limit by itself.

Research

R1 · Which hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 fails in Example 2.2?

Example 2.2 has both mixed partials differ at the origin. Identify which hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 fails, and explain why its failure is forced by the conclusion.

Solution

The hypotheses are existence of \(f_x\), \(f_y\), \(f_{xy}\) near \(p\) and continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at \(p\). The first holds in Example 2.2. If \(f_{xy}\) were continuous at the origin, the theorem would force \(f_{yx}(0,0) = f_{xy}(0,0)\), contradicting \(-1 \ne 1\). So continuity of \(f_{xy}\) at the origin must fail, and the example is a case of a discontinuous mixed partial.

R2 · Extending Theorem 3.1 to three variables

State the hypotheses and the bound for the expansion of \(f\) on \(\mathbb{R}^3\), and identify which step of the proof of Theorem 3.1 changes.

Solution

Assume \(f\) is twice differentiable on a ball around \(p\), with the \(3 \times 3\) Hessian continuous at \(p\). With \(n = 3\), the bound becomes \(|R(h)| \le n\,|h|^2\,\omega(|h|) = 3|h|^2\,\omega(|h|)\), with \(\omega\) as in Theorem 3.1, the largest change in an entry of \(H\) over \(|q| \le |h|\). The step that changes is the entry bound \(|h^{\top} M h| \le \bigl(\sum_i |h_i|\bigr)^2 \max_{ij}|M_{ij}| \le n\,|h|^2 \max_{ij}|M_{ij}|\), where the last inequality follows from \(\bigl(\sum_i |h_i|\bigr)^2 = \sum_i h_i^2 + 2\sum_{i<j}|h_i||h_j| \le \sum_i h_i^2 + \sum_{i<j}(h_i^2 + h_j^2) = n\,|h|^2\), using \(2|h_i||h_j| \le h_i^2 + h_j^2\). The mean value argument is unchanged.

R3 · When a quadratic form decides the sign of a change

Suppose \(\nabla f(p) = 0\), \(H\) is continuous at \(p\), and \(d^{\top} H(p) d > 0\) for every unit vector \(d\). Explain what Theorem 3.1 says about \(f(p + sd) - f(p)\) for small \(s\), and what it does not say about a fixed step.

Solution

Let \(m = \min\{d^{\top} H(p) d : |d| = 1\}\). The minimum exists because the unit circle is compact and the quadratic form is continuous, and \(m > 0\) by hypothesis. With \(\nabla f(p) = 0\), Theorem 3.1 gives \(f(p + sd) - f(p) \ge \tfrac12 s^2 m - 2s^2\omega(|s|) = s^2\bigl(\tfrac12 m - 2\omega(|s|)\bigr)\) for every unit \(d\), which is positive for every \(s \ne 0\) with \(\omega(|s|) < m/4\). That threshold does not depend on \(d\). The statement is about small steps, since \(\omega(|s|) \to 0\) is a limit, and it says nothing about a fixed step such as \(s = 0.1\) unless the threshold is checked for that step.

    A total over two inputs, such as an expected payoff over a joint range of two factors or a sum of a density over a rectangle of states, can be written as a double integral. Definition 1.1 defines it through sums over cells, and Lab 1 computes an example one variable at a time. This lesson defines the double integral over a rectangle, computes one example in both orders of integration, and shows, with an explicit counterexample in Section 3 that is conditional on an assumed fact about arctan, that the two orders can give different answers when the integrand is not bounded near a point.

    1Double Riemann sums over a rectangle

    Let \(R = [a, b] \times [c, d]\). A partition of \(R\) cuts \([a, b]\) into \(n\) cells and \([c, d]\) into \(m\) cells, giving \(nm\) rectangular cells of widths \(\Delta x_i\) and \(\Delta y_j\). A tag is a point \((\xi_i, \eta_j)\) in each cell. Lesson L09 defines the one-variable definite integral through upper and lower sums, and its Corollary 2.3 shows that for monotone integrands the Riemann sums \(\sum_i f(\xi_i)\,\Delta x_i\) converge to it. The double case uses the same idea with cells.

    Definition 1.1 (double integral over a rectangle)

    A double integral of \(f\) over \(R\), written \(\iint_R f\), is the number \(L\) such that the double Riemann sums \(\sum_{i,j} f(\xi_i, \eta_j)\,\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j\) converge to \(L\) as the largest cell width (the mesh) tends to \(0\), for every choice of tags, when such a number exists. The double integral is not defined by this statement for an integrand that the definition does not cover, and this page never assumes that every integrand is covered.

    Theorem 1.2 (a product-type integrand)

    Let \(R = [0, 1] \times [0, 2]\) and \(f(x, y) = xy + x^2\), which is continuous on \(R\). Then \(\iint_R f\) exists and equals \(\tfrac53\). The one-variable input is that Riemann sums of \(x\), \(x^2\) on \([0,1]\) and of \(y\) on \([0,2]\) converge to their integrals for every choice of tags as the mesh tends to \(0\), which is Corollary 2.3 of Lesson L09 for monotone integrands: \(x\) and \(x^2\) are nondecreasing on \([0, 1]\), and \(y\) is nondecreasing on \([0, 2]\).

    Proof

    Take any partition with cells \(\Delta x_i\), \(\Delta y_j\) and any tags \((\xi_i, \eta_j)\). Since \(f = xy + x^2\), the double Riemann sum splits into two products:

    \[S = \sum_{i,j} (\xi_i \eta_j + \xi_i^2)\,\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j = \Bigl(\sum_i \xi_i\,\Delta x_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_j \eta_j\,\Delta y_j\Bigr) + \Bigl(\sum_i \xi_i^2\,\Delta x_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_j \Delta y_j\Bigr).\]

    As the mesh tends to \(0\), the three one-variable sums converge: \(\sum_i \xi_i \Delta x_i \to \int_0^1 x\,dx = \tfrac12\), \(\sum_i \xi_i^2 \Delta x_i \to \int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \tfrac13\), and \(\sum_j \eta_j \Delta y_j \to \int_0^2 y\,dy = 2\). Also \(\sum_j \Delta y_j = 2\) exactly. So \(S \to \tfrac12 \cdot 2 + \tfrac13 \cdot 2 = \tfrac53\), for every choice of tags. \(\blacksquare\)

    2Iterated integrals and the order of integration

    Definition 2.1 (iterated integrals)

    The iterated integral with \(x\) integrated first is \(\int_c^d \Bigl(\int_a^b f(x, y)\,dx\Bigr) dy\). The iterated integral with \(y\) integrated first is \(\int_a^b \Bigl(\int_c^d f(x, y)\,dy\Bigr) dx\). Each is computed one variable at a time, using the definite integral of Lesson L09 for the inner integral and the outer integral of the resulting function of the other variable.

    Proposition 2.2 (both orders for the product-type integrand)

    For \(f(x, y) = xy + x^2\) on \([0, 1] \times [0, 2]\), both iterated integrals equal \(\tfrac53\).

    Proof

    With \(y\) integrated first: \(\int_0^2 (xy + x^2)\,dy = 2x + 2x^2\), so \(\int_0^1 (2x + 2x^2)\,dx = 1 + \tfrac23 = \tfrac53\). With \(x\) integrated first: \(\int_0^1 (xy + x^2)\,dx = \tfrac y2 + \tfrac13\), so \(\int_0^2 (\tfrac y2 + \tfrac13)\,dy = 1 + \tfrac23 = \tfrac53\). Each step is a polynomial integral, which the fundamental theorem of calculus (Lesson L10) evaluates. \(\blacksquare\)

    Remark 2.3 (a general statement this page does not prove)

    A general statement says that the two iterated integrals agree for a continuous integrand on a rectangle. This page does not prove that statement, carries no grade for it, and no proof on this page depends on it. Proposition 2.2 is checked for one integrand by direct computation. Lab 1 checks both orders for the same integrand by sums, so it adds no support for the general statement.

    Lab 1 · Iterated midpoint sums in both orders, computed exactly

    The integrand is \(f(x, y) = xy + x^2\) on \([0, 1] \times [0, 2]\), a SIMULATED test integrand chosen for this lab and not market data. Predicted before running: the midpoint rule (Riemann sums with each tag at the centre of its cell) with \(n\) cells in each direction gives, for both orders, the value \(\tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\). The derivation is Proposition 2.4 below. For \(n = 1\) the sums should equal \(\tfrac32\), and for \(n = 2\) they should equal \(\tfrac{13}{8}\). The lab uses exact fractions, so no rounding enters the printed values.

    """M2 lab L20: a double integral over a rectangle, by iterated midpoint sums in both orders.

SIMULATED: the integrand f(x, y) = x * y + x**2 on the rectangle [0, 1] x [0, 2] is a test function chosen for
this lab. It is not market data.
The lab uses exact rational arithmetic (fractions.Fraction), so every printed value is exact. For each n it
computes the iterated midpoint sum with n cells in each direction, once with x as the outer variable and once with
y as the outer variable, and compares each sum with the exact integral 5/3 and with the predicted error -1/(6 n**2).
The lab checks the two orders numerically on this one integrand. It does not invoke any theorem that equates
iterated integrals, and it makes no claim about other integrands.
Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from fractions import Fraction

X_LO, X_HI = Fraction(0), Fraction(1)
Y_LO, Y_HI = Fraction(0), Fraction(2)
EXACT = Fraction(5, 3)


def integrand(x: Fraction, y: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """f(x, y) = x * y + x**2, evaluated exactly."""
    return x * y + x * x


def midpoints(lo: Fraction, hi: Fraction, n: int) -> list[Fraction]:
    """The n midpoints of the n equal cells of [lo, hi]. A cell count below 1 raises ValueError."""
    if n < 1:
        raise ValueError(f"the cell count must be at least 1, got {n}")
    width = (hi - lo) / n
    return [lo + (i + Fraction(1, 2)) * width for i in range(n)]


def sum_x_outer(n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Iterated midpoint sum with x outer: the outer sum over x-midpoints of the inner sum over y-midpoints."""
    dx = (X_HI - X_LO) / n
    dy = (Y_HI - Y_LO) / n
    total = Fraction(0)
    for x in midpoints(X_LO, X_HI, n):
        inner = sum((integrand(x, y) * dy for y in midpoints(Y_LO, Y_HI, n)), Fraction(0))
        total += inner * dx
    return total


def sum_y_outer(n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Iterated midpoint sum with y outer: the outer sum over y-midpoints of the inner sum over x-midpoints."""
    dx = (X_HI - X_LO) / n
    dy = (Y_HI - Y_LO) / n
    total = Fraction(0)
    for y in midpoints(Y_LO, Y_HI, n):
        inner = sum((integrand(x, y) * dx for x in midpoints(X_LO, X_HI, n)), Fraction(0))
        total += inner * dy
    return total


def sum_of_odd_squares_identity(limit: int) -> bool:
    """True when 1**2 + 3**2 + ... + (2n-1)**2 equals n * (4 n**2 - 1) / 3 for every n from 1 to limit."""
    for n in range(1, limit + 1):
        odd_sum = sum((2 * i + 1) ** 2 for i in range(n))
        if 3 * odd_sum != n * (4 * n * n - 1):
            return False
    return True


def main() -> None:
    print("integrand f(x, y) = x*y + x**2 on [0, 1] x [0, 2]; exact integral 5/3")
    for n in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32):
        sx = sum_x_outer(n)
        sy = sum_y_outer(n)
        error_x = sx - EXACT
        error_y = sy - EXACT
        predicted = Fraction(-1, 6 * n * n)
        match = error_x == predicted and error_y == predicted
        print(
            f"n = {n:>2}: x outer {sx}, error {error_x}; y outer {sy}, error {error_y}; "
            f"predicted error -1/(6 n^2) = {predicted}; match {match}"
        )
    print(f"sum of odd squares identity for n from 1 to 50: {sum_of_odd_squares_identity(50)}")

    Proposition 2.4 (the midpoint error for the product-type integrand)

    For every \(n \ge 1\), the iterated midpoint sum with \(n\) cells in each direction equals \(\tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\) in either order of integration.

    Proof

    Two facts first. (i) For \(n\) cells of \([0, 1]\), the midpoints are \(x_i = (2i+1)/(2n)\) for \(i = 0, \dots, n-1\), each cell has width \(1/n\), and \(\sum_{i<n} (2i+1) = n^2\), so \(\sum_i x_i \cdot \tfrac1n = \tfrac12\). (ii) \(\sum_{i<n} (2i+1)^2 = n(4n^2 - 1)/3\). This holds at \(n = 1\), since \(1 = 1 \cdot 3/3\). If it holds at \(n\), then adding \((2n+1)^2\) gives \(\bigl[n(4n^2-1) + 3(2n+1)^2\bigr]/3 = (4n^3 + 12n^2 + 11n + 3)/3 = (n+1)\bigl(4(n+1)^2 - 1\bigr)/3\), which is the formula at \(n+1\). Hence \(\sum_i x_i^2 \cdot \tfrac1n = \tfrac{1}{4n^3}\cdot \tfrac{n(4n^2-1)}{3} = \tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{12n^2}\).

    The midpoint rule for the \(y\)-direction on \([0, 2]\) with \(n\) cells of width \(2/n\) is exact for a linear function, because on each cell the midpoint value equals the cell average of a linear function. So its sum of \(y\) is \(2\) and its sum of \(1\) is \(2\).

    With \(x\) outer: the inner sum over \(y\) gives \(x_i \cdot 2 + x_i^2 \cdot 2\), and the outer sum over \(x\) gives \(2\cdot\tfrac12 + 2\bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{12n^2}\bigr) = \tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\). With \(y\) outer: the inner sum over \(x\) gives \(\tfrac{y_j}{2} + \bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{12n^2}\bigr)\), and the outer sum over \(y\) gives \(\tfrac12 \cdot 2 + 2\bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{12n^2}\bigr) = \tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\). \(\blacksquare\)

    3Where the canon breaks

    The product-type example is the safe case. The next example, under Assumption 3.1, shows that the two iterated integrals can disagree when the integrand is not bounded near a point.

    Example 3.1 (the two orders disagree)

    Let \(f(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)^2\) on \((0, 1] \times (0, 1]\). Since \(\partial_y\bigl[y/(x^2+y^2)\bigr] = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2\), for each \(x \in (0, 1]\) we get \(\int_0^1 f(x, y)\,dy = \tfrac{1}{1 + x^2}\). Since \(\partial_x\bigl[-x/(x^2+y^2)\bigr] = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2\), for each \(y \in (0, 1]\) we get \(\int_0^1 f(x, y)\,dx = -\tfrac{1}{1 + y^2}\). The outer integrals are \(\int_0^1 \tfrac{dx}{1+x^2} = \tfrac\pi4\) and \(\int_0^1 \bigl(-\tfrac{dy}{1+y^2}\bigr) = -\tfrac\pi4\), assuming Assumption 3.1 (inverse tangent, stated below on this page), which this page does not prove.

    Assumption 3.1 (inverse tangent). \(\arctan\) is differentiable on \(\mathbb{R}\) with derivative \(1/(1+x^2)\), \(\arctan 0 = 0\) and \(\arctan 1 = \pi/4\). This page takes these facts as given.

    The integrand is unbounded near the origin: \(f(t, 0) = 1/t^2\) for \(t \in (0, 1]\). The page does not check the hypothesis of Definition 1.1 for this integrand, which is stated for a closed rectangle, so it makes no claim that the double integral exists. Under Assumption 3.1 it computes the two iterated integrals, which are \(\pi/4\) and \(-\pi/4\), so the two orders of integration give different values.

    • Order matters without a hypothesis. Under Assumption 3.1, Example 3.1 shows that the iterated integrals can differ. Proposition 2.2 shows that they agree for one bounded continuous integrand, and Remark 2.3 leaves the general case unproved on this page.
    • The domain of Example 3.1 is not a closed rectangle. Example 3.1 uses the half-open square \((0, 1]^2\), and its integrand is unbounded near the corner at the origin, which is not in that set.
    • Non-rectangular regions are outside this lesson. The page defines the double integral over rectangles only, and it makes no claim about regions with curved boundaries.

    4Misconceptions

    1. “The two orders of integration always give the same value.”

      Example 3.1 gives, under Assumption 3.1, \(\pi/4\) for one order and \(-\pi/4\) for the other on \((0,1]^2\), for an integrand unbounded at the origin. Proposition 2.2 shows agreement for one bounded integrand only.

    2. “A double integral of \(xy + x^2\) is the product of two one-variable integrals.”

      The proof of Theorem 1.2 writes the double sum as a sum of two products, one for each term. The term \(xy\) contributes \(\tfrac12 \cdot 2\), and the term \(x^2\) contributes \(\tfrac13 \cdot 2\), and the total \(\tfrac53\) is their sum, not a single product of two integrals.

    3. “Midpoint sums converge faster than the square of the cell width.”

      This is false for this integrand. Proposition 2.4 gives an error of exactly \(-\tfrac{1}{6n^2}\) for \(n\) cells in each direction, so the error falls like \(1/n^2\), which is the square of the \(x\)-cell width \(1/n\). Lab 1 prints the exact errors \(-\tfrac16, -\tfrac1{24}, -\tfrac1{96}\) for \(n = 1, 2, 4\).

    M1 · An iterated integral by hand

    Compute \(\int_0^1 \int_0^2 (x + y)\,dy\,dx\) and \(\int_0^2 \int_0^1 (x + y)\,dx\,dy\).

    Solution

    Inner in \(y\): \(\int_0^2 (x + y)\,dy = 2x + 2\). Outer: \(\int_0^1 (2x + 2)\,dx = 1 + 2 = 3\). Inner in \(x\): \(\int_0^1 (x+y)\,dx = \tfrac12 + y\). Outer: \(\int_0^2 (\tfrac12 + y)\,dy = 1 + 2 = 3\). Both orders give 3.

    M2 · A product integrand

    For \(f(x, y) = x y^2\) on \([0, 2] \times [0, 1]\), compute the iterated integral with \(x\) integrated first and check it with the other order.

    Solution

    With \(x\) first: \(\int_0^2 xy^2\,dx = 2y^2\), and \(\int_0^1 2y^2\,dy = \tfrac23\). With \(y\) first: \(\int_0^1 xy^2\,dy = \tfrac x3\), and \(\int_0^2 \tfrac x3\,dx = \tfrac23\). The two orders agree.

    M3 · A midpoint sum with one cell

    Use one cell in each direction on \([0,1] \times [0,2]\), with midpoint tags, for \(f = xy + x^2\). Compute the double Riemann sum and compare with \(\tfrac53\).

    Solution

    The single midpoint is \((\tfrac12, 1)\), with cell area \(1 \cdot 2 = 2\). Then \(f(\tfrac12, 1) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34\), and the sum is \(2 \cdot \tfrac34 = \tfrac32\). The difference from \(\tfrac53\) is \(-\tfrac16\), which equals \(-\tfrac{1}{6n^2}\) at \(n = 1\) as Proposition 2.4 gives.

    Proof

    P1 · The identity for odd squares

    Prove \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (2i+1)^2 = n(4n^2 - 1)/3\) for every \(n \ge 1\), by induction, and state the base case.

    Solution

    Base case \(n = 1\): the left side is \(1\), and the right side is \(1 \cdot 3/3 = 1\). Step: assuming the formula at \(n\), the sum at \(n+1\) is \(n(4n^2-1)/3 + (2n+1)^2\), which equals \((4n^3 + 12n^2 + 11n + 3)/3\). The right side at \(n+1\) is \((n+1)(4n^2 + 8n + 3)/3 = (4n^3 + 12n^2 + 11n + 3)/3\), so the two agree.

    P2 · The midpoint rule is exact for linear functions

    Show that on a cell \([u, v]\) with midpoint \(m = (u+v)/2\), the midpoint value \((v-u)\,f(m)\) equals \(\int_u^v (\alpha + \beta x)\,dx\) when \(f(x) = \alpha + \beta x\).

    Solution

    \(f(m) = \alpha + \beta m\), so \((v-u)f(m) = (v-u)\alpha + \beta (v-u)m\). The integral is \(\alpha(v-u) + \beta(v^2 - u^2)/2 = \alpha(v-u) + \beta (v-u)(v+u)/2\), and \((v-u)\beta(v+u)/2 = (v-u)\beta m\). The two expressions agree.

    P3 · The product form of a double Riemann sum

    Show that for \(f = g(x)h(y)\) and any tags, the double Riemann sum equals \(\bigl(\sum_i g(\xi_i)\Delta x_i\bigr)\bigl(\sum_j h(\eta_j)\Delta y_j\bigr)\).

    Solution

    \(\sum_{i,j} g(\xi_i)h(\eta_j)\Delta x_i \Delta y_j = \sum_i g(\xi_i)\Delta x_i \sum_j h(\eta_j)\Delta y_j\), since the summand is a product of a factor depending only on \(i\) and a factor depending only on \(j\), and a finite double sum of such products factors as a product of sums.

      A relation between economic quantities can be stated as an equation rather than as a formula for one of them, for example an equation that fixes a price and a yield together. When the equation can be solved locally for one variable, its slope follows from the equation without solving it. This lesson states the local result, derives the slope formula from the chain rule, and shows where the solvability condition fails, using one example solved in closed form (the circle) and one solved numerically (a cubic relation in the lab).

      1A relation that defines a function locally

      Let \(F\) be a real function of two variables on an open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2\). The set \(\{(x, y) \in U : F(x, y) = 0\}\) is a curve in the plane, and a point on it is a pair \((x, y)\) that satisfies the relation. A curve of this kind need not be the graph of a function \(y = g(x)\) over any interval.

      Definition 1.1 (locally implicit function)

      Let \((x_0, y_0)\) satisfy \(F(x_0, y_0) = 0\). A function \(g\) on an open interval \(I\) containing \(x_0\) is a locally implicit function of \(F\) at \((x_0, y_0)\) if \(g(x_0) = y_0\) and \(F(x, g(x)) = 0\) for every \(x \in I\). The implicit-function theorem is the statement that gives conditions under which such a \(g\) exists, is unique among functions with values in an open interval \(J\) around \(y_0\), and is differentiable.

      Assumption 1.3 (intermediate value theorem, assumed here and not proved)

      If \(\varphi\) is continuous on a closed interval \([p, q]\) with \(p < q\), and \(\varphi(p) < 0 < \varphi(q)\), then \(\varphi(c) = 0\) for some \(c\) with \(p < c < q\). This is a stated assumption of this lesson. No proof is given here, and no grade is given to it. Every result below that uses it begins its statement with "Assuming Assumption 1.3".

      Theorem 1.2 (the implicit-function theorem, one dependent variable)

      Assuming Assumption 1.3, let \(F\) be continuously differentiable on an open set \(U\) containing \((x_0, y_0)\), with \(F(x_0, y_0) = 0\) and \(F_y(x_0, y_0) \ne 0\). Then there are open intervals \(I \ni x_0\) and \(J \ni y_0\), with \(I \times J \subseteq U\) and \(F_y \ne 0\) on \(I \times J\), and a unique function \(g: I \to J\) such that \(F(x, g(x)) = 0\) for all \(x \in I\). Moreover \(g\) is continuously differentiable on \(I\).

      Proof

      Replacing \(F\) by \(-F\) if necessary, assume \(F_y(x_0, y_0) > 0\). Since \(F_y\) is continuous, there are \(a > 0\) and \(c > 0\) such that the rectangle \(R = [x_0 - a, x_0 + a] \times [y_0 - c, y_0 + c]\) lies in \(U\) and \(F_y > 0\) on \(R\). Put \(J = (y_0 - c, y_0 + c)\). The function \(y \mapsto F(x_0, y)\) is strictly increasing on \([y_0 - c, y_0 + c]\) and vanishes at \(y_0\), so \(F(x_0, y_0 - c) < 0 < F(x_0, y_0 + c)\). By continuity of \(F\) there is \(0 < a_1 \le a\) with \(F(x, y_0 - c) < 0 < F(x, y_0 + c)\) for \(|x - x_0| < a_1\). Put \(I = (x_0 - a_1, x_0 + a_1)\), so that \(I \times J \subseteq R \subseteq U\), and \(F_y > 0\) on \(I \times J\) because \(F_y > 0\) on \(R\).

      (Existence and uniqueness.) For each \(x \in I\), the function \(y \mapsto F(x, y)\) is strictly increasing on \([y_0 - c, y_0 + c]\) and changes sign there, so by Assumption 1.3 it has exactly one zero \(g(x)\) in \(J\). This defines \(g: I \to J\) with \(g(x_0) = y_0\) and \(F(x, g(x)) = 0\). Any \(J\)-valued function with the same property must take the zero \(g(x)\), so \(g\) is unique.

      (Continuity.) Let \(x_1 \in I\) and let \(\varepsilon > 0\) be small enough that \([g(x_1) - \varepsilon, g(x_1) + \varepsilon] \subseteq J\). Then \(F(x_1, g(x_1) - \varepsilon) < 0 < F(x_1, g(x_1) + \varepsilon)\), so by continuity of \(F\) the same two inequalities hold for all \(x\) near \(x_1\). For such \(x\), strict monotonicity in \(y\) places the zero \(g(x)\) between \(g(x_1) - \varepsilon\) and \(g(x_1) + \varepsilon\). Hence \(g\) is continuous at \(x_1\).

      (Differentiability.) Fix \(x_1 \in I\), put \(y_1 = g(x_1)\), and let \(h \ne 0\) be small enough that \(x_1 + h \in I\). Put \(y_2 = g(x_1 + h)\). The mean value theorem in \(x\), applied to \(s \mapsto F(s, y_2)\), and then in \(y\), applied to \(s \mapsto F(x_1, s)\), gives \(\xi_h\) between \(x_1\) and \(x_1 + h\), and \(\eta_h\) between \(y_1\) and \(y_2\), with

      \[0 = F(x_1 + h, y_2) - F(x_1, y_1) = h\,F_x(\xi_h, y_2) + (y_2 - y_1)\,F_y(x_1, \eta_h).\]

      If \(y_2 = y_1\), the second step is trivial and the identity still holds. The point \((x_1, \eta_h)\) lies in \(R\), so \(F_y(x_1, \eta_h) > 0\), and therefore \(\dfrac{y_2 - y_1}{h} = -\dfrac{F_x(\xi_h, y_2)}{F_y(x_1, \eta_h)}\). As \(h \to 0\), \(y_2 \to y_1\) by continuity of \(g\), so \(\xi_h \to x_1\) and \(\eta_h \to y_1\). Continuity of \(F_x\) and \(F_y\) gives \(\lim_{h \to 0} (y_2 - y_1)/h = -F_x(x_1, y_1)/F_y(x_1, y_1)\). So \(g\) is differentiable at \(x_1\).

      Finally \(g'(x) = -F_x(x, g(x))/F_y(x, g(x))\) is continuous on \(I\), because \(g\) is continuous, \(F_x\) and \(F_y\) are continuous, and \(F_y > 0\) on \(R\). So \(g\) is continuously differentiable on \(I\). \(\blacksquare\)

      2The slope of the implicit function

      Theorem 2.1 (the slope formula)

      Assuming Assumption 1.3, under the hypotheses of Theorem 1.2, and with \(g\) the function it provides, \(g'(x) = -F_x(x, g(x)) / F_y(x, g(x))\) for every \(x \in I\), where \(F_x\) and \(F_y\) are the partial derivatives of \(F\).

      Proof

      By Theorem 1.2, \(F_y \ne 0\) on \(I \times J\), so the division below is allowed at every \(x \in I\). Since \(F\) is continuously differentiable and \(g\) is differentiable, the chain rule applies to \(x \mapsto F(x, g(x))\), which is identically zero on \(I\). Its derivative is \(F_x(x, g(x)) + F_y(x, g(x))\,g'(x)\), so this sum is zero. Dividing by \(F_y(x, g(x)) \ne 0\) gives the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

      Example 2.2 (the circle, solved in closed form)

      Let \(F(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 1\) and \((x_0, y_0) = (0.6, 0.8)\). Here \(F_y = 2y = 1.6 \ne 0\). The function \(g(x) = \sqrt{1 - x^2}\) on \(I = (-1, 1)\) satisfies \(F(x, g(x)) = 0\) and \(g(0.6) = 0.8\). Its derivative is \(g'(x) = -x/\sqrt{1 - x^2}\), which at \(x_0\) is \(-0.6/0.8 = -0.75\). The formula of Theorem 2.1 gives \(-F_x/F_y = -2x/2y = -0.6/0.8 = -0.75\), so the two agree.

      Remark 2.3 (the formula does not need the closed form)

      Assuming Assumption 1.3, Theorem 2.1 gives \(g'(x)\) from the values of \(F_x\) and \(F_y\) at the point \((x, g(x))\), with no formula for \(g\). So a derivative can be computed when \(g\) is only known through a numerical root. Lab 1 uses this.

      Lab 1 · The slope of a root found numerically, against the formula

      The relation is \(F(x, y) = y^3 + xy - 1 = 0\), a SIMULATED test relation chosen for this lab and not market data. The lab does not use the cubic formula; it finds \(y(x) > 0\) by Newton's method. Here \(F_x = y\) and \(F_y = 3y^2 + x\). For \(x > 0\) and \(y > 0\), \(F_y > 0\), so the hypothesis of Theorem 1.2 holds at every point the lab uses. Predicted before running: the formula \(dy/dx = -y/(3y^2 + x)\) at each root should agree with a central difference of the computed roots at step \(10^{-5}\) to about \(10^{-11}\) or better, and each residual should be at the rounding level.

      """M2 lab L21: the derivative of an implicitly defined function, against finite differences of its root.

SIMULATED: the relation F(x, y) = y**3 + x * y - 1 = 0 is a test equation chosen for this lab. It is not market data.
For each x in a fixed list the lab finds the root y(x) > 0 by Newton's method, not the cubic formula. It then
compares the implicit derivative dy/dx = -F_x / F_y, evaluated at the root, with a central difference of the computed
roots at step h = 1e-5. On this relation F_y = 3 y**2 + x is positive at every x > 0 used, so the hypothesis of the
implicit-function theorem (F_y not zero at the point) holds at each listed point.
Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

X_VALUES: tuple[float, ...] = (0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0)
STEP = 1e-5
TOLERANCE = 1e-15
MAX_ITERATIONS = 100


def relation(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """F(x, y) = y**3 + x * y - 1."""
    return y * y * y + x * y - 1.0


def relation_y(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """The partial derivative F_y(x, y) = 3 y**2 + x."""
    return 3.0 * y * y + x


def root(x: float) -> float:
    """The root y > 0 of F(x, y) = 0 for a given x > 0, by Newton's method from y = 1.

    Starting at y = 1 is to the right of the root for every x > 0, because F(x, 1) = x > 0 and F is increasing
    in y there, so the iterates decrease to the root. A non-convergent run raises RuntimeError.
    """
    if x <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"this lab takes x > 0, got {x}")
    y = 1.0
    for _ in range(MAX_ITERATIONS):
        step = relation(x, y) / relation_y(x, y)
        y -= step
        if abs(step) < TOLERANCE:
            return y
    raise RuntimeError(f"Newton's method did not converge at x = {x}")


def implicit_derivative(x: float, y: float) -> float:
    """dy/dx = -F_x / F_y at (x, y), with F_x = y for this relation and F_y = 3 y**2 + x."""
    return -y / relation_y(x, y)


def central_difference_of_root(x: float, h: float) -> float:
    """The central difference (y(x + h) - y(x - h)) / (2 h) of the computed root. The step h must be positive."""
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    return (root(x + h) - root(x - h)) / (2.0 * h)


def main() -> None:
    print("relation F(x, y) = y^3 + x*y - 1, root y > 0, step h = 1e-5")
    for x in X_VALUES:
        y = root(x)
        residual = relation(x, y)
        formula = implicit_derivative(x, y)
        fd = central_difference_of_root(x, STEP)
        print(
            f"x = {x:<5} y = {y:.10f} residual {residual: .1e} F_y = {relation_y(x, y):.6f} "
            f"dy/dx formula {formula:.10f} central difference {fd:.10f} difference {abs(formula - fd):.1e}"
        )

      3Where the canon breaks

      Theorem 1.2 is local. It produces a function on an interval around \(x_0\), and it says nothing about the whole solution curve. The condition \(F_y(x_0, y_0) \ne 0\) is the hypothesis under which Theorem 1.2 gives a graph over \(x\). It is sufficient, not necessary: for \(F = y^3 - x\) at the origin, \(F_y = 0\), yet the zero set is the graph \(y = x^{1/3}\) over all of \(\mathbb{R}\).

      Example 3.1 (the circle at its two kinds of points)

      Take \(F(x, y) = x^2 + y^2 - 1\). At \((0.6, 0.8)\) the condition \(F_y \ne 0\) holds and the curve is a graph \(y = \sqrt{1-x^2}\) near that point. At \((1, 0)\) we have \(F_y = 0\) and \(F_x = 2 \ne 0\). Near \((1, 0)\) the curve is the graph \(x = \sqrt{1 - y^2}\) over \(y\), and its slope \(dx/dy = -y/\sqrt{1-y^2}\) is zero at \(y = 0\). The same relation can be a function of \(y\) near one point and of \(x\) near another, and the condition on the partial that is non-zero decides which.

      Example 3.2 (no implicit function at all)

      Take \(F(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) at \((0, 0)\), where \(F = 0\) and both partials vanish. For \(x \ne 0\), \(F(x, y) = 0\) requires \(y^2 = -x^2 < 0\), which has no real solution. So no function \(g\) on an open interval containing \(0\) satisfies \(F(x, g(x)) = 0\) with \(g(0) = 0\), because \(F(x, g(x)) = x^2 + g(x)^2\) is positive for \(x \ne 0\).

      • The theorem is local. The circle has two branches \(y = \pm\sqrt{1-x^2}\) that a single function on \((-1, 1)\) cannot both be. Theorem 1.2 gives one branch near each point with \(F_y \ne 0\).
      • The condition is not implied by the relation. Example 3.2 satisfies \(F = 0\) at the origin and has no implicit function there, so the relation alone does not determine a function.
      • The slope formula needs \(F_y\), not \(F_x\). At the point of Example 3.1 where \(F_y = 0\), the slope \(-F_x/F_y\) is undefined. There the curve is a graph over \(y\), and the theorem with the roles of the variables exchanged gives \(dx/dy = -F_y/F_x\).

      4Misconceptions

      1. “Every equation \(F(x,y) = 0\) defines \(y\) as a function of \(x\).”

        Example 3.2 has a zero set with no function near the origin. The circle of Example 3.1 has two branches over \(x\), so no single function of \(x\) on \((-1, 1)\) describes it. Theorem 1.2 needs \(F_y \ne 0\) at the point.

      2. “The slope is \(dy/dx = F_y/F_x\).”

        The chain rule in Theorem 2.1 gives \(F_x + F_y\,g' = 0\), so \(g' = -F_x/F_y\). For the circle at \((0.6, 0.8)\), the wrong formula gives \(F_y/F_x = 1.6/1.2 = 4/3\), while Example 2.2 gives \(-0.75\).

      3. “When \(F_y = 0\) the formula still gives a slope, so the theorem is not needed.”

        At a point where \(F_y = 0\) the formula divides by zero, and Example 3.1 shows that the zero set may not be a graph over \(x\) near that point. Where \(F_y = 0\) and \(F_x \ne 0\), the theorem with the roles exchanged gives \(dx/dy = -F_y/F_x\) instead.

      Mechanics

      M1 · A slope at a point on a conic

      For \(F(x, y) = x^2 + xy + y^2 - 3\) and the point \((1, 1)\), check that \(F = 0\), and find \(dy/dx\) there by Theorem 2.1.

      Solution

      \(F(1,1) = 1 + 1 + 1 - 3 = 0\). Then \(F_x = 2x + y = 3\) and \(F_y = x + 2y = 3\), both non-zero at the point, so \(dy/dx = -F_x/F_y = -3/3 = -1\).

      M2 · A hyperbola checked against its explicit form

      For \(F(x, y) = xy - 1\) and the point \((1, 1)\), compute \(dy/dx\) by Theorem 2.1, then check it against \(y = 1/x\).

      Solution

      \(F_x = y = 1\) and \(F_y = x = 1\) at the point, so \(dy/dx = -1\). From \(y = 1/x\), \(dy/dx = -1/x^2 = -1\) at \(x = 1\), which agrees.

      M3 · An exponential relation

      For \(F(x, y) = e^x + y - 2\) at \((0, 1)\), find \(dy/dx\) by Theorem 2.1 and by solving for \(y\).

      Solution

      \(F(0,1) = 1 + 1 - 2 = 0\). \(F_x = e^x = 1\) and \(F_y = 1\), so \(dy/dx = -1\). Solving gives \(y = 2 - e^x\), whose derivative is \(-e^x = -1\) at \(x = 0\).

      Proof

      P1 · A linear relation

      Let \(F(x, y) = ax + by - c\) with \(b \ne 0\). Show from first principles that the unique \(y = g(x)\) has \(g'(x) = -F_x/F_y\).

      Solution

      Solving \(ax + by - c = 0\) gives \(g(x) = (c - ax)/b\) on all of \(\mathbb{R}\), which is unique because \(b \ne 0\). Its derivative is \(-a/b\). Since \(F_x = a\) and \(F_y = b\), \(-F_x/F_y = -a/b\), which is the same.

      P2 · The circle's branch

      Show that \(g(x) = \sqrt{1 - x^2}\) on \((-1, 1)\) is differentiable with \(g'(x) = -x/\sqrt{1-x^2}\), and that this equals \(-F_x/F_y\) for \(F = x^2 + y^2 - 1\) wherever \(y = g(x) > 0\).

      Solution

      The function \(1 - x^2\) is positive on \((-1, 1)\), and the square root is differentiable where its argument is positive, with derivative \(\tfrac{1}{2}(1-x^2)^{-1/2}\cdot(-2x) = -x/\sqrt{1-x^2}\). With \(y = \sqrt{1-x^2} > 0\), \(-F_x/F_y = -2x/(2y) = -x/\sqrt{1-x^2}\), which is the same expression.

      P3 · No function through the origin

      For \(F(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\), show that there is no function \(g\) on an open interval \(I\) containing \(0\) with \(g(0) = 0\) and \(F(x, g(x)) = 0\) on \(I\).

      Solution

      For \(x \in I\) with \(x \ne 0\), which exists because \(I\) is open and contains \(0\), \(F(x, g(x)) = x^2 + g(x)^2 \ge x^2 > 0\), so \(F(x, g(x)) \ne 0\). That contradicts the requirement, so no such \(g\) exists.

      E2 · Agreement at the four points (Lab 1)

      Lab 1's largest difference between the formula and the central difference is 3.9e-12. Explain why a central difference at step \(10^{-5}\) can agree to about this size, and why the agreement is not a proof of the theorem.

      Solution

      A central difference has truncation error of order \(h^2\), about \(10^{-10}\) times a third derivative, and rounding error of order \(\varepsilon/h\), about \(2 \times 10^{-11}\) at this step. Both are of the order of the printed difference. The agreement checks the formula at four points only. The theorem is the statement that the slope equals the formula for every \(x\) in \(I\), which four points cannot establish.

      Lab 1 prints residuals at the rounding level for each root. Explain why small residuals show that the computed \(y\) is close to a solution, and why they do not show that the root is the one that Theorem 1.2 provides.

      Solution

      A residual \(|F(x, y)|\) at the rounding level means \((x, y)\) is nearly on the curve. Assuming Assumption 1.3, Theorem 1.2 gives a unique solution \(g(x)\) in the interval \(J\) around \(y_0\). The lab takes the root from Newton's method started at \(y = 1\). Its docstring says the iterates converge to the root from that start, but the printed output does not show that the root is unique, and the lab does not check uniqueness.

      Take \(P_0\) in the role of \(x\) and \(y\) in the role of the dependent variable. A higher price goes with a lower yield. The sign rests on the stated hypothesis and on nothing else.

      State the analogue of Theorem 1.2 for two relations \(F_1(x_1, x_2, y_1, y_2) = 0\) and \(F_2 = 0\), using the determinant of the Jacobian with respect to \((y_1, y_2)\) as the non-degeneracy condition, and name the Jacobian object of Lesson L18 that it uses.

      Assume \(F = (F_1, F_2)\) is continuously differentiable near a point where \(F = 0\), and that the Jacobian matrix of \(F\) with respect to \((y_1, y_2)\) is invertible there, which is the condition that its determinant is non-zero. Then the relations define \(y\) as a continuously differentiable function of \(x\) near the point. The condition uses the Jacobian of Lesson L18 with respect to the dependent variables.       R3 · When is the local statement enough?

      An analyst uses the slope of \(g\) at one point to approximate a change over a wide range of \(x\). Explain what Theorem 1.2 licenses and what it does not, and name the quantity that controls how far the approximation holds.

      Solution

      Assuming Assumption 1.3, Theorem 1.2 licenses a differentiable \(g\) on an interval \(I\) that depends on the point, and the slope formula holds on \(I\). It does not give the size of \(I\), and the slope may change quickly across it. If \(g\) is twice differentiable on the interval, then by Taylor's theorem (Lesson L08, Theorem 2.1) the error of the first-order approximation over a change \(\Delta x\) is at most \(\tfrac12 \sup |g''| \, \Delta x^2\) over the interval; Theorem 1.2 does not supply a bound on \(g''\), and does not state that \(g\) is twice differentiable; Lesson L19 treats second-order expansions.

        A firm, a household or a portfolio chooses a quantity to make an objective as large as possible, and the best achievable value depends on a parameter such as a price, a rate or a budget. The question "how much does the best value change when the parameter moves?" is answered by solving for the optimal choice and differentiating. The envelope theorem of Definition 1.1 gives the derivative of the best value as the partial derivative of the objective at the optimal choice, without differentiating that choice. This lesson proves it for one choice variable, shows how a constraint is handled by substitution, and shows the corner where the result fails.

        1The value of a maximization and its derivative

        Let \(J\) and \(I\) be open intervals, with \(x\) in \(J\) the choice and \(a\) in \(I\) the parameter. Let \(f\) be a real function on \(J \times I\). For each \(a\), the value function is \(V(a) = \max_{x \in J} f(x, a)\) whenever the maximum exists, and a maximizer is a point \(\hat{x}(a) \in J\) with \(f(\hat{x}(a), a) = V(a)\).

        Definition 1.1 (the envelope theorem)

        The envelope theorem is the statement that, under stated hypotheses, the derivative of the value function \(V\) at \(a\) equals the partial derivative \(f_a\) of the objective at the optimal choice \(\hat{x}(a)\), with the choice held at its optimum and not differentiated.

        Lemma 1.2 (interior maximum, first-order condition)

        Let \(\varphi\) be differentiable on an open interval \(J\), and let \(\hat{x} \in J\) be a maximizer of \(\varphi\) on \(J\). Then \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) = 0\).

        Proof

        If \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) > 0\), then \(\bigl(\varphi(\hat{x} + t) - \varphi(\hat{x})\bigr)/t \to \varphi'(\hat{x}) > 0\) as \(t \to 0\), so for small \(t > 0\) the quotient is positive and \(\varphi(\hat{x} + t) > \varphi(\hat{x})\). That contradicts maximality. If \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) < 0\), the same argument with \(t < 0\) gives \(\varphi(\hat{x} + t) > \varphi(\hat{x})\) for small \(t < 0\), again a contradiction. So \(\varphi'(\hat{x}) = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

        Theorem 1.3 (envelope theorem, one choice variable)

        Assume the following. (H1) \(f\) is continuously differentiable on \(J \times I\). (H2) For each \(a \in I\), \(\hat{x}(a) \in J\) is a maximizer of \(f(\cdot, a)\) on \(J\). (H3) The function \(\hat{x}\) is differentiable on \(I\). Then \(V(a) = f(\hat{x}(a), a)\) is differentiable on \(I\), and

        \[V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a).\]

        Proof

        For fixed \(a\), the function \(\varphi(x) = f(x, a)\) is differentiable on \(J\), and \(\hat{x}(a)\) maximizes it by (H2). By Lemma 1.2, \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\) for every \(a \in I\).

        The map \(a \mapsto (\hat{x}(a), a)\) is differentiable by (H3), and \(f\) is differentiable by (H1). The chain rule therefore applies to \(V(a) = f(\hat{x}(a), a)\), giving

        \[V'(a) = f_x(\hat{x}(a), a)\,\hat{x}^{\prime}(a) + f_a(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0 \cdot \hat{x}^{\prime}(a) + f_a(\hat{x}(a), a).\]

        The first term vanishes because \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\), so \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\). \(\blacksquare\)

        Remark 1.4 (where (H3) comes from)

        Suppose \(f\) is twice continuously differentiable, \(\hat{x}(a_0)\) is a point where \(f_x = 0\) and \(f_{xx} \ne 0\), and \(\hat{x}\) is continuous at \(a_0\). Apply Theorem 1.2 of Lesson L21, assuming its Assumption 1.3 (the intermediate value theorem), to \(G(a, x) = f_x(x, a)\), with \(a\) as the independent variable and \(x\) as the dependent one, at \((a_0, \hat{x}(a_0))\), where the partial derivative of \(G\) in \(x\) is \(f_{xx} \ne 0\). The theorem gives a differentiable function \(\tilde{x}\) on an interval \(I\) around \(a_0\), with values in an interval \(J\) around \(\hat{x}(a_0)\), and \(f_x(\tilde{x}(a), a) = 0\). For \(a\) near \(a_0\), \(\hat{x}(a)\) lies in \(J\) by continuity, and \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\) by Lemma 1.2. The zero of \(f_x(\cdot, a)\) in \(J\) is unique, so \(\hat{x}(a) = \tilde x(a)\) there, which gives (H3) locally.

        Example 1.5 (a logarithmic objective)

        Let \(f(x, a) = a\ln(1 + x) - x\) on \(J = (-1, \infty)\) and \(I = (0, \infty)\). Here \(f_x = a/(1+x) - 1\), which vanishes exactly at \(x = a - 1\), and \(f_{xx} = -a/(1+x)^2 < 0\). So \(f(\cdot, a)\) is strictly concave, and its unique critical point \(\hat{x}(a) = a - 1\) is its global maximizer on \(J\), which gives (H2). The map \(\hat{x}\) is differentiable, which gives (H3). The value is \(V(a) = a\ln a - (a - 1)\). Assuming Assumption 1.1 below, the product rule gives \(V'(a) = \ln a + a\cdot\tfrac1a - 1 = \ln a\). Theorem 1.3 predicts \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a) = \ln(1 + \hat{x}(a)) = \ln a\), which agrees.

        Assumption 1.1 (derivative of the logarithm). For \(a > 0\), \(\tfrac{d}{da}\ln a = 1/a\). This page takes the fact from the prerequisite calculus and does not prove it.

        """M2 lab L22: the envelope theorem, checked on two maximization problems with closed-form answers.

SIMULATED: both objectives are test functions chosen for this lab. They are not market data.
Part A: maximize f(x, a) = a * ln(1 + x) - x over x in [0, 10], for a in {1.5, 2, 3, 4}. The maximizer is x_hat = a - 1
and the value function is V(a) = a ln a - (a - 1), so V'(a) = ln a. The lab finds x_hat by golden-section search, then
compares a central difference of V (step 1e-4) with ln a and with the partial derivative f_a(x_hat, a) = ln(1 + x_hat).
Part B: maximize x * y subject to x + y = a. The constraint is absorbed by substituting y = a - x, which leaves the
unconstrained problem F(x, a) = x * (a - x). The value is V(a) = a**2 / 4, so V'(a) = a / 2. The lab compares the
central difference of V with a / 2, and it prints the naive partial derivative of x * y with respect to a at fixed x
and y, which is 0, to show why the constraint cannot be ignored.
Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from collections.abc import Callable

PARAMETERS_A: tuple[float, ...] = (1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0)
PARAMETERS_B: tuple[float, ...] = (1.0, 2.0, 3.0)
STEP = 1e-4
GOLDEN = (math.sqrt(5.0) - 1.0) / 2.0


def golden_max(fun: Callable[[float], float], lo: float, hi: float, tol: float = 1e-12) -> float:
    """The maximizer of a concave function fun on [lo, hi], by golden-section search to width tol.

    The interval must satisfy lo < hi and tol > 0; otherwise ValueError is raised.
    """
    if not lo < hi:
        raise ValueError(f"need lo < hi, got {lo} and {hi}")
    if tol <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the tolerance must be positive, got {tol}")
    a, b = lo, hi
    c = b - GOLDEN * (b - a)
    d = a + GOLDEN * (b - a)
    fc, fd = fun(c), fun(d)
    while b - a > tol:
        if fc > fd:
            b, d, fd = d, c, fc
            c = b - GOLDEN * (b - a)
            fc = fun(c)
        else:
            a, c, fc = c, d, fd
            d = a + GOLDEN * (b - a)
            fd = fun(d)
    return (a + b) / 2.0


def objective_a(x: float, a: float) -> float:
    """f(x, a) = a * ln(1 + x) - x for x > -1."""
    return a * math.log(1.0 + x) - x


def maximizer_a(a: float) -> float:
    """The maximizer x_hat of f(x, a) over [0, 10], by golden-section search."""
    return golden_max(lambda x: objective_a(x, a), 0.0, 10.0)


def maximizer_b(a: float) -> float:
    """The maximizer x_hat of x * (a - x) over [0, a], by golden-section search."""
    return golden_max(lambda x: x * (a - x), 0.0, a)


def value_a(a: float) -> float:
    """V(a) = max over x in [0, 10] of f(x, a), computed numerically."""
    x_star = maximizer_a(a)
    return objective_a(x_star, a)


def value_b(a: float) -> float:
    """V(a) = max over x of x * (a - x), computed numerically on [0, a]; the constraint y = a - x is substituted."""
    x_star = maximizer_b(a)
    return x_star * (a - x_star)


def reduced_objective(x: float) -> Callable[[float], float]:
    """The map a -> x * (a - x) with the choice x held fixed; its derivative in a is x."""
    return lambda a: x * (a - x)


def frozen_product(x: float, y: float) -> Callable[[float], float]:
    """The map a -> x * y with x and y held fixed; the product has no a in it, so its derivative in a is zero."""
    return lambda a: x * y


def central_difference(v: Callable[[float], float], a: float, h: float) -> float:
    """The central difference (v(a + h) - v(a - h)) / (2 h). The step h must be positive."""
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    return (v(a + h) - v(a - h)) / (2.0 * h)


def main() -> None:
    print("Part A: maximize a*ln(1 + x) - x over x in [0, 10]; V'(a) should equal ln(a)")
    for a in PARAMETERS_A:
        x_star = maximizer_a(a)
        fd = central_difference(value_a, a, STEP)
        partial = math.log(1.0 + x_star)
        error = max(abs(fd - math.log(a)), abs(partial - math.log(a)))
        print(
            f"a = {a:<4} x_hat = {x_star:.8f} (a - 1 = {a - 1.0:.1f}) V' central difference {fd:.8f} "
            f"ln(a) {math.log(a):.8f} f_a(x_hat, a) {partial:.8f} max error {error:.1e}"
        )
    print("Part B: maximize x*y subject to x + y = a; V'(a) should equal a/2")
    for a in PARAMETERS_B:
        x_star = maximizer_b(a)
        fd = central_difference(value_b, a, STEP)
        # Reduced objective F(x, a) = x * (a - x) with x held at its optimum: its partial in a is computed, not typed.
        reduced_partial = central_difference(reduced_objective(x_star), a, STEP)
        # Naive partial: x and y are frozen at their optimum values, and x * y is then a constant in a, so the
        # central difference of that frozen product is computed as zero.
        naive_partial = central_difference(frozen_product(x_star, a - x_star), a, STEP)
        print(
            f"a = {a:<4} x_hat = {x_star:.8f} V' central difference {fd:.8f} a/2 {a / 2.0:.8f} "
            f"reduced F_a(x_hat, a) {reduced_partial:.8f} naive partial of x*y in a {naive_partial:.8f} "
            f"error {abs(fd - a / 2.0):.1e}"
        )

        2Constraints absorbed into the parameter

        When the constraint depends on the parameter, the naive partial derivative can be wrong, as Example 2.2 shows. The fix is to solve the constraint for one choice variable and substitute, which leaves an unconstrained problem.

        Proposition 2.1 (a constraint solved for one variable)

        Let \(J\) and \(I\) be open intervals as in Theorem 1.3. Let \(g\) be continuously differentiable on an open set \(U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^3\) that contains \(\{(x, c(x, a), a) : (x, a) \in J \times I\}\), and let \(c\) be a real function on \(J \times I\) that is continuously differentiable there. The constraint is \(y = c(x, a)\). Put \(F(x, a) = g(x, c(x, a), a)\). Assume that \(F\) satisfies (H1) to (H3) of Theorem 1.3 on \(J \times I\), with maximizer \(\hat{x}(a)\), and put \(y^*(a) = c(\hat{x}(a), a)\). Then the problem of maximizing \(g(x, y, a)\) over \(x \in J\) subject to \(y = c(x, a)\) has value \(V(a) = F(\hat{x}(a), a)\), and

        \[V'(a) = g_y\bigl(\hat{x}(a), y^*(a), a\bigr)\,c_a\bigl(\hat{x}(a), a\bigr) + g_a\bigl(\hat{x}(a), y^*(a), a\bigr).\]

        Proof

        Along feasible points \(y = c(x, a)\), so the value is \(\max_x F(x, a)\), and Theorem 1.3 applies to \(F\), giving \(V'(a) = F_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\). The chain rule gives \(F_a(x, a) = g_y(x, c(x,a), a)\,c_a(x,a) + g_a(x, c(x,a), a)\), and substituting \(x = \hat{x}(a)\) gives the formula. \(\blacksquare\)

        Example 2.2 (maximize \(xy\) on the line \(x + y = a\))

        The objective is \(g(x, y, a) = xy\), and the constraint is \(y = c(x, a) = a - x\), so \(c_a = 1\). Then \(F(x, a) = x(a - x)\) and \(F_x = a - 2x\), which vanishes at \(\hat{x}(a) = a/2\), with \(F_{xx} = -2 < 0\). So \(\hat{x}(a) = a/2\) is the maximizer, \(y^*(a) = a/2\), and \(V(a) = a^2/4\), giving \(V'(a) = a/2\). Proposition 2.1 gives \(g_y c_a + g_a = (a/2)(1) + 0 = a/2\), which agrees. The naive partial \(g_a\) of \(xy\) at fixed \(x\) and \(y\) is zero, and it misses the term \(g_y c_a\).

        Example 2.3 (a corner where the result fails)

        Let \(f(x, a) = ax\) on \(x \in [0, 1]\), so \(V(a) = \max_{x \in [0,1]} ax\). For \(a > 0\) the unique maximizer is \(\hat{x} = 1\), an endpoint, and \(V(a) = a\). For \(a < 0\) the unique maximizer is \(\hat{x} = 0\), and \(V(a) = 0\). At \(a = 0\) every \(x\) in \([0,1]\) is a maximizer, and \(V(0) = 0\). The one-sided derivatives are \(V'(0^+) = 1\) and \(V'(0^-) = 0\), so \(V\) is not differentiable at \(0\).

        • The naive partial ignores the constraint. In Example 2.2 the constraint moves \(y\) with \(a\), and a derivative that ignores this gives zero where the answer is \(a/2\).
        • Theorem 1.3 needs a differentiable selection of maximizers on an open interval. Hypotheses (H2) and (H3) require \(\hat{x}(a)\) to maximize \(f(\cdot, a)\) and to be differentiable, and \(J\) is open, so the maximizer is interior. Example 2.3 has a closed domain \([0, 1]\), so the theorem does not apply there. At \(a > 0\) the formula still gives \(f_a(1, a) = 1\), which equals \(V'(a) = 1\). At \(a = 0\) the maximizer is not unique, and the formula gives \(f_a(1, 0) = 1\) on one side and \(f_a(0, 0) = 0\) on the other, matching the two one-sided derivatives.
        • The value function can have a kink. In Example 2.3 the kink occurs at \(a = 0\), the one parameter value where the maximizer is not unique. The theorem does not claim differentiability of \(V\) at such a point.

        3Misconceptions

        1. “When a constraint depends on \(a\), the derivative of the value is still the partial of the objective in \(a\).”

          Example 2.2 has objective \(xy\), with partial \(0\) in \(a\), and the true derivative is \(a/2\). Proposition 2.1 shows the missing term \(g_y c_a\), and Lab 1 Part B prints the zero naive partial next to the correct central difference.

        2. “The envelope theorem needs no assumption about the optimum.”

          Theorem 1.3 uses (H2), that \(\hat{x}(a)\) maximizes \(f(\cdot,a)\). Example 2.3 shows that at \(a = 0\), where the maximizer is not unique, the value function has no derivative, so the formula cannot hold there.

        3. “A small error in the optimal choice ruins the derivative of the value.”

          Lab 1 prints \(\hat{x}\) with an error of about \(10^{-8}\), and the central difference of \(V\) still matches \(\ln a\) to the printed digits. At an interior maximum, where the first derivative in \(x\) is zero, a location error \(e\) changes the objective by about \(\tfrac12 |f_{xx}|\,e^2\) to leading order. At \(a = 1.5\), \(\hat{x} = 0.5\) and \(f_{xx} = -a/(1+\hat{x})^2 = -0.666667\) (Example 1.5), so a location error of \(10^{-8}\) changes the objective by about \(0.5 \times 0.666667 \times 10^{-16} \approx 3.3 \times 10^{-17}\) (Section 1, interpretation of Lab 1).

        Mechanics

        M1 · The envelope of a quadratic objective

        Let \(f(x, a) = ax - x^2/2\). Find \(\hat{x}(a)\), \(V(a)\) and \(V'(a)\), and check that \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\).

        Solution

        \(f_x = a - x = 0\) gives \(\hat{x}(a) = a\), and \(f_{xx} = -1 < 0\) makes it the maximizer. Then \(V(a) = a^2 - a^2/2 = a^2/2\), so \(V'(a) = a\). Also \(f_a = x\), which at \(\hat{x} = a\) is \(a\), so the two agree.

        M2 · A shifted objective

        Let \(f(x, a) = -(x - a)^2 + 3x\). Find \(V'(a)\) by the envelope theorem and check it by differentiating \(V\) directly.

        Solution

        \(f_x = -2(x - a) + 3 = 0\) gives \(\hat{x} = a + \tfrac32\), and \(f_{xx} = -2 < 0\). Then \(V(a) = -\tfrac94 + 3a + \tfrac92 = 3a + \tfrac94\), so \(V'(a) = 3\). The envelope formula gives \(f_a = 2(x - a) = 2 \cdot \tfrac32 = 3\), which agrees.

        M3 · Maximize \(xy\) on the line \(x + 2y = a\)

        Find \(V'(a)\) for the constrained problem of maximizing \(xy\) subject to \(x + 2y = a\), using Proposition 2.1.

        Solution

        Solve the constraint as \(y = c(x, a) = (a - x)/2\), so \(c_a = 1/2\). Then \(F(x, a) = x(a-x)/2\), with \(F_x = (a - 2x)/2 = 0\) at \(\hat{x} = a/2\), and \(F_{xx} = -1 < 0\). So \(y^* = a/4\) and \(V(a) = a^2/8\). The formula gives \(g_y c_a + g_a = (a/2)(1/2) + 0 = a/4\), with \(g(x, y, a) = xy\), and \(V'(a) = a/4\) directly.

        Proof

        P1 · The chain-rule step

        Using only the chain rule and Lemma 1.2, prove that \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\) under (H1) to (H3).

        Solution

        Lemma 1.2 applied to \(f(\cdot, a)\) for each \(a\) gives \(f_x(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\). The chain rule applied to \(a \mapsto f(\hat{x}(a), a)\) gives \(f_x \cdot \hat{x}^{\prime} + f_a\), and the first term is zero, so \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\).

        P2 · The corner value function

        For Example 2.3, show that \(V(a) = \max(a, 0)\) and that \(V\) has one-sided derivatives \(1\) and \(0\) at \(a = 0\).

        Solution

        For \(a > 0\) the largest value of \(ax\) on \([0,1]\) is at \(x = 1\), giving \(a\). For \(a < 0\) it is at \(x = 0\), giving \(0\). So \(V(a) = \max(a, 0)\). At \(0\), \(V(h) - V(0) = h\) for \(h > 0\), giving right derivative \(1\), and \(V(-h) - V(0) = 0\) for \(h > 0\), giving left derivative \(0\).

        P3 · Why the constrained formula has two terms

        For a constraint \(y = c(x, a)\), explain in one sentence why \(g_a\) alone is not the derivative of the value, and identify the term that the substitution adds.

        Solution

        Along the constraint, \(y\) moves with \(a\) through \(c_a\), so the objective changes through \(y\) as well as through its explicit dependence on \(a\). The added term is \(g_y c_a\), which accounts for the change in \(y\) forced by the constraint.

        Empirical

        E1 · The location error of the maximizer (Lab 1)

        Lab 1 prints \(\hat{x} = 0.50000001\) for \(a = 1.5\), while the exact maximizer is \(0.5\). Explain why the search is off by about \(10^{-8}\) and why the value of the maximum is much more accurate.

        Solution

        Near a maximum, \(f(\hat{x} + t, a) - f(\hat{x}, a)\) is of order \(t^2\), so differences between nearby points are at the level of machine precision once \(t\) is about the square root of that precision, roughly \(10^{-8}\). The search therefore resolves the location only to that scale. The value at a point within \(10^{-8}\) of the maximum differs from the maximum by about \(10^{-16}\), which is why the value is accurate.

        E2 · The naive partial (Lab 1)

        Lab 1 Part B prints \(0\) for the naive partial of \(xy\) in \(a\), and the central difference of \(V\) equals \(a/2\). State which quantity the naive partial computes and which the central difference computes.

        Solution

        The naive partial holds \(x\) and \(y\) fixed, and \(xy\) has no \(a\), so it is zero. The central difference differentiates \(V(a) = a^2/4\), in which the constraint moves \(x\) and \(y\) with \(a\). Their values differ by \(a/2\), which is the term \(g_y c_a\) of Proposition 2.1.

        E3 · Comparing the two columns (Lab 1)

        At \(a = 1.5\) Lab 1 prints a central difference of \(V\) and an \(f_a\) value that differ in the last digit. Explain which of these depends on the printed \(\hat{x}\), and why.

        Solution

        The \(f_a\) value is \(\ln(1 + \hat{x})\), which depends on \(\hat{x}\) directly, so the error in \(\hat{x}\) of about \(10^{-8}\) passes into it. The central difference of \(V\) uses only the computed values of \(V\), which are insensitive to the location error to first order. That is why the two columns differ at the last digit.

        R2 · Several choice variables

        State what the envelope theorem says when the choice \(x\) is a vector in \(\mathbb{R}^2\), using the gradient with respect to \(x\), and name the matrix of second derivatives that the differentiability of \(\hat{x}\) needs. Lesson L19 defines that matrix.

        Solution

        The formula becomes \(\nabla_a V(a) = \nabla_a f(\hat{x}(a), a)\) with the vector of parameter partials, and the first-order condition is \(\nabla_x f(\hat{x}(a), a) = 0\). In the vector case the differentiability of \(\hat{x}\) would need the Hessian matrix in \(x\) to be invertible, the vector form of the condition \(f_{xx} \ne 0\) in Remark 1.4; this page does not prove the vector case.         R3 · Sensitivity without solving

        Explain why the envelope theorem lets an analyst compute the sensitivity of an optimal value without computing \(\hat{x}(a)\), and state what the analyst must still be able to do for the formula to be useful.

        Solution

        The formula \(V'(a) = f_a(\hat{x}(a), a)\) needs only the optimal choice at the point of interest, not its derivative, so a numerical optimizer that returns \(\hat{x}\) is enough. The analyst must still find the maximizer at each point and know that the hypotheses hold there, in particular that \(\hat{x}(a)\) is a differentiable maximizer there (H2 and H3). Example 2.3 shows that \(V\) can fail to be differentiable at a parameter where the maximizer is not unique.

          A bond's price moves when its yield moves. This lesson uses a SIMULATED bond to show how the price depends on the yield, how its first and second derivatives follow from the cash flows, and how a Taylor expansion approximates the price for a small change in yield. At a shift of \(\pm 0.01\) the lab's second-order expansion is off by 15 and 16 cents, and for positive cash flows the sign of its error is the sign of \(-\Delta\) (Proposition 3.2).

          1A bond's price as a function of its yield

          A bond pays cash flows \(c_1, \dots, c_N\), in cents, at the ends of years \(1, \dots, N\). Its yield \(y\) is a decimal with \(y > -1\), and the price is the present value of the cash flows at that yield, with annual compounding. In the terms of M1 (Lesson L05, present value and discount factor) this is the sum of each cash flow times its discount factor \((1+y)^{-t}\):

          Definition 1.1 (price as a function of yield)

          For \(y > -1\), the price is \(P(y) = \sum_{t=1}^{N} c_t\,(1 + y)^{-t}\), an exact number of cents before rounding (Section 4). Shifts in this lesson are decimal changes in yield: a shift of \(0.01\) is 100 basis points.

          The lab uses a SIMULATED bond, a generated specification and not a quoted security: face value 100000 cents, a coupon of 5 percent of face paid each year (5000 cents), ten annual payments, and the face repaid with the tenth coupon. So \(c_t = 5000\) for \(t < 10\) and \(c_{10} = 105000\). The base yield is \(y_0 = 0.04\). A bond priced at its face value is said to be at par.

          2The first and second derivatives

          Proposition 2.1 (derivatives of the price)

          \(P\) is infinitely differentiable on \((-1, \infty)\), with

          \[P'(y) = -\sum_{t=1}^{N} t\,c_t\,(1+y)^{-t-1}, \qquad P''(y) = \sum_{t=1}^{N} t(t+1)\,c_t\,(1+y)^{-t-2}.\]

          If every \(c_t > 0\), then \(P'(y) < 0\) and \(P''(y) > 0\) for every \(y > -1\).

          Proof

          \(P\) is a finite sum of functions \(y \mapsto c_t (1+y)^{-t}\), each of which is infinitely differentiable on \((-1, \infty)\). Differentiating term by term, \(\tfrac{d}{dy}(1+y)^{-t} = -t(1+y)^{-t-1}\) and \(\tfrac{d^2}{dy^2}(1+y)^{-t} = t(t+1)(1+y)^{-t-2}\), which gives the two formulas. If every \(c_t > 0\), each term of \(P'\) is negative, because \(t\,c_t\,(1+y)^{-t-1} > 0\), and each term of \(P''\) is positive, because \(t(t+1)c_t(1+y)^{-t-2} > 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

          Proposition 2.2 (price at face when the yield equals the coupon rate)

          Let \(N \ge 1\), a face value \(F > 0\), and a coupon rate \(r > -1\). Let the coupons be \(rF\) at each year \(t < N\), with the face \(F\) added to the last coupon. If the yield equals the coupon rate \(r\), the price equals \(F\).

          Proof

          Put \(v = 1/(1+r)\), so \(v\) is defined because \(r > -1\). At yield \(r\) the price is \(\sum_{t=1}^{N} rF\,v^t + F v^N\). If \(r = 0\), every coupon is \(0\) and \(v = 1\), so the price is \(F\). If \(r \ne 0\), then \(1 - v = rv\), and the geometric series of Lesson M1 gives \(\sum_{t=1}^{N} v^t = v\,(1 - v^N)/(1 - v) = (1 - v^N)/r\). Hence \(rF \sum_{t=1}^N v^t = F(1 - v^N)\), and the price is \(F(1 - v^N) + F v^N = F\). \(\blacksquare\)

def price(y: float) -> Fraction:
    """The unrounded price P(y) in cents for yield y > -1, with annual compounding, as an exact rational."""
    v = growth_factor(y)
    return sum((Fraction(cash_flow_cents(t)) / v**t for t in range(1, YEARS + 1)), Fraction(0))


def price_first_derivative(y: float) -> Fraction:
    """P'(y) = minus the sum over t of t c_t (1 + y)**(-t - 1), in cents per unit of yield, exact."""
    v = growth_factor(y)
    return -sum((Fraction(t * cash_flow_cents(t)) / v ** (t + 1) for t in range(1, YEARS + 1)), Fraction(0))


def price_second_derivative(y: float) -> Fraction:
    """P''(y) = the sum over t of t (t + 1) c_t (1 + y)**(-t - 2), in cents per unit of yield squared, exact."""
    v = growth_factor(y)
    return sum((Fraction(t * (t + 1) * cash_flow_cents(t)) / v ** (t + 2) for t in range(1, YEARS + 1)), Fraction(0))


def price_cents(y: float) -> int:
    """The price as an integer number of cents: the unrounded price rounded half to even."""
    return round(price(y))


def taylor_first(y0: float, dy: float) -> Fraction:
    """First-order Taylor polynomial of P about y0, evaluated at y0 + dy, exact for the float shift dy."""
    return price(y0) + price_first_derivative(y0) * Fraction(dy)


def taylor_second(y0: float, dy: float) -> Fraction:
    """Second-order Taylor polynomial of P about y0, evaluated at y0 + dy, exact for the float shift dy."""
    return taylor_first(y0, dy) + Fraction(1, 2) * price_second_derivative(y0) * Fraction(dy) ** 2


def central_difference(fun: Callable[[float], float], y: float, h: float) -> float:
    """The central difference (fun(y + h) - fun(y - h)) / (2 h) of a one-variable function; h must be positive."""
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    return (fun(y + h) - fun(y - h)) / (2.0 * h)

          3The Taylor expansion and its remainder

          Theorem 3.1 (the second-order expansion of the price)

          Let \(y_0 > -1\) and \(\Delta\) be such that \(y_0 + \Delta > -1\). Put \(\mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) = P(y_0) + P'(y_0)\Delta\) and \(\mathcal{P}_2(\Delta) = \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) + \tfrac12 P''(y_0)\Delta^2\). Then \(P(y_0+\Delta) = \mathcal{P}_2(\Delta) + R(\Delta)\) with \(|R(\Delta)| \le \tfrac16 M|\Delta|^3\), where \(M\) is the maximum of \(|P'''|\) on the closed interval between \(y_0\) and \(y_0 + \Delta\).

          Proof

          \(P\) is infinitely differentiable on \((-1, \infty)\) by Proposition 2.1, so \(P'''\) is continuous and the maximum \(M\) exists on the compact interval. The Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder (Lesson L08), applied with three derivatives, gives \(\theta\) between \(y_0\) and \(y_0 + \Delta\) with \(R(\Delta) = \tfrac16 P'''(\theta)\Delta^3\). Then \(|R(\Delta)| \le \tfrac16 M|\Delta|^3\). \(\blacksquare\)

          Proposition 3.2 (the sign of the remainder)

          Let \(y_0 > -1\). If every \(c_t > 0\), then \(R(\Delta)\) has the sign of \(-\Delta\) for every \(\Delta \ne 0\) with \(y_0 + \Delta > -1\), where \(R\) is the remainder of Theorem 3.1. In particular \(R(\Delta) < 0\) for \(\Delta > 0\), and \(R(\Delta) > 0\) for \(\Delta < 0\).

          Proof

          Differentiating \(P''(y) = \sum t(t+1)c_t(1+y)^{-t-2}\) once more gives \(P'''(y) = -\sum t(t+1)(t+2)c_t(1+y)^{-t-3}\), which is negative when every \(c_t > 0\). The remainder is \(R(\Delta) = \tfrac16 P'''(\theta)\Delta^3\) by the proof of Theorem 3.1, so \(R\) has the sign of \(P'''(\theta)\Delta^3\), which is the sign of \(-\Delta^3\), that is, of \(-\Delta\). \(\blacksquare\)

          Proposition 3.3 (the first-order expansion lies below the price)

          Let \(y_0 > -1\). If every \(c_t > 0\), then \(P(y_0 + \Delta) - \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) \ge 0\) for every \(\Delta\) with \(y_0 + \Delta > -1\).

          Proof

          By the Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with two derivatives, \(P(y_0 + \Delta) - \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) = \tfrac12 P''(\theta)\Delta^2\) for some \(\theta\) between \(y_0\) and \(y_0 + \Delta\). Since \(P'' > 0\) by Proposition 2.1, this is non-negative. \(\blacksquare\)

          Remark 3.4 (what the expansion does not give)

          Theorem 3.1 bounds the error by a constant times \(|\Delta|^3\), where the constant depends on the third derivative over the whole interval between the two yields. The bound holds for a large shift too, but the interval is then wide, \(M\) is taken over it, and this page computes no value of that bound. Proposition 3.3 is an inequality for every shift, and it does not give the size of the error.

          4Where the canon breaks

          The expansion is a statement about one bond with fixed cash flows. It assumes the cash flows do not depend on the yield. A bond with a call or prepayment option can have cash flows that depend on the yield, and the derivatives of Proposition 2.1 assume fixed cash flows, so they do not apply to such a bond as written.

          • The price is a sum of powers of \((1+y)\), not a linear function of \(y\). Proposition 3.3 shows that the tangent line, the graph of the first-order polynomial \(\mathcal{P}_1\) of Theorem 3.1, never lies above the price for a bond with positive cash flows, so a linear approximation never overstates the price at any shift.
          • Money is rounded once, at the end. The price, its derivatives and the Taylor terms are exact rational numbers, computed from the float yields with Fraction, so no rounding happens inside the calculation. The price is rounded to an integer number of cents once, at the last step, with halves to even. Lab 1 rounds only the printed price and the printed expansion to whole cents; its central differences are float checks, and they are printed only as checks.
          • The preview ratios are named, not defined. The lab prints \(-P'/P\) and \(P''/P\). This lesson does not define the terms they are named after, and no claim about their use is made here.

          5Misconceptions

          1. “The second-order expansion gives the bond price exactly.”

            Lab 1 prints a difference of \(-15\) cents between the price and the second-order expansion, both in whole cents, at a shift of \(+0.01\), and Theorem 3.1 bounds the remainder by a constant times a cubic term. The expansion is an approximation, and its error is the remainder, which is not zero.

          2. “A bond whose yield equals its coupon rate has a price different from face.”

            Proposition 2.2 proves that the price equals the face value at that yield. Lab 1 prints 100000 cents at the yield 0.05, which equals the coupon rate of 5 percent.

          3. “A higher yield always raises the price of a bond with positive cash flows.”

            Proposition 2.1 gives \(P'(y) < 0\) when every cash flow is positive, so the price falls as the yield rises. The expansion in Lab 1 has \(P'(y_0) = -851466.74\) cents per unit of yield, which is negative.

          Mechanics

          M1 · Par for a two-year bond

          A bond of face 1000 pays a coupon of 5 percent a year, annually, for 2 years, with the face repaid at year 2. Find its price at yield 0.05.

          Solution

          Here \(F = 1000\), the coupon rate \(r = 0.05\), the yield \(y = 0.05\), \(N = 2\), and the coupon is \(rF = 50\). Proposition 2.2 gives a price equal to the face, 1000. Directly, \(50/1.05 + 1050/1.05^2 = 47.619 + 952.381 = 1000.000\), with the terms rounded to three decimals for display.

          M2 · A single cash flow

          Let a single cash flow \(c\) be paid at year 1, so \(P(y) = c(1+y)^{-1}\). Compute \(P'\) and \(P''\) from Proposition 2.1, and check them by differentiating directly.

          Solution

          Proposition 2.1 with \(N = 1\) gives \(P'(y) = -c(1+y)^{-2}\) and \(P''(y) = 2c(1+y)^{-3}\). Differentiating \(c(1+y)^{-1}\) directly gives \(-c(1+y)^{-2}\), and differentiating again gives \(2c(1+y)^{-3}\), which agrees.

          M3 · A zero-coupon bond

          A zero-coupon bond pays 100 cents at year 2 only. Write its price function and its first derivative at \(y = 0.04\).

          Solution

          \(P(y) = 100(1+y)^{-2}\), so \(P'(y) = -200(1+y)^{-3}\). At \(y = 0.04\), \((1.04)^{-3}\) is the factor, and \(P'(0.04) = -200/1.04^3\), a negative number, which agrees with Proposition 2.1 because the one cash flow is positive.

          Proof

          P1 · A decreasing, convex price function

          Using Proposition 2.1, show that \(P\) is strictly decreasing and convex on \((-1, \infty)\) when every cash flow is positive.

          Solution

          \(P' < 0\) means \(P\) is strictly decreasing. \(P'' > 0\) on an interval means \(P'\) is strictly increasing there, and a differentiable function with strictly increasing derivative is convex. So \(P\) is convex on \((-1, \infty)\). Both facts come from the signs of the terms of \(P'\) and \(P''\) in Proposition 2.1.

          P2 · The sign of the cubic term

          Show that \(P'''(y) < 0\) on \((-1, \infty)\) when every cash flow is positive, and state the sign of \(R(\Delta)\) for \(\Delta > 0\).

          Solution

          Differentiating \(P''\) gives \(P''' = -\sum t(t+1)(t+2)c_t(1+y)^{-t-3}\), and each term is negative. So \(P'''<0\), and \(R(\Delta) = \tfrac16 P'''(\theta)\Delta^3 < 0\) for \(\Delta > 0\), which matches Proposition 3.2.

          P3 · The first-order error sign

          Prove that the first-order error \(P(y_0 + \Delta) - \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta)\) is non-negative for every \(\Delta\), using only Proposition 2.1 and Taylor's theorem.

          Solution

          Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder of order two gives \(P(y_0+\Delta) - \mathcal{P}_1(\Delta) = \tfrac12 P''(\theta)\Delta^2\) for some \(\theta\). Since \(P''(\theta) > 0\) and \(\Delta^2 \ge 0\), the error is non-negative, which is Proposition 3.3.

            A sensitivity computed by a finite difference depends on the step it uses. A step that is too large misses the curvature, a step that is too small loses digits to subtraction, and a function with a kink (a corner where the left and right slopes differ) gives a finite difference that looks stable but has no derivative behind it. This lesson proves the truncation errors of the forward and central quotients, derives the step that balances truncation against round-off under a stated error hypothesis, and shows a kink where the quotients return values with no derivative behind them.

            1Difference quotients and the definition of the derivative

            For a function \(f\) of one variable, a point \(x\), and a step \(h > 0\), write the forward quotient \(D^+_h f(x) = (f(x + h) - f(x))/h\), the backward quotient \(D^-_h f(x) = (f(x) - f(x - h))/h\), and the central quotient \(D^0_h f(x) = (f(x + h) - f(x - h))/(2h)\). These are the finite-difference approximations of Lesson L13. The derivative of Lesson L04 is the limit of the difference quotient over both signs of \(h\), when that limit exists. The forward quotient can have a limit as \(h \to 0^+\) where the derivative does not exist (Theorem 2.1 below). Whenever the derivative exists, the central quotient converges to it too, because \(D^0_h f(x)\) is the average of \(D^+_h f(x)\) and \(D^-_h f(x)\), and both tend to \(f'(x)\) as \(h \to 0^+\). The converse fails: Theorem 2.1 gives a central quotient of \(|x|\) at \(0\) equal to \(0\) at every step, so that limit exists while the derivative does not.

            2A kink returns values with no derivative behind them

            Theorem 2.1 (the quotients of \(|x|\) at its kink)

            Let \(f(x) = |x|\). For every step \(h > 0\), \(D^+_h f(0) = 1\), \(D^-_h f(0) = -1\) and \(D^0_h f(0) = 0\). The function \(f\) has no derivative at \(0\).

            Proof

            For \(h > 0\), \(D^+_h f(0) = (|h| - 0)/h = 1\), and \(D^-_h f(0) = (0 - |{-h}|)/h = -1\). Their average gives \(D^0_h f(0) = (|h| - |{-h}|)/(2h) = 0\). Since the forward quotient is \(1\) for every \(h > 0\) and the backward quotient is \(-1\) for every \(h > 0\), the limit of the difference quotient as \(h \to 0\) does not exist, because the two one-sided limits differ. Therefore \(f\) has no derivative at \(0\) by the definition of Lesson L04. \(\blacksquare\)

            The point of Theorem 2.1 is that a finite-difference value at a fixed step is a number, and a number from a finite step does not show that a limit exists. Here the forward quotient is \(1\) at every step, which looks stable, and the derivative does not exist. Lab 1 Part A prints these values at six steps.

            3Truncation error

            Proposition 3.1 (truncation of the forward quotient)

            Let \(f\) be twice differentiable on \([x, x + h]\), with \(f''\) bounded there by \(M_2\). Then \(|D^+_h f(x) - f'(x)| \le \tfrac12 M_2\,h\).

            Proof

            The Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder of order one (Lesson L08) gives \(\xi \in (x, x + h)\) with \(f(x + h) = f(x) + h f'(x) + \tfrac12 h^2 f''(\xi)\). Dividing the difference \(f(x+h) - f(x)\) by \(h\) gives \(D^+_h f(x) - f'(x) = \tfrac12 h f''(\xi)\), and \(|f''(\xi)| \le M_2\) gives the bound. \(\blacksquare\)

            Proposition 3.2 (truncation of the central quotient)

            Let \(f\) be three times differentiable on \([x - h, x + h]\), with \(f'''\) bounded there by \(M_3\). Then \(|D^0_h f(x) - f'(x)| \le \tfrac16 M_3\,h^2\).

            Proof

            The Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder of order two gives \(\xi_1 \in (x, x+h)\) and \(\xi_2 \in (x-h, x)\) with

            \[f(x + h) = f(x) + hf'(x) + \tfrac12 h^2 f''(x) + \tfrac16 h^3 f'''(\xi_1),\]

            \[f(x - h) = f(x) - hf'(x) + \tfrac12 h^2 f''(x) - \tfrac16 h^3 f'''(\xi_2).\]

            Subtracting, \(f(x+h) - f(x-h) = 2hf'(x) + \tfrac16 h^3\bigl(f'''(\xi_1) + f'''(\xi_2)\bigr)\). Dividing by \(2h\), \(D^0_h f(x) - f'(x) = \tfrac{1}{12}h^2\bigl(f'''(\xi_1) + f'''(\xi_2)\bigr)\), whose absolute value is at most \(\tfrac16 M_3 h^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

            4Round-off and the step that balances the errors

            Round-off is not in the mathematics of Sections 2 and 3, because those proofs use exact values of \(f\). A computer evaluates \(f\) with an error. Subtracting two nearly equal computed values loses digits; this loss is called cancellation. The model below states the evaluation error as a hypothesis.

            Definition 4.1 (the error hypothesis)

            Let \(\tilde f\) denote the computed value of \(f\). The hypothesis is that \(|\tilde f(t) - f(t)| \le \delta\) for every \(t\) at which the quotient is evaluated, for a stated \(\delta > 0\). The model counts only the error in evaluating \(f\). It ignores the rounding of the subtraction and the division, and it is a hypothesis about the arithmetic, not a measurement.

            Proposition 4.2 (the computed forward quotient, under Definition 4.1)

            Under the hypotheses of Proposition 3.1 and Definition 4.1, with \(M_2 > 0\), \(|\tilde D^+_h f(x) - f'(x)| \le \tfrac12 M_2 h + 2\delta/h\). The bound is minimized at \(h^* = 2\sqrt{\delta/M_2}\), where it equals \(2\sqrt{M_2\,\delta}\).

            Proof

            The computed quotient is \((\tilde f(x+h) - \tilde f(x))/h\). Its distance from \(D^+_h f(x)\) is at most \((\delta + \delta)/h = 2\delta/h\), and Proposition 3.1 bounds the distance of \(D^+_h f(x)\) from \(f'(x)\) by \(\tfrac12 M_2 h\). The triangle inequality gives the bound. For \(g(h) = Ah + B/h\) with \(A, B > 0\), \(g'(h) = A - B/h^2\) vanishes at \(h = \sqrt{B/A}\), where \(g\) is minimal, and \(g = 2\sqrt{AB}\) there. With \(A = M_2/2\) and \(B = 2\delta\), \(h^* = \sqrt{4\delta/M_2} = 2\sqrt{\delta/M_2}\) and \(g(h^*) = 2\sqrt{M_2\,\delta}\). \(\blacksquare\)

            Proposition 4.3 (the computed central quotient, under Definition 4.1)

            Under the hypotheses of Proposition 3.2 and Definition 4.1, with \(M_3 > 0\), \(|\tilde D^0_h f(x) - f'(x)| \le \tfrac16 M_3 h^2 + \delta/h\). The bound is minimized at \(h^* = (3\delta/M_3)^{1/3}\).

            Proof

            The computed quotient differs from \(D^0_h f(x)\) by at most \((\delta + \delta)/(2h) = \delta/h\). Proposition 3.2 gives the truncation bound. Put \(g(h) = Ch^2 + B/h\) with \(C = M_3/6\) and \(B = \delta\). Then \(g'(h) = 2Ch - B/h^2\) vanishes when \(h^3 = B/(2C) = 3\delta/M_3\), which is the stated \(h^*\). \(\blacksquare\)

            These are bounds on the error. The step they name minimizes the bound, not the error itself, so the actual minimizer of the error can differ from it. Remark 4.4 shows what that difference can look like.

            Remark 4.4 (a bound is not the error)

            Proposition 4.2 gives an upper bound, and the true error at \(h^*\) is only guaranteed to be at most the bound. The truncation and round-off errors can have opposite signs, so the true error at a given step can be smaller than the bound. Lab 1 Part B prints the true errors on a grid of decade steps, and it does not measure \(\delta\) or \(M_2\).

quotients converge to different limits (+1 and -1 for the one-sided quotients, 0 for the central quotient) instead of
to one derivative.
Part B is a smooth function with cancellation. f(x) = exp(x) at x0 = 1, whose derivative is e. The forward and central
quotients are computed at h = 10**-k for k = 1 to 15. Their errors fall while the truncation error dominates, then
rise again as the subtraction of nearly equal function values loses digits. The lab prints the decade with the smallest
error for each quotient. The grid is decades only, so the printed minimum is a grid result, not the exact optimum.
Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from collections.abc import Callable

KINK_EXPONENTS: tuple[int, ...] = (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6)
SMOOTH_EXPONENTS: tuple[int, ...] = tuple(range(1, 16))
X0 = 1.0


def absolute(x: float) -> float:
    """f(x) = |x|."""
    return abs(x)


def smooth(x: float) -> float:
    """f(x) = exp(x), with f'(x) = exp(x)."""
    return math.exp(x)


def forward(fun: Callable[[float], float], x: float, h: float) -> float:
    """The forward quotient (fun(x + h) - fun(x)) / h. The step h must be positive."""
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    return (fun(x + h) - fun(x)) / h


def backward(fun: Callable[[float], float], x: float, h: float) -> float:
    """The backward quotient (fun(x) - fun(x - h)) / h. The step h must be positive."""
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    return (fun(x) - fun(x - h)) / h


def central(fun: Callable[[float], float], x: float, h: float) -> float:
    """The central quotient (fun(x + h) - fun(x - h)) / (2 h). The step h must be positive."""
    if h <= 0.0:
        raise ValueError(f"the step must be positive, got {h}")
    return (fun(x + h) - fun(x - h)) / (2.0 * h)


def main() -> None:
    print("Part A: f(x) = |x| at x = 0, quotients at h = 10^-k")
    for k in KINK_EXPONENTS:
        h = 10.0**-k
        print(
            f"k = {k}: forward {forward(absolute, 0.0, h):+.6f} "
            f"backward {backward(absolute, 0.0, h):+.6f} central {central(absolute, 0.0, h):+.6f}"
        )

    target = math.e
    print(f"Part B: f(x) = exp(x) at x0 = 1, true derivative e = {target:.10f}")
    best_forward = best_central = (0, math.inf)
    for k in SMOOTH_EXPONENTS:
        h = 10.0**-k
        ef = abs(forward(smooth, X0, h) - target)
        ec = abs(central(smooth, X0, h) - target)
        print(f"k = {k:>2}: h = {h:.0e} forward error {ef:.3e} central error {ec:.3e}")
        if ef < best_forward[1]:
            best_forward = (k, ef)
        if ec < best_central[1]:
            best_central = (k, ec)
    print(f"smallest forward error at h = 10^-{best_forward[0]}: {best_forward[1]:.3e}")
    print(f"smallest central error at h = 10^-{best_central[0]}: {best_central[1]:.3e}")

            5Where the canon breaks

            The error statements in this lesson are bounds or models, and each is conditional on its stated hypotheses. The smooth-function results need the stated differentiability, and the round-off results need Definition 4.1.

            • A stable finite difference does not certify a derivative. Theorem 2.1 gives the same forward quotient at every step for \(|x|\), and no derivative exists. A check on a function must test the function's smoothness at the point, not only the stability of its quotients.
            • The optimal step depends on quantities the user does not know. Proposition 4.2 needs \(M_2\), and Proposition 4.3 needs \(M_3\). They are bounds on higher derivatives over an interval. The page's prediction takes \(M_2 \approx e\) from the known function \(e^x\) (Lab 1 computes no \(M_2\)), and Lab 1 Part B finds the grid minimum by a sweep over decades; the page gives no method for estimating \(M_2\) or \(M_3\) from data.
            • Subtraction of close numbers is the cancellation. The round-off term \(2\delta/h\) is the cancellation in \(\tilde f(x+h) - \tilde f(x)\). An algebraic rewrite can remove the subtraction, as Problem P3 shows for \(\sqrt{1+x} - 1\).

            6Misconceptions

            1. “A smaller step always gives a better finite-difference derivative.”

              Proposition 4.2 bounds the error by \(\tfrac12 M_2 h + 2\delta/h\), which increases as \(h\) falls below \(h^*\). Lab 1 Part B shows the forward error rising from about \(6.6 \times 10^{-9}\) at \(10^{-8}\) to \(2.15 \times 10^{-7}\) at \(10^{-9}\).

            2. “The central quotient gives the derivative of \(|x|\) at \(0\).”

              Theorem 2.1 shows \(D^0_h f(0) = 0\) for every \(h\), while the forward quotient is \(1\) and the backward quotient is \(-1\). The central quotient averages two different one-sided slopes and returns their mean, and that value is not a derivative of \(|x|\) at \(0\), which does not exist.

            3. “A finite-difference value that does not change with the step proves the function is differentiable.”

              The forward quotient of \(|x|\) at \(0\) is \(1\) at every step from \(10^{-1}\) to \(10^{-6}\) (Lab 1 Part A), and \(|x|\) has no derivative at \(0\) by Theorem 2.1. Stability across steps is a property of the quotients, not evidence of differentiability.

            Mechanics

            M1 · Three quotients for \(x^2\)

            For \(f(x) = x^2\) at \(x = 1\), compute \(D^+_h f(1)\), \(D^-_h f(1)\) and \(D^0_h f(1)\) exactly, and state the error of each.

            Solution

            \(D^+_h f(1) = ((1+h)^2 - 1)/h = 2 + h\), so the error is \(h\). \(D^-_h f(1) = (1 - (1-h)^2)/h = 2 - h\), with error \(-h\). \(D^0_h f(1) = ((1+h)^2 - (1-h)^2)/(2h) = 4h/(2h) = 2\), with error zero. The central quotient is exact for a quadratic.

            M2 · The central quotient of \(|x|\)

            For \(f(x) = |x|\) at \(x = 0\) and \(h = 0.5\), compute the central quotient and the forward quotient.

            Solution

            The central quotient is \((|0.5| - |{-0.5}|)/(2 \cdot 0.5) = 0\). The forward quotient is \((|0.5| - 0)/0.5 = 1\). Both agree with Theorem 2.1.

            M3 · A forward quotient of a cubic

            For \(f(x) = x^3\) at \(x = 2\) and \(h = 0.1\), compute \(D^+_h f(2)\) and its error against \(f'(2) = 12\).

            Solution

            \(D^+_h f(2) = (2.1^3 - 2^3)/0.1 = (9.261 - 8)/0.1 = 12.61\), so the error is \(0.61\). Proposition 3.1 gives \(\tfrac12 M_2 h\) with \(M_2 = \sup |6x|\) on \([2, 2.1]\), which is \(12.6\), so \(\tfrac12 \cdot 12.6 \cdot 0.1 = 0.63\), and the actual error \(0.61\) is below it.

            P1 · The kink has no derivative

            From the definition of the derivative as a limit of difference quotients, prove that \(|x|\) has no derivative at \(0\), using the one-sided quotients.

            Solution

            For \(h > 0\), the quotient \((|0 + h| - |0|)/h = 1\), and for \(h < 0\) the quotient is \((|h|)/h = -1\). The limits as \(h \to 0^+\) and \(h \to 0^-\) are \(1\) and \(-1\), which differ. So the two-sided limit does not exist, and \(|x|\) has no derivative at \(0\).

            P2 · Truncation of the forward quotient

            Prove Proposition 3.1 from the Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem with remainder of order one.

            Solution

            Taylor's theorem gives \(f(x+h) = f(x) + hf'(x) + \tfrac12 h^2 f''(\xi)\) for some \(\xi\) between \(x\) and \(x+h\). Subtracting \(f(x)\) and dividing by \(h\) gives \(D^+_h f(x) = f'(x) + \tfrac12 h f''(\xi)\). Taking absolute values and bounding \(|f''(\xi)|\) by \(M_2\) gives the claim.

            P3 · The rewrite that removes cancellation

            Show that \(\sqrt{1+x} - 1 = x/(\sqrt{1+x} + 1)\) for \(x > -1\), and explain why the right side avoids the cancellation of the left side when \(x\) is small.

            Solution

            Multiplying the left side by \((\sqrt{1+x}+1)/(\sqrt{1+x}+1)\) gives \((1 + x - 1)/(\sqrt{1+x}+1) = x/(\sqrt{1+x}+1)\), where the denominator is positive for \(x > -1\). For small \(x\), the left side subtracts two nearly equal numbers, losing digits, while the right side divides \(x\) by a number near \(2\), with no subtraction of nearly equal values.

            Empirical

            E1 · The constant quotient (Lab 1)

            Lab 1 Part A prints \(+1\) for the forward quotient at every step. Explain from Theorem 2.1 why this is the expected output, and say what the printed constancy does not show.

            Solution

            For \(h > 0\), \(D^+_h|x|\) at \(0\) equals \(|h|/h = 1\) exactly, so it is constant in \(h\) by the algebra of the absolute value, not by a limit. The printed constancy does not show differentiability, because the derivative is a limit that does not exist here.

            E2 · The rise at small steps (Lab 1)

            Lab 1 prints a forward error of \(3.263 \times 10^{-5}\) at \(h = 10^{-11}\) and \(3.903 \times 10^{-1}\) at \(h = 10^{-15}\). Explain which error term produces the rise, using Proposition 4.2.

            Solution

            The truncation term \(\tfrac12 M_2 h\) decreases as \(h\) falls, so it cannot produce the rise. The term \(2\delta/h\) grows as \(h\) falls and is the round-off term, which dominates at these steps. At \(h = 10^{-15}\), the difference \(\tilde f(x+h) - \tilde f(x)\) is a difference of values that agree in almost all digits, so the quotient's error is of the size of \(2\delta/h\). With the assumed \(\delta = 2.22 \times 10^{-16}\) of the Lab 1 discussion, that bound is \(0.444\) at \(h = 10^{-15}\), and the printed error \(0.390\) is consistent with it.

            E3 · The central minimum on a grid (Lab 1)

            Lab 1 prints the same central error, \(5.859 \times 10^{-11}\), at \(h = 10^{-5}\) and \(h = 10^{-7}\). State what this means for the grid result and where the predicted minimizer \(6.3 \times 10^{-6}\) lies.

            Solution

            The printed digits are the same for the two decades. The unrounded errors, which the lab does not print and which are obtained by rerunning its central quotient at these two steps without the printed rounding, are \(5.858691 \times 10^{-11}\) at \(10^{-5}\) and \(5.858736 \times 10^{-11}\) at \(10^{-7}\), which differ by about \(4.4 \times 10^{-16}\), one unit in the last place of values near \(e\). The grid's choice of \(10^{-5}\) is therefore a rounding-level decision, not a measured minimum. The predicted minimizer \(6.3 \times 10^{-6}\) lies between \(10^{-5}\) and \(10^{-6}\), closer in logarithm to \(10^{-5}\), and the grid answer \(10^{-5}\) is consistent with it. A finer grid would be needed to decide the minimum, and the lab does not run one.

            R1 · The forward step when \(M_2\) is overestimated

            Suppose only a bound \(M_2\) is known and the round-off hypothesis holds with \(\delta\). State the step the bound recommends and the size of the bound there, and say what happens to that step if \(M_2\) is overestimated by a factor of 100.

            Solution

            Proposition 4.2 recommends \(h^* = 2\sqrt{\delta/M_2}\), with bound \(2\sqrt{M_2\delta}\) there. If \(M_2\) is overestimated by a factor of 100, the recommended step \(2\sqrt{\delta/(100M_2)}\) is one tenth of the correct \(h^*\). At that smaller step the round-off term \(2\delta/h\) equals \(10\sqrt{M_2\delta}\), since \(2\delta/h^* = \sqrt{M_2\delta}\), so it is ten times the round-off term at \(h^*\), which is \(\sqrt{M_2\delta}\). The error is pushed toward the round-off-dominated regime.

            R2 · A Lipschitz function bounds every quotient

            Let \(|f(s) - f(t)| \le L|s - t|\) for all \(s, t\). Show that every forward and central quotient satisfies \(|D^+_h f(x)| \le L\) and \(|D^0_h f(x)| \le L\), and explain why this bound holds even at a kink.

            Solution

            \(|D^+_h f(x)| = |f(x+h) - f(x)|/h \le L h/h = L\), and \(|D^0_h f(x)| = |f(x+h) - f(x-h)|/(2h) \le L \cdot 2h/(2h) = L\). The bound uses only the Lipschitz inequality and not a derivative, so it holds at the kink of \(|x|\), where \(L = 1\) and every quotient in Theorem 2.1 has absolute value at most \(1\).

            R3 · Automatic differentiation without a step

            Explain why the step-balancing argument of Proposition 4.2 does not apply to a method that computes the derivative without a difference quotient, and name the lesson of this unit that treats such a method.

            Solution

            The argument needs a step \(h\) and a difference quotient, and it trades truncation against round-off through \(h\). A method that computes the derivative without a quotient has no step to choose, so the trade-off in Proposition 4.2 does not arise for it. The method is treated in Lesson L14, where forward-mode automatic differentiation is defined. This page makes no claim about its accuracy.
