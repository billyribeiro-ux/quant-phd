      A total over two inputs, such as an expected payoff over a joint range of two factors or a sum of a density over a rectangle of states, can be written as a double integral. Definition 1.1 defines it through sums over cells, and Lab 1 computes an example one variable at a time. This lesson defines the double integral over a rectangle, computes one example in both orders of integration, and shows, with an explicit counterexample in Section 3 that is conditional on an assumed fact about arctan, that the two orders can give different answers when the integrand is not bounded near a point.
      1Double Riemann sums over a rectangle
      Let \(R = [a, b] \times [c, d]\). A partition of \(R\) cuts \([a, b]\) into \(n\) cells and \([c, d]\) into \(m\) cells, giving \(nm\) rectangular cells of widths \(\Delta x_i\) and \(\Delta y_j\). A tag is a point \((\xi_i, \eta_j)\) in each cell. Lesson L09 defines the one-variable definite integral through upper and lower sums, and its Corollary 2.3 shows that for monotone integrands the Riemann sums \(\sum_i f(\xi_i)\,\Delta x_i\) converge to it. The double case uses the same idea with cells.
        Definition 1.1 (double integral over a rectangle)
        A double integral of \(f\) over \(R\), written \(\iint_R f\), is the number \(L\) such that the double Riemann sums \(\sum_{i,j} f(\xi_i, \eta_j)\,\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j\) converge to \(L\) as the largest cell width (the mesh) tends to \(0\), for every choice of tags, when such a number exists. The double integral is not defined by this statement for an integrand that the definition does not cover, and this page never assumes that every integrand is covered.
        Theorem 1.2 (a product-type integrand)
        Let \(R = [0, 1] \times [0, 2]\) and \(f(x, y) = xy + x^2\), which is continuous on \(R\). Then \(\iint_R f\) exists and equals \(\tfrac53\). The one-variable input is that Riemann sums of \(x\), \(x^2\) on \([0,1]\) and of \(y\) on \([0,2]\) converge to their integrals for every choice of tags as the mesh tends to \(0\), which is Corollary 2.3 of Lesson L09 for monotone integrands: \(x\) and \(x^2\) are nondecreasing on \([0, 1]\), and \(y\) is nondecreasing on \([0, 2]\).
        Proof
        Take any partition with cells \(\Delta x_i\), \(\Delta y_j\) and any tags \((\xi_i, \eta_j)\). Since \(f = xy + x^2\), the double Riemann sum splits into two products:
        \[S = \sum_{i,j} (\xi_i \eta_j + \xi_i^2)\,\Delta x_i\,\Delta y_j = \Bigl(\sum_i \xi_i\,\Delta x_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_j \eta_j\,\Delta y_j\Bigr) + \Bigl(\sum_i \xi_i^2\,\Delta x_i\Bigr)\Bigl(\sum_j \Delta y_j\Bigr).\]
        As the mesh tends to \(0\), the three one-variable sums converge: \(\sum_i \xi_i \Delta x_i \to \int_0^1 x\,dx = \tfrac12\), \(\sum_i \xi_i^2 \Delta x_i \to \int_0^1 x^2\,dx = \tfrac13\), and \(\sum_j \eta_j \Delta y_j \to \int_0^2 y\,dy = 2\). Also \(\sum_j \Delta y_j = 2\) exactly. So \(S \to \tfrac12 \cdot 2 + \tfrac13 \cdot 2 = \tfrac53\), for every choice of tags. \(\blacksquare\)
      2Iterated integrals and the order of integration
        Definition 2.1 (iterated integrals)
        The iterated integral with \(x\) integrated first is \(\int_c^d \Bigl(\int_a^b f(x, y)\,dx\Bigr) dy\). The iterated integral with \(y\) integrated first is \(\int_a^b \Bigl(\int_c^d f(x, y)\,dy\Bigr) dx\). Each is computed one variable at a time, using the definite integral of Lesson L09 for the inner integral and the outer integral of the resulting function of the other variable.
        Proposition 2.2 (both orders for the product-type integrand)
        For \(f(x, y) = xy + x^2\) on \([0, 1] \times [0, 2]\), both iterated integrals equal \(\tfrac53\).
        Proof
        With \(y\) integrated first: \(\int_0^2 (xy + x^2)\,dy = 2x + 2x^2\), so \(\int_0^1 (2x + 2x^2)\,dx = 1 + \tfrac23 = \tfrac53\). With \(x\) integrated first: \(\int_0^1 (xy + x^2)\,dx = \tfrac y2 + \tfrac13\), so \(\int_0^2 (\tfrac y2 + \tfrac13)\,dy = 1 + \tfrac23 = \tfrac53\). Each step is a polynomial integral, which the fundamental theorem of calculus (Lesson L10) evaluates. \(\blacksquare\)
        Remark 2.3 (a general statement this page does not prove)
        A general statement says that the two iterated integrals agree for a continuous integrand on a rectangle. This page does not prove that statement, carries no grade for it, and no proof on this page depends on it. Proposition 2.2 is checked for one integrand by direct computation. Lab 1 checks both orders for the same integrand by sums, so it adds no support for the general statement.
        Lab 1 · Iterated midpoint sums in both orders, computed exactly
        The integrand is \(f(x, y) = xy + x^2\) on \([0, 1] \times [0, 2]\), a SIMULATED test integrand chosen for this lab and not market data. Predicted before running: the midpoint rule (Riemann sums with each tag at the centre of its cell) with \(n\) cells in each direction gives, for both orders, the value \(\tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\). The derivation is Proposition 2.4 below. For \(n = 1\) the sums should equal \(\tfrac32\), and for \(n = 2\) they should equal \(\tfrac{13}{8}\). The lab uses exact fractions, so no rounding enters the printed values.
        """M2 lab L20: a double integral over a rectangle, by iterated midpoint sums in both orders.
SIMULATED: the integrand f(x, y) = x * y + x**2 on the rectangle [0, 1] x [0, 2] is a test function chosen for
this lab. It is not market data.
The lab uses exact rational arithmetic (fractions.Fraction), so every printed value is exact. For each n it
computes the iterated midpoint sum with n cells in each direction, once with x as the outer variable and once with
y as the outer variable, and compares each sum with the exact integral 5/3 and with the predicted error -1/(6 n**2).
The lab checks the two orders numerically on this one integrand. It does not invoke any theorem that equates
iterated integrals, and it makes no claim about other integrands.
Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""
from __future__ import annotations
from fractions import Fraction
X_LO, X_HI = Fraction(0), Fraction(1)
Y_LO, Y_HI = Fraction(0), Fraction(2)
EXACT = Fraction(5, 3)
def integrand(x: Fraction, y: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """f(x, y) = x * y + x**2, evaluated exactly."""
    return x * y + x * x
def midpoints(lo: Fraction, hi: Fraction, n: int) -> list[Fraction]:
    """The n midpoints of the n equal cells of [lo, hi]. A cell count below 1 raises ValueError."""
    if n < 1:
        raise ValueError(f"the cell count must be at least 1, got {n}")
    width = (hi - lo) / n
    return [lo + (i + Fraction(1, 2)) * width for i in range(n)]
def sum_x_outer(n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Iterated midpoint sum with x outer: the outer sum over x-midpoints of the inner sum over y-midpoints."""
    dx = (X_HI - X_LO) / n
    dy = (Y_HI - Y_LO) / n
    total = Fraction(0)
    for x in midpoints(X_LO, X_HI, n):
        inner = sum((integrand(x, y) * dy for y in midpoints(Y_LO, Y_HI, n)), Fraction(0))
        total += inner * dx
    return total
def sum_y_outer(n: int) -> Fraction:
    """Iterated midpoint sum with y outer: the outer sum over y-midpoints of the inner sum over x-midpoints."""
    dx = (X_HI - X_LO) / n
    dy = (Y_HI - Y_LO) / n
    total = Fraction(0)
    for y in midpoints(Y_LO, Y_HI, n):
        inner = sum((integrand(x, y) * dx for x in midpoints(X_LO, X_HI, n)), Fraction(0))
        total += inner * dy
    return total
def sum_of_odd_squares_identity(limit: int) -> bool:
    """True when 1**2 + 3**2 + ... + (2n-1)**2 equals n * (4 n**2 - 1) / 3 for every n from 1 to limit."""
    for n in range(1, limit + 1):
        odd_sum = sum((2 * i + 1) ** 2 for i in range(n))
        if 3 * odd_sum != n * (4 * n * n - 1):
            return False
    return True
def main() -> None:
    print("integrand f(x, y) = x*y + x**2 on [0, 1] x [0, 2]; exact integral 5/3")
    for n in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32):
        sx = sum_x_outer(n)
        sy = sum_y_outer(n)
        error_x = sx - EXACT
        error_y = sy - EXACT
        predicted = Fraction(-1, 6 * n * n)
        match = error_x == predicted and error_y == predicted
        print(
            f"n = {n:>2}: x outer {sx}, error {error_x}; y outer {sy}, error {error_y}; "
            f"predicted error -1/(6 n^2) = {predicted}; match {match}"
        )
    print(f"sum of odd squares identity for n from 1 to 50: {sum_of_odd_squares_identity(50)}")

        For \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16\) and \(32\), the two iterated sums are equal, and each differs from \(\tfrac53\) by exactly \(-\tfrac{1}{6n^2}\), as the printed "match True" lines show. The lab computes the sums only at these six values of \(n\), up to 32, and checks the identity used in Proposition 2.4 for \(n\) up to 50. The lab checks this one integrand on these grids only. It does not show that the two orders agree for other integrands, which Remark 2.3 leaves open on this page.
        Proposition 2.4 (the midpoint error for the product-type integrand)
        For every \(n \ge 1\), the iterated midpoint sum with \(n\) cells in each direction equals \(\tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\) in either order of integration.
        Proof
        Two facts first. (i) For \(n\) cells of \([0, 1]\), the midpoints are \(x_i = (2i+1)/(2n)\) for \(i = 0, \dots, n-1\), each cell has width \(1/n\), and \(\sum_{i<n} (2i+1) = n^2\), so \(\sum_i x_i \cdot \tfrac1n = \tfrac12\). (ii) \(\sum_{i<n} (2i+1)^2 = n(4n^2 - 1)/3\). This holds at \(n = 1\), since \(1 = 1 \cdot 3/3\). If it holds at \(n\), then adding \((2n+1)^2\) gives \(\bigl[n(4n^2-1) + 3(2n+1)^2\bigr]/3 = (4n^3 + 12n^2 + 11n + 3)/3 = (n+1)\bigl(4(n+1)^2 - 1\bigr)/3\), which is the formula at \(n+1\). Hence \(\sum_i x_i^2 \cdot \tfrac1n = \tfrac{1}{4n^3}\cdot \tfrac{n(4n^2-1)}{3} = \tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{12n^2}\).
        The midpoint rule for the \(y\)-direction on \([0, 2]\) with \(n\) cells of width \(2/n\) is exact for a linear function, because on each cell the midpoint value equals the cell average of a linear function. So its sum of \(y\) is \(2\) and its sum of \(1\) is \(2\).
        With \(x\) outer: the inner sum over \(y\) gives \(x_i \cdot 2 + x_i^2 \cdot 2\), and the outer sum over \(x\) gives \(2\cdot\tfrac12 + 2\bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{12n^2}\bigr) = \tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\). With \(y\) outer: the inner sum over \(x\) gives \(\tfrac{y_j}{2} + \bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{12n^2}\bigr)\), and the outer sum over \(y\) gives \(\tfrac12 \cdot 2 + 2\bigl(\tfrac13 - \tfrac{1}{12n^2}\bigr) = \tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\). \(\blacksquare\)
        3Where the canon breaks
        The product-type example is the safe case. The next example shows that the two iterated integrals can disagree when the integrand is not bounded near a point.
          Example 3.1 (the two orders disagree)
          Let \(f(x, y) = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2 + y^2)^2\) on \((0, 1] \times (0, 1]\). Since \(\partial_y\bigl[y/(x^2+y^2)\bigr] = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2\), for each \(x \in (0, 1]\) we get \(\int_0^1 f(x, y)\,dy = \tfrac{1}{1 + x^2}\). Since \(\partial_x\bigl[-x/(x^2+y^2)\bigr] = (x^2 - y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2\), for each \(y \in (0, 1]\) we get \(\int_0^1 f(x, y)\,dx = -\tfrac{1}{1 + y^2}\). The outer integrals are \(\int_0^1 \tfrac{dx}{1+x^2} = \tfrac\pi4\) and \(\int_0^1 \bigl(-\tfrac{dy}{1+y^2}\bigr) = -\tfrac\pi4\), assuming Assumption 3.1 (inverse tangent, stated below on this page), which this page does not prove.
        Assumption 3.1 (inverse tangent). \(\arctan\) is differentiable on \(\mathbb{R}\) with derivative \(1/(1+x^2)\), \(\arctan 0 = 0\) and \(\arctan 1 = \pi/4\). This page takes these facts as given.
        The integrand is unbounded near the origin: \(f(t, 0) = 1/t^2\) for \(t \in (0, 1]\). The page does not check the hypothesis of Definition 1.1 for this integrand, which is stated for a closed rectangle, so it makes no claim that the double integral exists. Under Assumption 3.1 it computes the two iterated integrals, which are \(\pi/4\) and \(-\pi/4\), so the two orders of integration give different values.
          Order matters without a hypothesis. Under Assumption 3.1, Example 3.1 shows that the iterated integrals can differ. Proposition 2.2 shows that they agree for the single integrand \(xy + x^2\), and Remark 2.3 leaves the general case unproved on this page.
          The domain of Example 3.1 is not a closed rectangle. Example 3.1 uses the half-open square \((0, 1]^2\), and its integrand is unbounded near the corner at the origin, which is not in that set.
          Non-rectangular regions are outside this lesson. The page defines the double integral over rectangles only, and it makes no claim about regions with curved boundaries.
        4Misconceptions
            “The two orders of integration always give the same value.”
            Example 3.1 gives, under Assumption 3.1, \(\pi/4\) for one order and \(-\pi/4\) for the other on \((0,1]^2\), for an integrand unbounded at the origin. Proposition 2.2 shows agreement for the single integrand \(xy + x^2\) on \([0,1] \times [0,2]\).
            “A double integral of \(xy + x^2\) is the product of two one-variable integrals.”
            The proof of Theorem 1.2 writes the double sum as a sum of two products, one for each term. The term \(xy\) contributes \(\tfrac12 \cdot 2\), and the term \(x^2\) contributes \(\tfrac13 \cdot 2\), and the total \(\tfrac53\) is their sum, not a single product of two integrals.
            “Midpoint sums converge faster than the square of the cell width.”
            This is false for this integrand. Proposition 2.4 gives an error of exactly \(-\tfrac{1}{6n^2}\) for \(n\) cells in each direction, so the error falls like \(1/n^2\), which is the square of the \(x\)-cell width \(1/n\). Lab 1 prints the exact errors \(-\tfrac16, -\tfrac1{24}, -\tfrac1{96}\) for \(n = 1, 2, 4\).
        5Problem set
        Attempt each problem before opening its solution. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.
          Mechanics
            M1 · An iterated integral by hand
            Compute \(\int_0^1 \int_0^2 (x + y)\,dy\,dx\) and \(\int_0^2 \int_0^1 (x + y)\,dx\,dy\).
            Solution
              Inner in \(y\): \(\int_0^2 (x + y)\,dy = 2x + 2\). Outer: \(\int_0^1 (2x + 2)\,dx = 1 + 2 = 3\). Inner in \(x\): \(\int_0^1 (x+y)\,dx = \tfrac12 + y\). Outer: \(\int_0^2 (\tfrac12 + y)\,dy = 1 + 2 = 3\). Both orders give 3.
            M2 · A product integrand
            For \(f(x, y) = x y^2\) on \([0, 2] \times [0, 1]\), compute the iterated integral with \(x\) integrated first and check it with the other order.
            Solution
              With \(x\) first: \(\int_0^2 xy^2\,dx = 2y^2\), and \(\int_0^1 2y^2\,dy = \tfrac23\). With \(y\) first: \(\int_0^1 xy^2\,dy = \tfrac x3\), and \(\int_0^2 \tfrac x3\,dx = \tfrac23\). The two orders agree.
            M3 · A midpoint sum with one cell
            Use one cell in each direction on \([0,1] \times [0,2]\), with midpoint tags, for \(f = xy + x^2\). Compute the double Riemann sum and compare with \(\tfrac53\).
            Solution
              The single midpoint is \((\tfrac12, 1)\), with cell area \(1 \cdot 2 = 2\). Then \(f(\tfrac12, 1) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34\), and the sum is \(2 \cdot \tfrac34 = \tfrac32\). The difference from \(\tfrac53\) is \(-\tfrac16\), which equals \(-\tfrac{1}{6n^2}\) at \(n = 1\) as Proposition 2.4 gives.
          Proof
            P1 · The identity for odd squares
            Prove \(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} (2i+1)^2 = n(4n^2 - 1)/3\) for every \(n \ge 1\), by induction, and state the base case.
            Solution
              Base case \(n = 1\): the left side is \(1\), and the right side is \(1 \cdot 3/3 = 1\). Step: assuming the formula at \(n\), the sum at \(n+1\) is \(n(4n^2-1)/3 + (2n+1)^2\), which equals \((4n^3 + 12n^2 + 11n + 3)/3\). The right side at \(n+1\) is \((n+1)(4n^2 + 8n + 3)/3 = (4n^3 + 12n^2 + 11n + 3)/3\), so the two agree.
            P2 · The midpoint rule is exact for linear functions
            Show that on a cell \([u, v]\) with midpoint \(m = (u+v)/2\), the midpoint value \((v-u)\,f(m)\) equals \(\int_u^v (\alpha + \beta x)\,dx\) when \(f(x) = \alpha + \beta x\).
            Solution
              \(f(m) = \alpha + \beta m\), so \((v-u)f(m) = (v-u)\alpha + \beta (v-u)m\). The integral is \(\alpha(v-u) + \beta(v^2 - u^2)/2 = \alpha(v-u) + \beta (v-u)(v+u)/2\), and \((v-u)\beta(v+u)/2 = (v-u)\beta m\). The two expressions agree.
            P3 · The product form of a double Riemann sum
            Show that for \(f = g(x)h(y)\) and any tags, the double Riemann sum equals \(\bigl(\sum_i g(\xi_i)\Delta x_i\bigr)\bigl(\sum_j h(\eta_j)\Delta y_j\bigr)\).
            Solution
              \(\sum_{i,j} g(\xi_i)h(\eta_j)\Delta x_i \Delta y_j = \sum_i g(\xi_i)\Delta x_i \sum_j h(\eta_j)\Delta y_j\), since the summand is a product of a factor depending only on \(i\) and a factor depending only on \(j\), and a finite double sum of such products factors as a product of sums.
          Empirical
            E1 · The printed errors (Lab 1)
            Lab 1 prints the error \(-1/24\) for \(n = 2\) and \(-1/96\) for \(n = 4\). Check both against Proposition 2.4 by computing \(\tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\) for each.
            Solution
              For \(n = 2\), \(6n^2 = 24\), so the predicted error is \(-1/24\). For \(n = 4\), \(6n^2 = 96\), so the predicted error is \(-1/96\). Both match the printed values exactly, and the lab's exact fractions leave no rounding to explain.
            E2 · Reading the lab for the other order (Lab 1)
            Lab 1 prints the same error for the \(x\)-outer and the \(y\)-outer sums at each of the six printed values of \(n\). Explain from Proposition 2.4 why the two orders agree for this integrand, and say what the printed agreement does not establish.
            Solution
              Proposition 2.4 computes both orders and gets \(\tfrac53 - \tfrac{1}{6n^2}\) for each, so the agreement is a consequence of the two computations. The printed agreement does not establish the general equality of iterated integrals for other integrands, which Remark 2.3 leaves open on this page.
            E3 · Scaling the grid (Lab 1)
            From the printed errors for \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8\), state how the error changes when \(n\) doubles, and explain the rate with the formula of Proposition 2.4.
            Solution
              The printed errors are \(-\tfrac16, -\tfrac1{24}, -\tfrac1{96}, -\tfrac1{384}\), and each is one quarter of the previous. The formula \(-1/(6n^2)\) divides by four when \(n\) doubles, so the rate is \(n^{-2}\).
          Research
            R1 · Where Definition 1.1 fails
            Explain which step of the proof of Theorem 1.2 would fail for \(f(x, y) = (x^2-y^2)/(x^2+y^2)^2\) on \([0,1]^2\), and why Example 3.1 is not a counterexample to Theorem 1.2.
            Solution
              Theorem 1.2 needs a product-type integrand with three one-variable limits, and this integrand is not of that form and is unbounded at the origin. The splitting step of the proof, which writes the sum as a sum of products of one-variable sums, does not apply, because this \(f\) is not a sum of products of one-variable functions with the required limits. The page does not decide whether the double Riemann sums of this \(f\) converge for every choice of tags, so Definition 1.1 is not verified for it. Example 3.1 is not a counterexample to Theorem 1.2, because its integrand is outside the scope of that theorem.
            R2 · The statement that is not proved here
            State precisely a hypothesis under which the two iterated integrals of \(f\) on \([a,b]\times[c,d]\) must agree, and explain why the half-open square \((0, 1]^2\) of Example 3.1 does not satisfy it.
            Solution
              Continuity of \(f\) on the closed rectangle is the hypothesis in the general statement of Remark 2.3, which this page does not prove. The half-open square \((0, 1]^2\) is not a closed rectangle, so the continuity hypothesis of Remark 2.3 does not apply to it. Its integrand is also unbounded near the origin, since \(f(t, 0) = 1/t^2\) for \(t \in (0, 1]\). Under Assumption 3.1, Example 3.1 shows that the two iterated integrals on that square differ. This page does not prove the closed-rectangle statement under the continuity hypothesis.
            R3 · Why the tags must be arbitrary
            Explain why Definition 1.1 requires the limit for every choice of tags and not for midpoint tags only, and give an example of a function where the choice of tags changes the value of a finite sum.
            Solution
              A limit over midpoint tags alone does not show that the integral exists, because a different tag rule may give a different limit for the same integrand. For \(f(x) = x\) on \([0,1]\) with one cell, the tag \(\xi = 1\) gives \(1\), while the midpoint gives \(\tfrac12\), so the value of the sum depends on the tag. Definition 1.1 requires agreement over all tags, which removes this dependence in the limit.
