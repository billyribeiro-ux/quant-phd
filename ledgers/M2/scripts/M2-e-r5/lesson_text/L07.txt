Higher Derivatives and Taylor Polynomials

A first derivative gives the slope of a function at a point, and a second derivative gives the rate at which that slope changes. A Taylor polynomial (Definition 2.1, in Section 2) uses the whole list of derivatives at one point to approximate the function nearby. Its quality is a question of the error, which is the function minus the polynomial. For one function with a closed form, this lesson computes the error exactly, which shows the mechanism that the general remainder of Lesson 8 controls. The lesson also shows that a derivative of order two can fail to exist, even when the first derivative exists everywhere.
1Higher derivatives
Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\). Where the derivative \(f'\) of Lesson 4 exists on \(I\), it is itself a function on \(I\), and it may have a derivative of its own. The notation is recursive: \(f^{(0)} = f\), and \(f^{(k+1)} = (f^{(k)})'\) wherever the right side is defined.
Definition 1.1 (higher derivative)
The k-th derivative \(f^{(k)}\) of \(f\) is defined recursively as above. It exists at a point \(x\) of \(I\) when \(f^{(k-1)}\) exists on an open interval containing \(x\) and its derivative exists at \(x\). Other notations \(f''\) and \(f'''\) are used for \(k = 2\) and \(k = 3\).
Definition 1.2 (class \(C^k\))
A function \(f\) on an open interval \(I\) is of class \(C^k\) when \(f^{(k)}\) exists on \(I\) and is continuous there. The function is of class \(C^0\) when it is continuous on \(I\).
Example 1.3 (a polynomial)
For \(f(x) = x^3\), the first three derivatives are \(3x^2\), \(6x\) and \(6\), and \(f^{(4)}(x) = 0\) for every \(x\). The power rule used here for positive integer exponents follows from the product rule of Lesson 5 by induction on the exponent.
Proposition 1.4 (the geometric function)
Let \(f(x) = 1/(1 - x)\), defined for \(x \ne 1\). Then for every integer \(k \ge 0\) and every \(x \ne 1\),
\[ f^{(k)}(x) = \frac{k!}{(1 - x)^{k+1}}. \]
Proof
Induction on \(k\). For \(k = 0\), the claim is \(f(x) = 1/(1-x)\), which holds. Assume the claim for \(k\), and write \(w(x) = (1 - x)^{k+1}\). For \(x \ne 1\), \(w(x) \ne 0\), and by the chain rule \(w'(x) = -(k+1)(1 - x)^{k}\). The quotient rule for \(1/w\) gives \(-w'/w^2\). Hence, for \(x \ne 1\),
\[ \bigl(f^{(k)}\bigr)'(x) = k!\,\frac{(k+1)(1-x)^{k}}{(1-x)^{2k+2}} = \frac{(k+1)!}{(1-x)^{k+2}}. \]
This is the claim for \(k + 1\). The quotient rule needs \(w(x) \ne 0\), which holds for every \(x \ne 1\), so the argument covers both \(x < 1\) and \(x > 1\).
Example 1.5 (the second derivative at a point)
At \(x = 1/2\), Proposition 1.4 gives \(f''(1/2) = 2/(1/2)^3 = 16\) and \(f'''(1/2) = 6/(1/2)^4 = 96\). A check by the recursion: \(f'(1/2) = 1/(1/2)^2 = 4\), and \(f''(1/2) = 2 \cdot f'(1/2)/(1/2) = 16\).
Lemma 1.6 (finite geometric sum)
For every \(x\) and every integer \(n \ge 0\), \((1 - x)\sum_{k=0}^{n} x^{k} = 1 - x^{n+1}\). Hence, for \(x \ne 1\), \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} x^{k} = (1 - x^{n+1})/(1 - x)\).
Proof
Induction on \(n\). For \(n = 0\), both sides equal \(1 - x\). If the identity holds for \(n\), then \((1 - x)\sum_{k=0}^{n+1} x^k = (1 - x^{n+1}) + (1 - x)x^{n+1} = 1 - x^{n+2}\). The second statement divides by \(1 - x\).
2The Taylor polynomial at 0
A polynomial of degree \(n\) has \(n + 1\) coefficients, and the derivatives of a function at one point determine them. The term Taylor polynomial below is plain prose in this course, not a registered concept, and it is defined here for use in Lesson 8.
Definition 2.1 (Taylor polynomial of degree \(n\) at 0)
Let \(f\) have derivatives \(f^{(k)}(0)\) for \(k = 0, 1, \ldots, n\). The Taylor polynomial of degree \(n\) at 0 is
\[ \mathcal{P}_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\,x^{k}. \]
Its coefficients are the derivatives divided by \(k!\), so it has the same derivatives as \(f\) at 0 up to order \(n\) (Problem L07-P3).
Remark 2.2 (degree 1 is the line with slope \(f'(0)\))
\(\mathcal{P}_1(x) = f(0) + f'(0)\,x\), the line through \((0, f(0))\) with slope \(f'(0)\), where \(f'(0)\) is the derivative of Lesson 4 at the point 0.
Proposition 2.3 (Taylor polynomials of \(1/(1-x)\))
For \(f(x) = 1/(1 - x)\) and every \(n \ge 0\), \(\mathcal{P}_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} x^{k}\), and for \(x \ne 1\),
\[ f(x) - \mathcal{P}_n(x) = \frac{x^{n+1}}{1-x}. \]
Proof
By Proposition 1.4 at \(x = 0\), \(f^{(k)}(0) = k!\), so \(f^{(k)}(0)/k! = 1\) and \(\mathcal{P}_n(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} x^k\). Lemma 1.6 gives \(\mathcal{P}_n(x) = (1 - x^{n+1})/(1 - x)\), so \(f(x) - \mathcal{P}_n(x) = [1 - (1 - x^{n+1})]/(1 - x) = x^{n+1}/(1 - x)\).
Example 2.4 (the error at \(x = 3/10\))
Take \(x = 3/10\). Then \(f(x) = 10/7\). The degree-1 polynomial gives \(\mathcal{P}_1(x) = 1.3\), with error \(10/7 - 1.3 = 9/70\), which is \(x^2/(1-x) = (9/100)/(7/10) = 9/70\) as Proposition 2.3 says. At degree 10 the error is \(x^{11}/(1-x) = (3/10)^{11}/(7/10)\), which is \(2.5307 \times 10^{-6}\) to five significant figures, and Lab 1, part B prints every degree from 0 to 10.
Lab 1 · Taylor polynomials of \(1/(1-x)\) in exact arithmetic
Stated before running: the closed form of Proposition 1.4 equals the recursion at every checked point (part A). At \(x = 3/10\), the error \(f(x) - \mathcal{P}_n(x)\) equals \(x^{n+1}/(1-x)\) exactly at every degree \(n\) from 0 to 10, and each error is \(3/10\) times the error one degree lower (part B). At \(x = 2\) the same identity holds, and the errors double in size with a negative sign (part C). For \(g(x) = x|x|\), the first derivative at 0 is 0 from the definition, and the right and left difference quotients of \(g'(x) = 2|x|\) at 0 are \(+2\) and \(-2\) (part D).
"""M2 L07 lab 1: Taylor polynomials of f(x) = 1/(1 - x) at 0, by degree, in exact rational arithmetic.
Part A checks the closed form f^(k)(x) = k! / (1 - x)^(k+1) against the recursion f^(j+1) = (j+1) f^(j) / (1 - x)
that the quotient rule gives, at three rational points and for k = 0 to 8. Part B takes x = 3/10 and degrees
n = 0 to 10, and checks that the error f(x) - P_n(x) equals x^(n+1) / (1 - x) exactly, where P_n(x) is the Taylor
polynomial of degree n at 0 (the sum of x^k for k = 0 to n, because f^(k)(0) = k!). Part C takes x = 2, outside
the interval |x| < 1, and prints the errors for the same identity. Part D takes g(x) = x |x|. Its first derivative
at 0 is computed from the definition; for x != 0 the page derives g'(x) = 2|x|, and the second derivative at 0 is
tested through
that formula.
Exact arithmetic uses fractions.Fraction throughout. Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""
from __future__ import annotations
from fractions import Fraction
from math import factorial
X_B = Fraction(3, 10)
N_MAX = 10
POINTS_A = (Fraction(3, 10), Fraction(-1, 2), Fraction(9, 10))
K_MAX_A = 8
def f(x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
"""f(x) = 1 / (1 - x) for x different from 1."""
if x == 1:
raise ValueError("f(x) = 1/(1 - x) is not defined at x = 1")
return 1 / (1 - x)
def derivative_formula(k: int, x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
"""The k-th derivative of 1/(1 - x), namely k! / (1 - x)^(k+1), for x different from 1."""
return Fraction(factorial(k)) / (1 - x) ** (k + 1)
def derivative_by_recursion(k: int, x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
"""The k-th derivative by the recursion f^(j+1) = (j+1) f^(j) / (1 - x), starting from f itself."""
value = f(x)
for j in range(k):
value = (j + 1) * value / (1 - x)
return value
def taylor_poly(x: Fraction, n: int) -> Fraction:
"""Taylor polynomial of degree n at 0: sum over k = 0..n of f^(k)(0) x^k / k!, with f^(k)(0) from the formula."""
total = Fraction(0)
for k in range(n + 1):
total += derivative_formula(k, Fraction(0)) * x**k / factorial(k)
return total
def part_a() -> None:
checks = 0
for x in POINTS_A:
for k in range(K_MAX_A + 1):
if derivative_formula(k, x) != derivative_by_recursion(k, x):
raise AssertionError(f"derivative formula fails at x = {x}, k = {k}")
checks += 1
print(f"part A: formula equals recursion at all {checks} checks (x in 3/10, -1/2, 9/10; k = 0 to {K_MAX_A})")
def part_b() -> None:
fx = f(X_B)
print(f"part B: x = 3/10, f(x) = {fx} = {float(fx):.10f}")
previous_error = None
for n in range(N_MAX + 1):
t = taylor_poly(X_B, n)
error = fx - t
if error != X_B ** (n + 1) / (1 - X_B):
raise AssertionError(f"remainder identity fails at n = {n}")
if previous_error is not None and error / previous_error != X_B:
raise AssertionError(f"error ratio is not x at n = {n}")
previous_error = error
print(f"part B: n = {n:2d}  P_n(x) = {float(t):.10f}  error = {float(error):.10f}")
print("part B: error equals x^(n+1)/(1-x) exactly at every degree, and each error is x times the one before")
def part_c() -> None:
x = Fraction(2)
fx = f(x)
print(f"part C: x = 2, f(x) = {fx}; the partial sums P_n(2) are 2^(n+1) - 1, printed below")
for n in range(N_MAX + 1):
t = taylor_poly(x, n)
error = fx - t
if error != x ** (n + 1) / (1 - x):
raise AssertionError(f"remainder identity fails at x = 2, n = {n}")
print(f"part C: n = {n:2d}  P_n(2) = {int(t):6d}  error = {int(error):6d}")
print("part C: the error at n = 10 grows in absolute value, so the degree does not help at x = 2")
def g(x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
"""g(x) = x * |x|."""
return x * abs(x)
def g_prime_for_x_nonzero(x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
"""The derivative of g at x != 0, derived on the page: 2|x|."""
return 2 * abs(x)
def part_d() -> None:
print("part D: first derivative of g at 0 from the definition: (g(h) - g(0)) / h")
for k in range(1, 7):
h = Fraction(1, 10**k)
quotient = (g(h) - g(Fraction(0))) / h
if quotient != abs(h):
raise AssertionError(f"quotient is not |h| at h = {h}")
print(f"part D: h = 1e-{k}, quotient = {float(quotient):.1e}")
print("part D: the quotient equals |h| and tends to 0, so g'(0) = 0")
print("part D: second derivative at 0 from g'(x) = 2|x|: (g'(h) - g'(0)) / h")
for k in range(1, 7):
h = Fraction(1, 10**k)
right = (g_prime_for_x_nonzero(h) - g_prime_for_x_nonzero(Fraction(0))) / h
left = (g_prime_for_x_nonzero(-h) - g_prime_for_x_nonzero(Fraction(0))) / (-h)
if right != 2 or left != -2:
raise AssertionError(f"second-derivative quotients are not +2 and -2 at h = {h}")
print(f"part D: h = 1e-{k}, right quotient = {int(right):2d}, left quotient = {int(left):2d}")
print("part D: the right and left quotients are +2 and -2 at every step, so they have no common limit at 0")
def main() -> None:
part_a()
part_b()
part_c()
part_d()
main()

3The approximation as the degree grows
For \(|x| < 1\), the error of Proposition 2.3 shrinks as the degree grows. The next lemma makes this exact, using the limit laws of Lesson 2 and the fact \(1/m \to 0\) of Lesson 1, Problem M3.
Lemma 3.1 (powers of a number below one)
For \(0 \le t < 1\), \(t^{m} \to 0\) as the integer \(m\) grows.
Proof
If \(t = 0\), the claim is immediate. If \(0 < t < 1\), write \(t = 1/(1 + h)\) with \(h = 1/t - 1 > 0\). By induction, \((1 + h)^{m} \ge 1 + mh\) for every \(m \ge 0\): the case \(m = 0\) is an equality, and \((1 + h)^{m+1} \ge (1 + mh)(1 + h) \ge 1 + (m+1)h\), using \(1 + h > 0\) and \(mh^2 \ge 0\). Therefore, for \(m \ge 1\), \(0 \le t^m \le 1/(1 + mh) \le (1/h)(1/m)\). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Since \(1/m \to 0\) (Lesson 1, Problem M3), there is \(m_0\) with \(1/m \le h\varepsilon\) for every \(m \ge m_0\), and then \(0 \le t^m \le (1/h)(1/m) \le \varepsilon\) for every \(m \ge m_0\). So \(t^m \to 0\).
Theorem 3.2 (convergence inside the interval)
Let \(|x| < 1\). Then \(\mathcal{P}_n(x) \to 1/(1-x)\) as \(n\) grows, and the error satisfies \(|f(x) - \mathcal{P}_n(x)| = |x|^{n+1}/|1 - x| \to 0\).
Proof
By Proposition 2.3, \(|f(x) - \mathcal{P}_n(x)| = |x|^{n+1}/|1-x|\). Since \(|1 - x| \ge 1 - |x| > 0\), the denominator is at least a fixed positive number, and \(|x|^{n+1} \to 0\) by Lemma 3.1 applied to \(t = |x|\). Multiplying a sequence that tends to 0 by a fixed constant gives a sequence that tends to 0 (a limit law of Lesson 2).
Example 3.3 (a factor of \(x\) at each degree)
Proposition 2.3 gives \(\text{error}_n / \text{error}_{n-1} = x\) for every \(n \ge 1\) and \(x \ne 0\), because the error at degree \(n\) is \(x\) times the error at degree \(n-1\). At \(x = 3/10\) this ratio is \(3/10\) at every degree, as Lab 1, part B shows.
4Existence of higher derivatives
The derivatives in Definition 2.1 must exist. A first derivative can exist while the second does not, and the next example shows this for a function that is simple to write down.
Example 4.1 (\(g(x) = x|x|\))
Let \(g(x) = x|x|\). For \(x > 0\), \(g(x) = x^2\), so \(g'(x) = 2x\). For \(x < 0\), \(g(x) = -x^2\), so \(g'(x) = -2x\). At 0, \(\bigl(g(h) - g(0)\bigr)/h = h|h|/h = |h|\) for \(h \ne 0\), which tends to 0. So \(g'(0) = 0\), and in all cases \(g'(x) = 2|x|\).
Proposition 4.2 (no second derivative of \(x|x|\) at 0)
For \(g(x) = x|x|\), the second derivative \(g''(0)\) does not exist.
Proof
By Example 4.1, \(g'(h) = 2|h|\) for every \(h\), and \(g'(0) = 0\). So \(\bigl(g'(h) - g'(0)\bigr)/h = 2|h|/h\), which equals \(2\) for every \(h > 0\) and \(-2\) for every \(h < 0\). The right-hand limit of this quotient at 0 is \(2\) and the left-hand limit is \(-2\). These one-sided limits differ, so the two-sided limit that defines \(g''(0)\) does not exist, by Proposition 2.2 of Lesson 3.
Remark 4.3 (what this blocks)
The Taylor polynomial of degree 1 of \(g\) at 0 exists and equals \(\mathcal{P}_1(x) = 0\). The Taylor polynomial of degree 2 does not exist, because \(g''(0)\) is part of its definition. Lesson 8 requires the existence of the derivative of order \(n+1\) on an interval, which is the stronger condition.
5Where the canon breaks
A derivative of order \(k\) can exist without the derivative of order \(k+1\). Proposition 4.2 gives one example. A Taylor polynomial of degree \(n\) requires derivatives up to order \(n\) at the point, so \(\mathcal{P}_2\) of \(g\) at 0 is undefined even though \(\mathcal{P}_1\) is defined.
The approximation is local. Proposition 2.3 shows that the error at \(x\) is \(x^{n+1}/(1-x)\). This goes to 0 when \(|x| < 1\) (Theorem 3.2). For \(|x| \ge 1\) with \(x \ne 1\) it does not: \(|x|^{n+1} \ge 1\) for every \(n\), so the error has absolute value at least \(1/|1-x| > 0\) at every degree. At \(x = 2\), the error at degree 10 is \(-2048\) (Lab 1, part C), and no degree helps.
Matching derivatives at one point does not fix the function. The polynomial \(\mathcal{P}_n\) uses only the derivatives at 0. The function \(h(x) = \mathcal{P}_n(x) + x^{n+1}\) has the same derivatives as \(\mathcal{P}_n\) up to order \(n\) at 0, yet differs from \(\mathcal{P}_n\) at every \(x \ne 0\). Lesson 8 bounds the difference between \(f\) and \(\mathcal{P}_n\) under its hypotheses.
6Misconceptions
“If \(f'\) exists everywhere, then \(f''\) exists everywhere.”
The function \(g(x) = x|x|\) has \(g'(x) = 2|x|\) at every point, but \(g''(0)\) does not exist (Proposition 4.2). The first derivative can exist at a point where the second does not, because the second derivative needs the first derivative to be differentiable at that point.
“The Taylor polynomial at 0 depends only on \(f(0)\).”
Definition 2.1 uses \(f^{(k)}(0)/k!\) for every \(k\) up to \(n\). For \(f(x) = 1/(1-x)\), the degree-2 polynomial is \(1 + x + x^2\), not \(1 + x\), because \(f''(0)/2! = 1\). Lab 1 does not check these coefficients independently: part A checks the closed form against the recursion at \(x = 3/10\), \(-1/2\) and \(9/10\), and part B builds \(\mathcal{P}_n\) from the closed form at 0, so the value \(f''(0)/2!\) is not checked by the recursion at 0.
“A higher-degree Taylor polynomial is a better approximation at every point.”
Proposition 2.3 gives the error as \(x^{n+1}/(1-x)\). At \(x = 3/10\) it shrinks with the degree, but at \(x = 2\) its absolute value doubles at each degree: at degree 10 the error is \(-2048\) (Lab 1, part C). The approximation converges for \(|x| < 1\) (Theorem 3.2), and at \(x = 2\) the error does not tend to 0 (Section 5, the case \(|x| \ge 1\)).

Mechanics
M2-L07 · Derivatives of the geometric function
For \(f(x) = 1/(1-x)\), find \(f''(0)\), \(f'''(0)\), \(f''(1/2)\) and \(f'''(1/2)\).
Solution
Proposition 1.4 gives \(f^{(k)}(x) = k!/(1-x)^{k+1}\). At \(x = 0\): \(f''(0) = 2!/1 = 2\) and \(f'''(0) = 3!/1 = 6\). At \(x = 1/2\): \((1/2)^3 = 1/8\), so \(f''(1/2) = 2 \cdot 8 = 16\), and \((1/2)^4 = 1/16\), so \(f'''(1/2) = 6 \cdot 16 = 96\). These match Example 1.5 and the closed form.
M2-L07 · A cubic is its own Taylor polynomial
Let \(g(x) = x^3 - 2x + 1\). Find the Taylor polynomial of degree 3 at 0 and compare it with \(g\).
Solution
The derivatives at 0 are \(g(0) = 1\), \(g'(x) = 3x^2 - 2\) so \(g'(0) = -2\), \(g''(x) = 6x\) so \(g''(0) = 0\), and \(g'''(x) = 6\). By Definition 2.1, \(\mathcal{P}_3(x) = 1 + (-2)x + \tfrac{0}{2}x^2 + \tfrac{6}{6}x^3 = 1 - 2x + x^3\). This equals \(g(x)\) for every real \(x\), so the difference \(g - \mathcal{P}_3\) is zero everywhere.
M2-L07 · Degree 2 at one half
For \(f(x) = 1/(1-x)\), find \(\mathcal{P}_2(1/2)\) and the error \(f(1/2) - \mathcal{P}_2(1/2)\).
Solution
Proposition 2.3 gives \(\mathcal{P}_2(x) = 1 + x + x^2\), so \(\mathcal{P}_2(1/2) = 1 + 1/2 + 1/4 = 7/4\). Since \(f(1/2) = 2\), the error is \(2 - 7/4 = 1/4\). Proposition 2.3 predicts \(x^{3}/(1-x) = (1/8)/(1/2) = 1/4\), which agrees.
Proof
M2-L07 · The derivative of \(1/(1-x)\) from the definition
Prove from the definition of the derivative that \(f'(x) = 1/(1-x)^2\) for \(x \ne 1\).
Solution
For \(h \ne 0\) small enough that \(x + h \ne 1\), the difference quotient is \(\dfrac{1}{h}\left(\dfrac{1}{1-x-h} - \dfrac{1}{1-x}\right) = \dfrac{1}{h}\cdot\dfrac{h}{(1-x-h)(1-x)} = \dfrac{1}{(1-x-h)(1-x)}\). As \(h \to 0\), the denominator tends to \((1-x)^2 \ne 0\) by the limit laws of Lesson 2, so the quotient tends to \(1/(1-x)^2\). This is Proposition 1.4 at \(k = 1\).
M2-L07 · The geometric identity by induction
Prove Lemma 1.6 for \(x \ne 1\) by induction on \(n\), without citing the lemma.
Solution
For \(n = 0\), the sum is \(1\) and \((1-x^{1})/(1-x) = 1\). Suppose \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} x^k = (1-x^{n+1})/(1-x)\). Then \(\sum_{k=0}^{n+1} x^k = (1 - x^{n+1})/(1-x) + x^{n+1} = \bigl(1 - x^{n+1} + x^{n+1} - x^{n+2}\bigr)/(1-x) = (1 - x^{n+2})/(1-x)\). This is the claim for \(n+1\).
M2-L07 · Taylor coefficients reproduce the derivatives
Let \(f\) have derivatives up to order \(n\) at 0, and let \(\mathcal{P}_n\) be as in Definition 2.1. Prove that \(\mathcal{P}_n^{(k)}(0) = f^{(k)}(0)\) for \(0 \le k \le n\).
Solution
Write \(\mathcal{P}_n(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} c_j x^j\) with \(c_j = f^{(j)}(0)/j!\). For a power, \(\frac{d^k}{dx^k} x^j = \frac{j!}{(j-k)!}x^{j-k}\) when \(j \ge k\), and it is 0 when \(j < k\). Evaluated at 0, the term \(x^{j-k}\) equals 0 when \(j > k\) and equals 1 when \(j = k\). So \(\mathcal{P}_n^{(k)}(0) = c_k \cdot k! = f^{(k)}(0)\). The case \(j < k\) contributes zero.

M2-L07 · The error ratio in the lab table
Using Lab 1, part B, compute the ratio of the error at degree 5 to the error at degree 4 for \(x = 3/10\), from the printed decimals.
Solution
The printed errors are \(0.0010414286\) at degree 5 and \(0.0034714286\) at degree 4. Their ratio is \(0.0010414286/0.0034714286 = 0.3000000058\), which agrees with \(x = 3/10\) to about seven significant figures (the relative difference is about \(2 \times 10^{-8}\)); the ten-decimal display limits the agreement. Example 3.3 proves that the exact ratio is \(x\) at every degree, so the printed decimals are consistent with the exact value and the ratio is not an artefact of the display.
M2-L07 · The smallest degree for a tolerance
For \(x = -1/2\), find the smallest degree \(n\) such that \(|f(x) - \mathcal{P}_n(x)| \le 0.001\).
Solution
By Proposition 2.3, \(|f(x) - \mathcal{P}_n(x)| = |x|^{n+1}/|1-x| = (1/2)^{n+1}/(3/2) = (2/3)(1/2)^{n+1}\). At \(n = 8\) this is \((2/3)/512 = 0.0013\), which is too large. At \(n = 9\) it is \((2/3)/1024 = 0.00065\), which is at most 0.001. So the smallest degree is \(n = 9\). The formula is exact, so no sampling is needed.
M2-L07 · The sign of the degree-10 error at three points
From Proposition 2.3, state the sign of \(f(x) - \mathcal{P}_{10}(x)\) for \(x = 3/10\), for \(x = -1/2\), and for \(x = 2\), and check the values at \(x = 3/10\) and \(x = 2\) against Lab 1, which prints them. Lab 1 does not print a value at \(x = -1/2\).
Solution
The error is \(x^{11}/(1-x)\). For \(x = 3/10\), the numerator is positive and \(1 - x > 0\), so the error is positive, which Lab 1, part B prints as \(0.0000025307\). For \(x = -1/2\), the numerator \((-1/2)^{11}\) is negative and \(1 - x > 0\), so the error is negative. For \(x = 2\), the numerator is positive and \(1 - x = -1 < 0\), so the error is negative; Lab 1, part C prints \(-2048\) at degree 10.
Research
M2-L07 · The expansion at one half
Find the Taylor polynomial of degree \(n\) of \(f(x) = 1/(1-x)\) at \(x_0 = 1/2\), and its remainder in closed form, for \(x \ne 1\).
Solution
Proposition 1.4 gives \(f^{(k)}(1/2) = k!\,2^{k+1}\), so the polynomial \(\sum_{k=0}^{n} f^{(k)}(1/2)(x - 1/2)^k/k!\) (Definition 2.1 with the centre \(1/2\) in place of 0) has coefficient \(f^{(k)}(1/2)/k! = 2^{k+1}\) on \((x - 1/2)^k\). Put \(u = 2x - 1\). Then \(x = (1+u)/2\) and \(1 - x = (1 - u)/2\), so \(f(x) = 2/(1-u)\). Lemma 1.6 gives \(1/(1-u) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} u^k + u^{n+1}/(1-u)\), so \(f(x) = 2\sum_{k=0}^{n} u^k + 2u^{n+1}/(1-u)\). Since \(2u^k = 2^{k+1}(x - 1/2)^k\), the first sum is the polynomial with the coefficients above. The remainder is \(2u^{n+1}/(1-u)\), which equals \((2x-1)^{n+1}/(1-x)\) because \(1 - u = 2(1 - x)\). It tends to 0 when \(|2x - 1| < 1\), that is when \(0 < x < 1\), by Lemma 3.1.
M2-L07 · A degree bound from the exact error
For \(f(x) = 1/(1-x)\) at \(x = 1/2\), find the smallest \(n\) such that \(|f(x) - \mathcal{P}_n(x)| \le 10^{-6}\).
Solution
Here \(x^{n+1}/(1-x) = (1/2)^{n+1}/(1/2) = (1/2)^n\) by Proposition 2.3. The inequality \((1/2)^n \le 10^{-6}\) means \(2^n \ge 10^6\). Since \(2^{19} = 524288 < 10^6\) and \(2^{20} = 1048576 \ge 10^6\), the smallest degree is \(n = 20\).
M2-L07 · Degree 1 without degree 2
Show that \(g(x) = x|x|\) has a Taylor polynomial of degree 1 at 0, and say which derivative of \(g\) is missing for degree 2.
Solution
Definition 2.1 with \(n = 1\) needs \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g'(0) = 0\), both shown in Example 4.1. So \(\mathcal{P}_1(x) = 0 + 0 \cdot x = 0\). For degree 2, the coefficient \(g''(0)/2!\) is needed, and Proposition 4.2 shows that \(g''(0)\) does not exist. Degree 2 is therefore undefined for \(g\) at 0.
