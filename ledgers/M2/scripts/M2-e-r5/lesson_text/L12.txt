Improper Integrals
Uses: the definite integral and its Darboux sums of Lesson 9, the fundamental theorem of Lesson 10, the power rule and quotient rule of Lessons 5 and 10, and the limit of a sequence of Lesson 1, and Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here)

Some quantities accumulate over an unbounded horizon, such as a continuous payoff that runs forever, and such a quantity is an integral over an unbounded interval. Such an integral is defined through a limit of ordinary integrals, and it may not have a finite value. This lesson defines the improper integral, evaluates the power integrals exactly, and proves that the integral of \(1/x\) diverges even though \(1/x\) tends to zero. It closes with a research problem that bounds the partial sums of the series \(\sum_k k^{-p}\) by an integral, for integers \(p \ge 2\). Each result is proved here from Lessons 1 to 10 and Assumptions 2.1 (Lesson 6) and 2.2 (stated where used); Lab 1 prints the truncation errors.
1Definition
Definition 1.1 (improper integral over \([a, \infty)\))
Let \(f\) be integrable on \([a, b]\) for every \(b \ge a\). The improper integral of \(f\) over \([a, \infty)\) is
\[ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \]
when the limit exists and is a finite number. The integral is then convergent. Otherwise it is divergent. The integrals \(\int_a^b f\) are the truncated integrals, and the limit is taken in the sense that for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(B\) with \(|\int_a^b f - L| \le \varepsilon\) for all \(b \ge B\).
Remark 1.2 (the limit along a sequence)
If the limit in Definition 1.1 exists and equals \(L\), then \(\int_a^{b_m} f \to L\) for every sequence \(b_m \to \infty\). This follows from the definition, because the sequence eventually lies beyond any \(B\). Theorem 3.3 uses this direction: a sequence \(b_m \to \infty\) along which the truncated integrals are unbounded rules out a finite limit.
2The power integrals
Lemma 2.1 (inverse powers tend to zero)
For every integer \(m \ge 1\), \(b^{-m} \to 0\) as \(b \to \infty\).
Proof
For \(b \ge 1\), \(b^m \ge b\), so \(0 < b^{-m} \le b^{-1}\). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\), and put \(B = \max(1, 1/\varepsilon)\). For \(b \ge B\), \(0 < b^{-1} \le \varepsilon\), so \(0 < b^{-m} \le b^{-1} \le \varepsilon\). Hence \(b^{-m} \to 0\) as \(b \to \infty\).
Theorem 2.2 (power integrals)
Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(p \ge 2\) be an integer and \(b > 1\) (for \(b = 1\) both sides are 0 by the convention of Lesson 9, Definition 1.3). Then
\[ \int_1^b x^{-p}\,dx = \frac{1 - b^{1-p}}{p-1}. \]
Hence \(\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx\) converges to \(1/(p-1)\), and the truncation error at \(b\) is exactly \(b^{1-p}/(p-1)\).
Proof
Let \(w(x) = (p-1)x^{p-1}\) and \(F = -1/w\) on \((0, \infty)\). The power rule gives \(w'(x) = (p-1)^2 x^{p-2}\), and the quotient rule for \(-1/w\) gives \(F' = w'/w^2 = (p-1)^2 x^{p-2}/\bigl((p-1)^2 x^{2p-2}\bigr) = x^{-p}\). On \([1, b]\), \(f(x) = x^{-p}\) is positive and nonincreasing, so it is integrable by Theorem 2.2 of Lesson 9, and \(F\) is continuous there. Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 10 gives
\[ \int_1^b x^{-p}\,dx = F(b) - F(1) = -\frac{1}{(p-1)b^{p-1}} + \frac{1}{p-1} = \frac{1 - b^{1-p}}{p-1}. \]
Since \(p - 1 \ge 1\), Lemma 2.1 gives \(b^{1-p} \to 0\), so the limit is \(1/(p-1)\). The error is \(1/(p-1) - (1 - b^{1-p})/(p-1) = b^{1-p}/(p-1)\).
Example 2.3 (\(p = 2\) and \(p = 3\))
By Theorem 2.2 (under Assumption 2.1), for \(p = 2\), \(\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = 1\), and the truncation error at \(b\) is \(1/b\). For \(p = 3\), \(\int_1^\infty x^{-3}\,dx = 1/2\), and the error is \(1/(2b^2)\). Lab 1, parts A and B print these errors and their scaled values.
Lab 1 · Truncated power integrals, and the lower sums of \(1/x\)
Stated before running: for \(f(x) = 1/x^2\), the truncated integral from 1 to \(b\) has error exactly \(1/b\) against the improper value 1, so \(b\) times the error is 1 at \(b = 10, 100, 1000, 10000\) (part A). For \(f(x) = 1/x^3\), the error is \(1/(2b^2)\) against the value \(1/2\), so \(b^2\) times the error is \(1/2\) at \(b = 10, 100, 1000\) (part B). For \(f(x) = 1/x\), the lower sums \(H_N - 1\) on \([1, N]\), where \(H_N = \sum_{i=1}^{N} 1/i\) is the harmonic sum (introduced in Lemma 3.1 below), with \(N = 2^m\) are at least \(m/2\) for \(m = 1\) to \(14\) (part C).
"""M2 L12 lab 1: truncated integrals against their improper limits, and the divergence of the integral of 1/x.
Part A uses f(x) = 1/x^2 on [1, infinity). The truncated integral from 1 to b equals 1 - 1/b exactly, by the
antiderivative F(x) = -1/x, so its error against the improper value 1 is exactly 1/b, and b times that error is 1
for every b in the table. Part B uses f(x) = 1/x^3, whose truncated integral is (1 - 1/b^2)/2, with error 1/(2 b^2),
so b^2 times the error is 1/2. Part C takes f(x) = 1/x on [1, N] with the uniform partition into unit steps. Since f
is decreasing, the infimum on [k, k+1] is 1/(k+1), so the lower sum is H_N - 1, where H_N is the N-th harmonic
number. For N = 2^m the program checks H_N - 1 >= m/2, which is the bound of Lemma 3.1 of the lesson; Theorem 3.3
uses it to show that the truncated integrals are unbounded. Parts A and B use exact fractions; Part C uses floating
point for the harmonic sums. Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""

from fractions import Fraction
B_VALUES_A = (10, 100, 1000, 10000)
B_VALUES_B = (10, 100, 1000)
M_VALUES_C = tuple(range(1, 15))
M_DISPLAY_C = (1, 4, 8, 12, 14)
def part_a() -> None:
print("part A: f(x) = 1/x^2, truncated integral from 1 to b, improper value 1")
print("part A:       b       truncated value     error      b * error")
for b in B_VALUES_A:
value = (-Fraction(1, b)) - (-Fraction(1))
error = Fraction(1) - value
if error != Fraction(1, b):
raise AssertionError(f"error is not 1/b at b = {b}")

raise AssertionError(f"b * error is not 1 at b = {b}")
print(f"part A: {b:8d}   {float(value):16.10f}  {float(error):12.10f}  {float(b * error):8.4f}")
print("part A: the error is exactly 1/b, so b times the error is 1 at every b in the table")
def part_b() -> None:
print("part B: f(x) = 1/x^3, truncated integral from 1 to b, improper value 1/2")
print("part B:       b       truncated value     error      b^2 * error")
for b in B_VALUES_B:
value = (-Fraction(1, 2 * b * b)) - (-Fraction(1, 2))
error = Fraction(1, 2) - value
if error != Fraction(1, 2 * b * b):
raise AssertionError(f"error is not 1/(2 b^2) at b = {b}")
if b * b * error != Fraction(1, 2):
raise AssertionError(f"b^2 * error is not 1/2 at b = {b}")
print(f"part B: {b:8d}   {float(value):16.10f}  {float(error):12.10f}  {float(b * b * error):8.4f}")
print("part B: the error is exactly 1/(2 b^2), so b^2 times the error is 1/2 at every b in the table")
def part_c() -> None:
print("part C: f(x) = 1/x on [1, N], unit steps, lower sum H_N - 1 (floating point)")
print("part C:      N = 2^m       H_N - 1      m / 2     H_N - 1 >= m/2")
harmonic = 0.0
n = 1
for m in M_VALUES_C:
while n < 2**m:
n += 1
harmonic += 1.0 / n
lower = harmonic
if lower < m / 2:
raise AssertionError(f"H_N - 1 is below m/2 at N = 2^{m}")
if m in M_DISPLAY_C:
print(f"part C: {2**m:10d}  {lower:12.8f}  {m / 2:9.4f}     True")
print("part C: the bound H_N - 1 >= m/2 holds for m = 1 to 14; the bound m/2 grows without limit (Theorem 3.3)")
def main() -> None:
part_a()
part_b()
part_c()
main()

3The integral of \(1/x\) diverges
Lemma 3.1 (harmonic blocks)
Let \(H_N = \sum_{i=1}^{N} 1/i\). For every integer \(m \ge 0\), \(H_{2^m} \ge 1 + m/2\).
Proof
Induction on \(m\). For \(m = 0\), \(H_1 = 1 \ge 1\). Assume \(H_{2^m} \ge 1 + m/2\). Then \(H_{2^{m+1}} - H_{2^m} = \sum_{i = 2^m + 1}^{2^{m+1}} 1/i\). This sum has \(2^m\) terms, each at least \(1/2^{m+1}\), so it is at least \(2^m/2^{m+1} = 1/2\). Hence \(H_{2^{m+1}} \ge 1 + m/2 + 1/2 = 1 + (m+1)/2\).
Lemma 3.2 (a lower sum for \(1/x\))
For every integer \(N \ge 2\), \(\int_1^N dx/x \ge H_N - 1\).
Proof
The function \(f(x) = 1/x\) is positive and nonincreasing on \([1, N]\), so it is integrable there. Take the partition of \([1, N]\) into the \(N - 1\) unit intervals \([k, k+1]\). On each, the infimum of \(f\) is at the right endpoint, \(f(k+1) = 1/(k+1)\). So the lower sum is \(L(f, P) = \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} 1/(k+1) = \sum_{j=2}^{N} 1/j = H_N - 1\). By Definition 1.3 of Lesson 9, \(L(f, P) \le \int_1^N f\).
Theorem 3.3 (divergence of \(\int_1^\infty dx/x\))
The improper integral \(\int_1^\infty dx/x\) diverges. Its truncated integrals are at least \(m/2\) at \(b = 2^m\), so they are unbounded.
Proof
Let \(G(b) = \int_1^b dx/x\). By Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 3.1, \(G(2^m) \ge H_{2^m} - 1 \ge m/2\) for every \(m \ge 1\). Suppose the improper integral converged to a finite \(L\). Then \(G(b) \to L\) as \(b \to \infty\), and so \(G(2^m) \to L\) along the sequence \(b_m = 2^m\) (Remark 1.2). But \(G(2^m) \ge m/2\), and \(m/2 \to \infty\), so \(G(2^m)\) cannot converge. This contradiction shows that the improper integral diverges.
Remark 3.4 (the integrand tends to zero)
The function \(1/x\) satisfies \(1/x \to 0\) as \(x \to \infty\), and Theorem 3.3 still shows divergence. The truncated integrals are unbounded because each block \(\{2^m + 1, \ldots, 2^{m+1}\}\), as used in the proof of Lemma 3.1, contributes at least \(1/2\) to \(H_N\), and Lemma 3.2 bounds the truncated integral below by \(H_N - 1\). Among integer exponents \(p \ge 1\), the integral converges for every integer \(p \ge 2\) (Theorem 2.2, under Assumption 2.1) and diverges at \(p = 1\) (Theorem 3.3); this lesson does not treat \(p \le 0\).
4Where the canon breaks
A truncation is not the value. The truncated integral \(\int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx = 1 - 1/b\) is never equal to 1 at any finite \(b\). The error \(1/b\) falls by a factor of 10 each time \(b\) is multiplied by 10, and Lab 1, part A prints it for four values of \(b\).
Among integer exponents \(p \ge 1\), the exponent decides convergence. Theorem 2.2 and Theorem 3.3 together show that \(x^{-p}\) integrates to a finite value for integers \(p \ge 2\) and diverges at \(p = 1\). This lesson does not treat \(p \le 0\). Non-integer powers need real exponents, which are not established in this lesson, so no claim is made for them.
The definition needs integrability on every finite interval. The finite integrals used in this lesson are over an interval \([a,b]\) on which the integrand is monotone, which Lesson 9 covers (Theorem 2.2): \([1,b]\) in the theorems, and \([2,b]\), \([a,b]\) with \(a > 0\) and \([\varepsilon,1]\) in the problems. Problem R2 defines \(\int_0^1 f\) as a limit of integrals over \([\varepsilon,1]\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0^+\), an integral at a finite endpoint that Definition 1.1 does not cover; that limit is a separate definition given in Problem R2. An integrand that is not integrable on some \([a, b]\) is outside Definition 1.1, and this lesson makes no claim about it.
5Misconceptions
“A large enough truncation gives the exact improper value.”
The truncation error of \(\int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx\) is exactly \(1/b\), which is positive at every finite \(b\) (Theorem 2.2). At \(b = 10000\), the truncated value is \(0.9999\), not 1 (Lab 1, part A). A large \(b\) makes the error small, and the value is reached only in the limit.
“If \(f(x) \to 0\) as \(x \to \infty\), then \(\int_1^\infty f\) converges.”
For \(f(x) = 1/x\), the integrand tends to 0, but the truncated integrals are at least \(m/2\) at \(b = 2^m\) (Theorem 3.3), so the improper integral diverges. Lab 1, part C prints \(H_{16384} - 1 = 9.28130671\), which is above the bound \(7\) at \(m = 14\).
“\(\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx\) converges for every \(p > 0\).”
The case \(p = 1\) diverges (Theorem 3.3), and the case \(p \ge 2\) converges (Theorem 2.2, under Assumption 2.1), so the belief fails. This lesson proves nothing for \(0 < p < 1\).

Mechanics
M2-L12 · The integral of \(x^{-4}\)
Evaluate \(\int_1^\infty x^{-4}\,dx\) by Theorem 2.2.
Solution
Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 2.2 with \(p = 4\) gives \(1/(p-1) = 1/3\). The truncated integral is \((1 - b^{-3})/3\), which tends to \(1/3\) by Lemma 2.1. So the improper integral is \(1/3\).
M2-L12 · The truncation at \(b = 100\)
For \(f(x) = 1/x^2\), find the truncated integral from 1 to 100 and its error against the improper value 1.
Solution
Theorem 2.2 gives \(\int_1^{100} x^{-2}\,dx = 1 - 1/100 = 0.99\), and the error is \(1/100 = 0.01\). This matches Lab 1, part A, which prints the value \(0.9900000000\) and the error \(0.0100000000\) at \(b = 100\).
M2-L12 · An integral starting at 2
Evaluate \(\int_2^\infty x^{-3}\,dx\), using the antiderivative \(F(x) = -1/(2x^2)\).
Solution
The derivative of \(-1/(2x^2)\) is \(x^{-3}\), by the quotient rule and the power rule. By Lesson 10, Theorem 2.1 (under Assumption 2.1), the truncated integral from 2 to \(b\) is \(F(b) - F(2) = -1/(2b^2) + 1/8\). As \(b \to \infty\), \(1/(2b^2) \to 0\) by Lemma 2.1, so the improper integral is \(1/8\).
Proof
M2-L12 · Inverse powers tend to zero by induction
Prove Lemma 2.1 for every integer \(m \ge 1\) without the squeeze argument, by induction on \(m\).
Solution
For \(m = 1\), let \(\varepsilon > 0\), put \(B = \max(1, 1/\varepsilon)\), and let \(b \ge B\). Then \(b \ge 1\) gives \(0 < b^{-1}\), and \(b \ge 1/\varepsilon\) gives \(b^{-1} \le \varepsilon\), so \(b^{-1} \to 0\). Assume \(b^{-m} \to 0\), and let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Take \(B\) with \(b^{-m} \le \varepsilon\) for \(b \ge B\), and put \(B' = \max(B, 1)\). For \(b \ge B'\), \(0 < b^{-(m+1)} = b^{-m} \cdot b^{-1} \le b^{-m} \le \varepsilon\), because \(0 < b^{-1} \le 1\). So \(b^{-(m+1)} \to 0\).
M2-L12 · The harmonic bound by induction
Prove Lemma 3.1 by induction on \(m\), checking the step explicitly.
Solution
For \(m = 0\), \(H_1 = 1\). Assume \(H_{2^m} \ge 1 + m/2\). The increment \(H_{2^{m+1}} - H_{2^m}\) is the sum of the reciprocals of the integers from \(2^m + 1\) to \(2^{m+1}\), which are \(2^m\) numbers, each at least \(1/2^{m+1}\). So the increment is at least \(2^m/2^{m+1} = 1/2\), and \(H_{2^{m+1}} \ge 1 + m/2 + 1/2\).
M2-L12 · Two sequences, one limit
Suppose \(\int_1^b f\) tends to \(L\) as \(b \to \infty\). Prove that \(\int_1^{2^m} f \to L\) as \(m \to \infty\), and explain why this is used in Theorem 3.3.
Solution
Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), there is \(B\) with \(|\int_1^b f - L| \le \varepsilon\) for \(b \ge B\). Since \(2^m \to \infty\), there is \(m_0\) with \(2^m \ge B\) for \(m \ge m_0\), and then \(|\int_1^{2^m} f - L| \le \varepsilon\). So the subsequence converges to \(L\). Theorem 3.3 uses this with \(f = 1/x\): the subsequence values are at least \(m/2\), which cannot converge, so the full limit cannot exist.
Empirical
M2-L12 · The error at \(b = 10000\)
Lab 1, part A prints an error of \(0.0001000000\) at \(b = 10000\) for \(f(x) = 1/x^2\). Recompute it from Theorem 2.2.
Solution
Theorem 2.2 gives the error \(b^{1-p}/(p-1)\) with \(p = 2\), which is \(1/b = 1/10000 = 0.0001\). The printed value agrees exactly.
M2-L12 · How far to truncate
For \(f(x) = 1/x^2\), how large must \(b\) be so that the truncated integral is within \(10^{-6}\) of 1?
Solution
The error is \(1/b\), so the condition is \(1/b \le 10^{-6}\), that is \(b \ge 10^6\). At \(b = 10^6\) the error is exactly \(10^{-6}\), which meets the bound. Lab 1 does not print this value, and the answer comes from Theorem 2.2.
M2-L12 · The gap to the bound at \(N = 16384\)
Lab 1, part C prints \(H_{16384} - 1 = 9.28130671\) at \(N = 2^{14}\). Compare it with the bound \(m/2 = 7\) for \(m = 14\).
Solution
The gap is \(9.28130671 - 7 = 2.28130671\), which is positive, as the lab's check requires. The lab prints \(H_N - 1\), so \(H_{16384} = 10.28130671\) at this \(N\). The bound \(m/2\) is a lower bound from Lemma 3.1, and the printed value is larger than it, so the bound is not tight at this \(N\).
Research
M2-L12 · The integral from \(a\) to infinity in general
For integer \(p \ge 2\) and \(a > 0\), prove \(\int_a^\infty x^{-p}\,dx = a^{1-p}/(p-1)\).
Solution
Use the antiderivative \(F(x) = -1/((p-1)x^{p-1})\) from the proof of Theorem 2.2, which is valid on \((0, \infty)\), so it covers \([a, b]\) for every \(a > 0\). By Lesson 10, Theorem 2.1 (under Assumption 2.1), the truncated integral is \(F(b) - F(a) = -1/((p-1)b^{p-1}) + 1/((p-1)a^{p-1})\). By Lemma 2.1 the first term tends to 0, so the limit is \(1/((p-1)a^{p-1}) = a^{1-p}/(p-1)\).
M2-L12 · An integral at a finite endpoint
Define \(\int_0^1 f = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+} \int_\varepsilon^1 f\), when the limit exists. Show that \(\int_0^1 x^{-2}\,dx\) diverges.
Solution
For \(0 < \varepsilon < 1\), Lesson 10, Theorem 2.1 (under Assumption 2.1) gives \(\int_\varepsilon^1 x^{-2}\,dx = F(1) - F(\varepsilon)\) with \(F(x) = -1/x\), which is \(-1 + 1/\varepsilon\). As \(\varepsilon \to 0^+\), \(1/\varepsilon\) grows without bound, so the limit does not exist as a finite number. The integral diverges.
M2-L12 · A series tested by an integral
For integer \(p \ge 2\), prove that the partial sums of \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty k^{-p}\) are bounded. Convergence uses the following assumption, which this course does not prove.
Assumption 2.2 (monotone convergence property). Every nondecreasing sequence of real numbers that is bounded above has a finite limit.
Solution
Let \(f(x) = x^{-p}\), which is decreasing on \([1, N]\). The lower sum for the unit partition is \(L(f, P) = \sum_{k=2}^{N} k^{-p}\), since the infimum on \([k-1, k]\) is \(k^{-p}\). By Definition 1.3 of Lesson 9, \(L(f, P) \le \int_1^N x^{-p}\,dx\). Theorem 2.2 (under Assumption 2.1) gives \(\int_1^N x^{-p}\,dx \le 1/(p-1)\). So \(1 + \sum_{k=2}^{N} k^{-p} \le 1 + 1/(p-1)\), a bound that does not depend on \(N\). The partial sums are nondecreasing and bounded by \(1 + 1/(p-1)\), so, assuming Assumption 2.2, they converge and the series converges.
