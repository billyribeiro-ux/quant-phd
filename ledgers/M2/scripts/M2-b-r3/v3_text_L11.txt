   52 Some integrals cannot be read off a list of standard antiderivatives, and the two rules of this lesson turn such an integral into a simpler one. Integration by parts moves a derivative from one factor to the other. Substitution rewrites the integral in a new variable, and it changes the limits as well as the integrand. Both rules are consequences of the fundamental theorem of Lesson 10, and each has hypotheses that must be checked before the rule is used. This lesson states the hypotheses, proves each rule, and checks the identities numerically in Lab 1.

   54 Linearity and polynomials
   57 Lemma 1.1
   57 (linearity)
   58 Let \(f\) and \(g\) be integrable on \([a, b]\), and let \(c\) be a real number. Then \(f + g\) and \(cf\) are integrable on \([a, b]\), and \[ \int_a^b (f + g) = \int_a^b f + \int_a^b g, \qquad \int_a^b cf = c\int_a^b f. \]
   63 Proof
   64 Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Since \(I_* = I^*\) for \(f\), there are partitions \(P_1\) and \(P_2\) with \(U(f, P_1) - L(f, P_1) < \varepsilon\) and \(U(g, P_2) - L(g, P_2) < \varepsilon\). Let \(R\) be the partition of all points of \(P_1\) and \(P_2\). By Lemma 1.1 of Lesson 9, refining does not increase \(U - L\), so both differences are below \(\varepsilon\) on \(R\). On each subinterval of \(R\), \(\sup(f + g) \le \sup f + \sup g\) and \(\inf(f+g) \ge \inf f + \inf g\). Hence \(U(f+g, R) - L(f+g, R) \le [U(f,R) - L(f,R)] + [U(g,R) - L(g,R)] < 2\varepsilon\). Since \(\varepsilon\) is arbitrary, \(f + g\) is integrable. Moreover \(L(f,R) + L(g,R) \le L(f+g,R) \le \int (f+g) \le U(f+g,R) \le U(f,R) + U(g,R)\), while \(\int f\) and \(\int g\) lie in the brackets \([L(f,R), U(f,R)]\) and \([L(g,R), U(g,R)]\). So \(\bigl|\int(f+g) - \int f - \int g\bigr| < 2\varepsilon\), and the sum formula follows. For the scalar case, if \(c \ge 0\) then \(U(cf, P) = cU(f,P)\) and \(L(cf,P) = cL(f,P)\) for every partition, so \(cf\) is integrable and \(\int cf = c\int f\). If \(c < 0\), then \(U(cf,P) = cL(f,P)\) and \(L(cf,P) = cU(f,P)\), which gives the same conclusion with the roles of the two sums swapped.
   72 Corollary 1.2
   72 (polynomials)
   73 Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), for every polynomial \(p(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{m} c_k x^k\) and every \(b > 0\), \(p\) is integrable on \([0, b]\), and \(\int_0^b p = \sum_{k=0}^{m} c_k\, b^{k+1}/(k+1)\).
   77 Proof
   78 Each power \(x^k\) is nondecreasing on \([0, b]\), as shown in the proof of Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 10, so it is integrable by Theorem 2.2 of Lesson 9. For \(b = 0\) both sides are 0 by the convention of Lesson 9, Definition 1.3. Lemma 1.1 makes every finite combination integrable, and Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 10 gives the value of each term.

   81 Integration by parts
   84 Theorem 2.1
   84 (integration by parts)
   85 Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(a < b\), and let \(u\) and \(v\) be differentiable on an open interval \(I\) that contains \([a, b]\). Suppose that \(u'v\) and \(uv'\) are integrable on \([a, b]\). Then \[ \int_a^b u(x)\,v'(x)\,dx = \bigl[uv\bigr]_a^b - \int_a^b u'(x)\,v(x)\,dx, \] where \([uv]_a^b = u(b)v(b) - u(a)v(a)\).
   91 Proof
   92 Let \(F = uv\). It is differentiable on \(I\) by the product rule of Lesson 5, so it is continuous on \([a, b]\) (differentiable functions are continuous, Lesson 4). Its derivative is \(F' = u'v + uv'\) on \(I\). By Lemma 1.1, \(f = u'v + uv'\) is integrable on \([a, b]\), so Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 10 gives \(\int_a^b (u'v + uv') = F(b) - F(a)\). Lemma 1.1 splits the left side into \(\int_a^b u'v + \int_a^b uv'\). Rearranging gives the formula.
   96 Example 2.2
   96 (a Beta-type integral)
   97 Take \(u(x) = x^2\) and \(v'(x) = (1 - x)^3\), so \(v(x) = -(1 - x)^4/4\). Both \(u'v = -x(1-x)^4/2\) and \(uv' = x^2(1-x)^3\) are polynomials, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\) by Corollary 1.2. Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 2.1 applies, and its boundary term is \([uv]_0^1 = 0\), because \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\). So \[ \int_0^1 x^2(1-x)^3\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 x(1-x)^4\,dx. \] Expanding \(x(1-x)^4 = x - 4x^2 + 6x^3 - 4x^4 + x^5\) and integrating term by term with Corollary 1.2 gives \(1/2 - 4/3 + 3/2 - 4/5 + 1/6 = 1/30\). Hence \(\int_0^1 x^2(1-x)^3\,dx = 1/60\), which Lab 1, part A checks numerically.
  103 Example 2.3
  103 (a boundary term that does not vanish)
  104 Take \(u(x) = x\) and \(v(x) = x\), so \(u'v = 1\cdot x\) and \(uv' = x\). The functions \(u'v = x\) and \(uv' = x\) are polynomials, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\) by Corollary 1.2. Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 2.1 applies to them. Then \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = [x^2]_0^1 - \int_0^1 x\,dx = 1 - \int_0^1 x\,dx\). Hence \(2\int_0^1 x\,dx = 1\), and \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = 1/2\), which Corollary 1.2 confirms. The boundary term is 1, not 0.

  107 Substitution
  110 Theorem 3.1
  110 (change of variables)
  111 Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(a < b\) and \(g(a) \le g(b)\). Assume:
  113 - \(g\) is differentiable on an open interval \(I\) that contains \([a, b]\);
  114 - \(f\) has an antiderivative \(F\) on an open interval \(J\) with \(g(I) \subseteq J\), so \(F' = f\) on \(J\);
  115 - \(f\) is integrable on \([g(a), g(b)]\), and \(t \mapsto f(g(t))\,g'(t)\) is integrable on \([a, b]\).
  117 Then \(\displaystyle \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(x)\,dx = \int_a^b f(g(t))\,g'(t)\,dt\).
  121 Proof
  122 The first part of the argument does not need \(g(a) < g(b)\). The points \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\) lie in \(g(I) \subseteq J\), and \(J\) is an interval, so \([g(a), g(b)] \subseteq J\), and \(F\) is continuous there. Let \(H = F \circ g\). It is differentiable on \(I\), and by the chain rule \(H'(t) = F'(g(t))\,g'(t) = f(g(t))\,g'(t)\). Thus \(H\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), and by hypothesis 3 the function \(f(g(t))g'(t)\) is integrable there. Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 10 gives \[ \int_a^b f(g(t))\,g'(t)\,dt = H(b) - H(a) = F(g(b)) - F(g(a)). \] If \(g(a) = g(b)\), the left side is 0 and so is \(F(g(b)) - F(g(a))\). If \(g(a) < g(b)\), Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 10 applied to \(F\) on \([g(a), g(b)]\), with hypothesis 3 for integrability, gives \(\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f = F(g(b)) - F(g(a))\). In both cases the two sides agree, which proves the claim.
  128 Example 3.2
  128 (\(x^2\) with \(g(t) = t^3 + t\))
  129 Let \(f(x) = x^2\), \(F(x) = x^3/3\), \(g(t) = t^3 + t\), \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\). Here \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g(1) = 2\), \(g\) is differentiable on \(I = \R\), and \(J = \R\) works for \(F\). The function \(f(g(t))g'(t) = (t^3 + t)^2(3t^2 + 1) = 3t^8 + 7t^6 + 5t^4 + t^2\) is a polynomial, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\), and \(f = x^2\) is a polynomial, hence integrable on \([0, 2] = [g(0), g(1)]\) (Corollary 1.2). Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 3.1 says the two sides are equal, and both equal \(8/3\): \(\int_0^2 x^2\,dx = 8/3\) and \(\int_0^1 (3t^8 + 7t^6 + 5t^4 + t^2)\,dt = 1/3 + 1 + 1 + 1/3 = 8/3\).
  133 Example 3.3
  133 (\(x\) with \(g(t) = 1 + t^2\))
  134 Let \(f(x) = x\), \(F(x) = x^2/2\), \(g(t) = 1 + t^2\), \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\). Then \(g(0) = 1\), \(g(1) = 2\), and \(f(g(t))g'(t) = 2t(1 + t^2) = 2t + 2t^3\). The hypotheses hold: \(g\) is differentiable on \(\R\), \(F = x^2/2\) is an antiderivative of \(f = x\) on \(J = \R\), \(f\) is integrable on \([1, 2]\) (\(f = x\) is nondecreasing there, so Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2 applies), and \(f(g(t))g'(t) = 2t + 2t^3\) is a polynomial, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\). Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 3.1 says the two sides are equal, and both are \(3/2\): \(\int_1^2 x\,dx = (4 - 1)/2\), and \(\int_0^1 (2t + 2t^3)\,dt = 1 + 1/2\).

  222 Where the canon breaks
  226 - The change of variables moves the limits. Under \(x = g(t)\), the limits of integration become \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\). Example 3.2 has limits 0 and 2 on the \(x\)-side and 0 and 1 on the \(t\)-side. Lab 1, part C shows that leaving the limits at \([0, 1]\) gives \(1/3\), not \(8/3\).
  227 - The boundary term is not always zero. Example 2.3 has a boundary term of 1. In Example 2.2 the boundary term vanishes because \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\).
  228 - The integrability hypotheses are checked, not assumed. Theorem 3.1 needs \(f(g(t))g'(t)\) to be integrable, which is a separate condition from the integrability of \(f\). Examples 3.2 and 3.3 check it: their composite integrands, \(3t^8 + 7t^6 + 5t^4 + t^2\) and \(2t + 2t^3\), are polynomials (Corollary 1.2), and in Example 3.3 the function \(f = x\) is monotone (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2). The lesson does not establish hypothesis 3 for a general composite.
  233 5
  233 Misconceptions
  235 -
  236 “In integration by parts the boundary term always vanishes.”
  237 In Example 2.3, \([uv]_0^1 = [x^2]_0^1 = 1\), and the boundary term is what makes the integral come out to \(1/2\). Dropping it would give \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = -\int_0^1 x\,dx\), which is false. The term vanishes in Example 2.2 because \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\).
  239 -
  240 “Substitution does not change the limits of integration.”
  241 Theorem 3.1 has the limits \(g(a)\) and \(g(b)\) on the left side and \(a\) and \(b\) on the right side. In Example 3.2, \(\int_0^2 x^2\,dx = 8/3\) and \(\int_0^1 x^2\,dx = 1/3\), and the substitution identity pairs \(8/3\) with the limits \(0\) and \(2\). Lab 1, part C prints \(1/3\) for the wrong limits.
  243 -
  244 “Integration by parts applies to any two differentiable functions.”
  245 Theorem 2.1 requires \(u'v\) and \(uv'\) to be integrable on \([a, b]\). Theorem 2.1 lists the integrability of \(u'v\) and \(uv'\) as a separate hypothesis, and this lesson does not derive it from the differentiability of \(u\) and \(v\). The worked examples check it, using polynomials on \([0, 1]\) (Corollary 1.2).

  257 M2-L11 · Integration by parts with \(u = x\)
  258 Evaluate \(\int_0^1 x(1-x)^2\,dx\) by Theorem 2.1, with \(u = x\) and \(v' = (1-x)^2\).
  259 Solution
  260 Take \(v(x) = -(1-x)^3/3\), which has derivative \((1-x)^2\). Theorem 2.1 applies under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6. Its hypotheses hold: \(u = x\) and \(v\) are polynomials, so both are differentiable on \(\R\), and \(u'v = v\) and \(uv' = x(1-x)^2\) are polynomials, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\) (Corollary 1.2). The boundary term is \([uv]_0^1 = 1 \cdot 0 - 0 = 0\), since \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\). Then \(\int_0^1 x(1-x)^2\,dx = -\int_0^1 1 \cdot v\,dx = \frac13\int_0^1 (1-x)^3\,dx = \frac13 \cdot \frac14 = \frac1{12}\). Direct expansion, \(1/2 - 2/3 + 1/4\), gives the same value.
  264 M2-L11 · Substitution with \(g(t) = 1 + t^2\)
  265 Evaluate \(\int_0^1 2t(1+t^2)^2\,dt\) by Theorem 3.1 with \(f(x) = x^2\) and \(g(t) = 1 + t^2\).
  266 Solution
  267 Here \(g(0) = 1\) and \(g(1) = 2\), and \(g'(t) = 2t\), so the integrand is \(f(g(t))g'(t)\). Theorem 3.1 applies under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, and its hypotheses hold. Here \(g(0) = 1 \le 2 = g(1)\); \(g\) is differentiable on \(I = \R\); \(F(x) = x^3/3\) is an antiderivative of \(f = x^2\) on \(J = \R\), with \(g(I) \subseteq J\); \(f = x^2\) is nondecreasing on \([1, 2]\), hence integrable there (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2); and \(f(g(t))g'(t) = 2t(1+t^2)^2\) is a polynomial, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\) (Corollary 1.2). So Theorem 3.1 gives \(\int_0^1 2t(1+t^2)^2\,dt = \int_1^2 x^2\,dx = (8 - 1)/3 = 7/3\). Expanding directly, \(2t + 4t^3 + 2t^5\) integrates to \(1 + 1 + 1/3 = 7/3\).
  271 M2-L11 · Integration by parts with a cubic factor
  272 Evaluate \(\int_0^1 x^3(1-x)\,dx\) by Theorem 2.1.
  273 Solution
  274 Let \(u = x^3\) and \(v' = 1 - x\), so \(v = -(1-x)^2/2\). Theorem 2.1 applies under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6. Its hypotheses hold: \(u = x^3\) and \(v\) are polynomials, so both are differentiable on \(\R\), and \(u'v\) and \(uv'\) are polynomials, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\) (Corollary 1.2). The boundary term is 0, since \(v(1) = 0\) and \(u(0) = 0\). Then \(\int_0^1 x^3(1-x)\,dx = \frac{3}{2}\int_0^1 x^2(1-x)^2\,dx\). Expanding \(x^2(1-x)^2 = x^2 - 2x^3 + x^4\) gives \(1/3 - 1/2 + 1/5 = 1/30\), so the integral is \(3/60 = 1/20\). Direct expansion \(1/4 - 1/5 = 1/20\) agrees.

  282 M2-L11 · Negation of an integral
  283 Prove from the definitions that if \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then \(-f\) is integrable and \(\int_a^b (-f) = -\int_a^b f\).
  284 Solution
  285 For any partition \(P\), the supremum of \(-f\) on a subinterval is the negative of the infimum of \(f\), so \(U(-f,P) = -L(f,P)\) and \(L(-f,P) = -U(f,P)\). Hence \(U(-f,P) - L(-f,P) = U(f,P) - L(f,P)\), which shows that \(-f\) is integrable. Taking the supremum of \(L(-f,P) = -U(f,P)\) over \(P\) gives \(-\inf_P U(f,P) = -\int_a^b f\), so \(\int_a^b(-f) = -\int_a^b f\).
  289 M2-L11 · Substitution for a linear map
  290 Let \(c > 0\) and \(d\) be constants. Assume that \(f\) has an antiderivative on \(\R\), that \(f\) is integrable on \([ca + d, cb + d]\), and that \(t \mapsto f(ct + d)\) is integrable on \([a, b]\). Prove \(\int_{ca+d}^{cb+d} f(x)\,dx = c\int_a^b f(ct+d)\,dt\), using Theorem 3.1 under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6.
  291 Solution
  292 Let \(g(t) = ct + d\), which is differentiable on \(\R\), with \(g' = c\), and \(g(a) = ca + d \le cb + d = g(b)\) because \(c > 0\). Take \(I = J = \R\) and let \(F\) be the assumed antiderivative of \(f\). Hypothesis 3 also needs \(f\) integrable on \([g(a), g(b)] = [ca+d, cb+d]\), which is assumed. Then \(f(g(t))g'(t) = c\,f(ct+d)\), which is integrable on \([a, b]\) by Lemma 1.1 and the assumption on \(f(ct+d)\). Theorem 3.1 gives \(\int_{ca+d}^{cb+d} f = \int_a^b c\,f(ct+d)\,dt\), and Lemma 1.1 moves the constant \(c\) outside.
  296 M2-L11 · The sup of a sum
  297 Show that for bounded \(f\) and \(g\) on a subinterval \(S\), \(\sup_S (f+g) \le \sup_S f + \sup_S g\), and that \(\inf_S (f+g) \ge \inf_S f + \inf_S g\).
  298 Solution
  299 For every \(x \in S\), \(f(x) + g(x) \le \sup_S f + \sup_S g\), so the supremum of \(f + g\) on \(S\) is at most the right side. Similarly, \(f(x) + g(x) \ge \inf_S f + \inf_S g\) for every \(x\), so the infimum is at least the right side. These are the two inequalities used in the proof of Lemma 1.1.

  307 M2-L11 · The number of midpoint parts for \(x^2\) on \([0, 2]\)
  308 For the midpoint rule with \(N\) parts applied to \(x^2\) on \([0, 2]\), the error equals \(2/(3N^2)\) exactly. Find the smallest \(N\) for which the error is at most \(10^{-6}\).
  309 Solution
  310 On a subinterval of width \(h\) with midpoint \(m\), the midpoint rule error of \(x^2\) is \(\int_{m-h/2}^{m+h/2} x^2\,dx - h m^2 = \int_{-h/2}^{h/2} t^2\,dt = h^3/12\), because the linear term in \(t\) integrates to zero. With \(N\) parts, \(h = 2/N\), so the total error is \(N \cdot (2/N)^3/12 = 2/(3N^2)\). The condition \(2/(3N^2) \le 10^{-6}\) means \(N^2 \ge 666\,667\), so \(N \ge 817\). Checking, \(N = 816\) gives \(1.0012 \times 10^{-6}\), which fails, and \(N = 817\) gives \(9.988 \times 10^{-7}\), which passes.

  332 M2-L11 · The Beta-type integral in general
  333 For integers \(m, n \ge 0\), prove by induction on \(n\) that \(\int_0^1 x^m (1-x)^n\,dx = m!\,n!/(m+n+1)!\), for every \(m\).
  334 Solution
  335 Write \(B(m,n)\) for the integral. For \(n = 0\), \(B(m,0) = 1/(m+1) = m!\,0!/(m+1)!\). Assume the formula for \(n\) and all \(m\). Integrate by parts, under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, with \(u = (1-x)^{n+1}\) and \(v' = x^m\), so \(v = x^{m+1}/(m+1)\). The hypotheses hold: \(u\) and \(v\) are polynomials, so both are differentiable on \(\R\), and \(u'v = -(n+1)(1-x)^n x^{m+1}/(m+1)\) and \(uv' = (1-x)^{n+1}x^m\) are polynomials, hence integrable on \([0, 1]\) (Corollary 1.2). The boundary term vanishes, since \(u(1) = 0\) and \(v(0) = 0\). Then \(B(m, n+1) = \frac{n+1}{m+1}B(m+1, n)\). By the hypothesis, \(B(m+1,n) = (m+1)!\,n!/(m+n+2)!\), so \(B(m,n+1) = (n+1)!\,m!/(m+n+2)!\), which is the formula for \(n+1\).
  339 M2-L11 · Substitution that scales the variable
  340 Use Theorem 3.1 with \(g(t) = bt\), \(b > 0\), and \(f(x) = x^k\) on \([0, b]\), to prove \(\int_0^b x^k\,dx = b^{k+1}/(k+1)\), checking each hypothesis.
  341 Solution
  342 Take \(a = 0\), \(g(0) = 0\), \(g(1) = b\), with \(I = J = \R\) and \(F(x) = x^{k+1}/(k+1)\), so that \(F' = f\) on \(J\). Hypothesis 1 holds since \(g\) is linear, hypothesis 2 holds since \(g(I) \subseteq \R = J\), and \(f = x^k\) is integrable on \([0, b]\) by Corollary 1.2. The integrand \(f(g(t))g'(t) = b^k t^k \cdot b = b^{k+1}t^k\) is a polynomial, hence integrable on \([0,1]\). Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 3.1 gives \(\int_0^b x^k\,dx = \int_0^1 b^{k+1}t^k\,dt = b^{k+1}/(k+1)\), which agrees with Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 10.
  346 M2-L11 · Checking Theorem 3.1 for a squaring map
  347 Check Theorem 3.1 for \(g(t) = t^2\) on \([0,1]\) and \(f(x) = x\), and state which hypothesis carries the integrability of the composite.
  348 Solution
  349 Here \(g(0) = 0\), \(g(1) = 1\), and \(g\) is differentiable on \(I = \R\). Take \(J = \R\) and \(F(x) = x^2/2\). Then \(f(g(t))g'(t) = t^2 \cdot 2t = 2t^3\), a polynomial, hence integrable, and hypothesis 3 is satisfied. Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 3.1 says the two sides are equal. The left side is \(\int_0^1 x\,dx = 1/2\), and the right side is \(\int_0^1 2t^3\,dt = 2/4 = 1/2\). Hypothesis 3 has two integrability conditions: \(f\) on \([g(0), g(1)] = [0, 1]\), which holds because \(f = x\) is monotone, and the composite \(f(g(t))g'(t) = 2t^3\) on \([0, 1]\). The second is the one that carries the integrability of the composite.
