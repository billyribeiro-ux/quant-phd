   51 A derivative of a product, a quotient or a composition of functions can be reduced to the derivatives of its parts. With a constant rate \(r\), the discount factor for \(t\) periods is \(1/(1 + r)^t\), and a price can be a function of a yield through several steps. Computing each derivative from the limit in Lesson 4 repeats the same argument. The three rules in this lesson reduce each such derivative to the derivatives of its parts, and each rule is proved from the definition of the derivative, with its hypotheses stated.

   53 Limit laws as the step goes to zero, and the product and quotient rules
   56 Lemma 1.1
   56 (limit laws as the step goes to zero)
   57 Let \(F\) and \(G\) be real functions defined for \(0 < |h| < r\), for some \(r > 0\). Suppose \(F(h) \to L\) and \(G(h) \to M\) as \(h \to 0\), in the sense of Lesson 3, Definition 1.1 with \(0\) in place of \(a\). Then: (a) \(F + G \to L + M\); (b) \(FG \to LM\); (c) if \(M \ne 0\), then \(G(h) \ne 0\) for small \(|h|\), and \(F/G \to L/M\). Hypotheses: the two limits exist and are real, and in (c) the limit \(M\) is nonzero.
   61 Proof (the argument of Lesson 2, Proposition 1.1, with the substitutions stated)
   62 The substitutions are: the index \(n\) is replaced by the real variable \(h\), the condition \(n \ge N\) is replaced by \(0 < |h| < \delta\), and the sequence limits are replaced by the limits as \(h \to 0\). Each step of the proof of Lesson 2, Proposition 1.1 then holds for every \(h\) with \(0 < |h| < \delta\) for a suitable \(\delta\). Part (a) is the triangle inequality with two thresholds. Part (b) uses the bound \(|F(h)| < |L| + 1\) for small \(|h|\), together with the estimate \(|FG - LM| \le |F|\,|G - M| + |M|\,|F - L|\). For part (c), choose \(r' > 0\) with \(|G(h) - M| < |M|/2\) for \(0 < |h| < r'\). Then \(|G(h)| > |M|/2 > 0\), so \(G(h) \ne 0\) there. Now \[\frac{F}{G} - \frac{L}{M} = \frac{M(F - L) - L(G - M)}{M\,G}, \qquad \left|\frac{F}{G} - \frac{L}{M}\right| \le \frac{2}{|M|^2}\bigl(|M|\,|F - L| + |L|\,|G - M|\bigr).\] Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Choose \(\delta > 0\), no larger than \(r'\), with \(|F(h) - L| < \varepsilon|M|/4\) and \(|G(h) - M| < \varepsilon|M|^2/(4(|L| + 1))\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta\), which is possible because both limits exist. Then the bracket is less than \(\varepsilon|M|^2/4 + \varepsilon|M|^2/4 = \varepsilon|M|^2/2\), and the whole expression is less than \(\varepsilon\). \(\blacksquare\)
   68 Proposition 1.2
   68 (product rule)
   69 Let \(f\) and \(g\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), and let \(a \in I\). Hypotheses: \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(a\) in the sense of Lesson 4, Definition 1.1. Then \(fg\) is differentiable at \(a\), and \((fg)'(a) = f'(a)\,g(a) + f(a)\,g'(a)\).
   73 Proof
   74 For \(h \ne 0\) with \(a + h \in I\), write \(Q_f(h)\) and \(Q_g(h)\) for the difference quotients of \(f\) and \(g\) at \(a\). Then
   75 \[\frac{f(a+h)g(a+h) - f(a)g(a)}{h} = f(a+h)\,\frac{g(a+h) - g(a)}{h} + g(a)\,\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h} = f(a+h)\,Q_g(h) + g(a)\,Q_f(h),\]
   76 where the middle term \(f(a+h)g(a)\) is added and subtracted. As \(h \to 0\), \(f(a+h) \to f(a)\), by the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) form established in the proof of Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1. Also \(Q_g(h) \to g'(a)\) and \(Q_f(h) \to f'(a)\). Lemma 1.1 (a) and (b) give the limit \(f(a)g'(a) + g(a)f'(a)\). \(\blacksquare\)
   80 Proposition 1.3
   80 (quotient rule)
   81 Let \(f\) and \(g\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), let \(a \in I\), and suppose \(g(a) \ne 0\). Hypotheses: \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(a\), and \(g(a) \ne 0\). Then \(f/g\) is defined for \(x\) near \(a\), it is differentiable at \(a\), and \((f/g)'(a) = \dfrac{f'(a)\,g(a) - f(a)\,g'(a)}{g(a)^2}\).
   85 Proof
   86 Since \(g\) is differentiable at \(a\), it is continuous there (Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1). So there is \(r > 0\) with \(|g(x) - g(a)| < |g(a)|/2\) for \(|x - a| < r\), and then \(|g(x)| > |g(a)|/2 > 0\). Thus \(f/g\) is defined on that neighbourhood. For \(0 < |h| < r\), with \(a + h \in I\),
   87 \[\frac{1}{h}\left(\frac{f(a+h)}{g(a+h)} - \frac{f(a)}{g(a)}\right) = \frac{g(a)\,Q_f(h) - f(a)\,Q_g(h)}{g(a)\,g(a+h)}.\]
   88 The numerator tends to \(g(a)f'(a) - f(a)g'(a)\) and the denominator to \(g(a)^2 \ne 0\), by Lemma 1.1 (a), (b) and (c), using \(g(a+h) \to g(a)\). So the difference quotient of \(f/g\) at \(a\) has the stated limit. \(\blacksquare\)
   91 For a function \(f\), a point \(x\) and a step \(h \ne 0\), the central difference is \((f(x + h) - f(x - h))/(2h)\). It uses values on both sides of \(x\).
   93 Assumption 1.4
   93 (central-difference expansion, assumed here and not proved)
   94 For a function \(f\) with a third derivative near \(x\), the central difference satisfies \((f(x + h) - f(x - h))/(2h) = f'(x) + h^2 f'''(x)/6 + o(h^2)\) as \(h \to 0\). This page states it as an assumption and does not prove it. Every statement below that uses it begins with "Assuming Assumption 1.4".

  175 The chain rule
  178 Proposition 2.1
  178 (chain rule)
  179 Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\) and differentiable at \(a \in I\). Let \(g\) be defined on an open interval \(J\) and differentiable at \(b = f(a)\), with \(f(I) \subset J\). Hypotheses: both derivatives exist in the sense of Lesson 4, Definition 1.1, and every value \(f(x)\) with \(x \in I\) lies in \(J\). Then \(g \circ f\) is differentiable at \(a\), and \((g \circ f)'(a) = g'(f(a))\,f'(a)\).
  183 Proof
  184 Define \(G\) on the set \(J - b = \{k : b + k \in J\}\) by \(G(k) = (g(b + k) - g(b))/k\) for \(k \ne 0\), and \(G(0) = g'(b)\). By the definition of \(g'(b)\) in Lesson 4, \(G(k) \to G(0)\) as \(k \to 0\), so \(G\) is continuous at 0. Let \(k(h) = f(a + h) - f(a)\). For \(h \ne 0\) with \(a + h \in I\),
  185 \[g(f(a+h)) - g(f(a)) = G\bigl(k(h)\bigr)\,k(h).\]
  186 If \(k(h) \ne 0\), this is the definition of \(G\) at \(k(h)\). If \(k(h) = 0\), both sides are 0. Dividing by \(h\), the difference quotient of \(g \circ f\) at \(a\) is \(G(k(h))\,Q_f(h)\). Now \(k(h) \to 0\) as \(h \to 0\), by the \(\varepsilon\)-\(\delta\) form in the proof of Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1. Let \(\varepsilon > 0\), choose \(\eta > 0\) with \(|G(k) - G(0)| < \varepsilon\) for \(|k| < \eta\), and then \(\delta > 0\) with \(|k(h)| < \eta\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta\). Then \(G(k(h)) \to G(0) = g'(b)\) as \(h \to 0\). Since \(Q_f(h) \to f'(a)\), Lemma 1.1 (b) gives \((g \circ f)'(a) = g'(b)\,f'(a)\). \(\blacksquare\)
  190 Example 2.2
  190 (a power of a sum of squares)
  191 Let \(c(x) = (x^2 + 1)^3\), the composite of \(f(x) = x^2 + 1\) with \(g(u) = u^3\). Here \(f'(x) = 2x\) by Lesson 4, Example 1.2 and the sum rule of Lesson 4, Problem P2, since the constant 1 has derivative 0 (Lesson 4, Problem R3). Also \(g'(u) = 3u^2\) by the power rule of Problem P1 in this lesson. The chain rule gives \(c'(x) = 3(x^2+1)^2 \cdot 2x = 6x(x^2+1)^2\). At \(x = 1.5\) this is \(6 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 3.25^2 = 95.0625\), which is the value in Lab 1.
  194 Example 2.2 uses \(g'(u) = 3u^2\) for the cube, which Problem P1 proves from the product rule.
  197 3
  197 Where the canon breaks
  198 Each rule holds under its hypotheses. The errors below come from omitting a hypothesis or evaluating a derivative at the wrong point.
  200 - The product of derivatives is not the derivative of the product. Proposition 1.2 has two cross terms. Misconception 1 gives the case that shows the omission.
  201 - The quotient rule needs a nonzero denominator at the point. Proposition 1.3 uses \(g(a) \ne 0\) and continuity of \(g\) at \(a\). Where \(g(a) = 0\) the quotient need not be defined at \(a\), and the rule does not apply there.
  202 - The chain rule evaluates the outer derivative at the inner value. Proposition 2.1 uses \(g'(f(a))\), not \(g'(a)\). Misconception 2 shows the error, and the condition \(f(I) \subset J\) makes the composition defined.
  207 4
  207 Misconceptions
  209 -
  210 “The derivative of a product is the product of the derivatives.”
  211 Take \(f(x) = x\) and \(g(x) = x\). Then \(fg(x) = x^2\), with derivative \(2x\), while \(f'g' = 1 \cdot 1 = 1\). The product rule of Proposition 1.2 gives \(x \cdot 1 + x \cdot 1 = 2x\), which is correct.
  213 -
  214 “The chain rule differentiates the outer function at the point \(a\).”
  215 Take \(f(x) = x + 1\) and \(g(u) = u^2\), at \(a = 0\). Then \(g \circ f(x) = (x+1)^2\), whose derivative at 0 is \(2 \cdot 1 = 2\). The wrong formula \(g'(a) f'(a)\) gives \(g'(0) \cdot 1 = 0\). The correct value uses \(g'(f(0)) = g'(1) = 2\).
  217 -
  218 “A central difference gives the derivative exactly for a cubic.”
  219 For \(x^3\), the central difference at \(x = 1\) is \(((1+h)^3 - (1-h)^3)/(2h) = 3 + h^2\), which equals 3 only when \(h = 0\). The error is exactly \(h^2\), which at \(h = 10^{-4}\) is \(10^{-8}\). Lab 1 prints central-difference errors for the other functions \(p\), \(q\) and \(c\): 2.35e-07, 3.33e-10 and 8.55e-07. None of these is \(h^2\). Assuming Assumption 1.4, the leading term of the central-difference error is \(h^2 f'''(x)/6\). Assuming Assumption 1.4, \(p'''(1.5) = 141\) gives 2.35e-07, which matches the printed error for \(p\), as in E1; Assumption 1.4 is not proved on this page.

  229 Mechanics
  231 M1 · A product of a linear factor and a line
  232 Use Proposition 1.2 to find the derivative of \(x(x + 1)\) at every point, and check it by expanding.
  233 Solution
  234 With \(f(x) = x\) and \(g(x) = x + 1\), \(f' = 1\) and \(g' = 1\). The rule gives \((fg)'(x) = 1 \cdot (x+1) + x \cdot 1 = 2x + 1\). Expanding, \(x^2 + x\) has derivative \(2x + 1\), which agrees.
  238 M2 · A quotient at a point where the derivative vanishes
  239 Let \(q(x) = x/(x^2 + 1)\). Find \(q'(x)\) and \(q'(1)\).
  240 Solution
  241 With \(f = x\), \(g = x^2 + 1\), \(f' = 1\), and \(g' = 2x\), Proposition 1.3 gives \(q'(x) = ((x^2+1) - x \cdot 2x)/(x^2+1)^2 = (1 - x^2)/(x^2+1)^2\). At \(x = 1\) the numerator is 0, so \(q'(1) = 0\). The denominator is never zero.
  245 M3 · A power of a linear function
  246 Let \(w(x) = (3x - 1)^4\). Find \(w'(1)\) using Proposition 2.1.
  247 Solution
  248 The outer function is \(u^4\), with derivative \(4u^3\), and the inner function is \(3x - 1\), with derivative 3. The chain rule gives \(w'(x) = 4(3x - 1)^3 \cdot 3 = 12(3x-1)^3\). At \(x = 1\), \(3x - 1 = 2\), so \(w'(1) = 12 \cdot 8 = 96\).
  254 Proof
  256 P1 · The power rule for positive integers
  257 Let \(n \ge 1\) be an integer and \(p_n(x) = x^n\). Prove by mathematical induction, using Proposition 1.2, that \(p_n'(x) = n x^{n-1}\) for every real \(x\).
  258 Solution
  259 For \(n = 1\), \(p_1(x) = x\) has derivative 1 by the difference quotient \((a + h - a)/h = 1\), which is \(1 \cdot x^0\). Assume \(p_n'(x) = n x^{n-1}\) for some \(n \ge 1\). Write \(p_{n+1} = x^n \cdot x\). Proposition 1.2 gives \(p_{n+1}'(x) = n x^{n-1} \cdot x + x^n \cdot 1 = (n+1)x^n\), which is the claim for \(n + 1\). The induction is complete.
  263 P2 · The reciprocal rule
  264 Let \(g\) be differentiable at \(a\) with \(g(a) \ne 0\). Prove that \(1/g\) is differentiable at \(a\) with \((1/g)'(a) = -g'(a)/g(a)^2\).
  265 Solution
  266 Apply Proposition 1.3 with \(f = 1\), a constant function. Its derivative is 0 by the constant case of Lesson 4, Problem R3. The rule gives \((1/g)'(a) = (0 \cdot g(a) - 1 \cdot g'(a))/g(a)^2 = -g'(a)/g(a)^2\).
  270 P3 · The square of a differentiable function
  271 Let \(f\) be differentiable at \(a\). Use Proposition 2.1 with \(g(u) = u^2\) to show that \(f^2\) is differentiable at \(a\) with \((f^2)'(a) = 2 f(a) f'(a)\).
  272 Solution
  273 Take \(J = \R\), so \(f(I) \subset J\) holds for any \(I\). The function \(g(u) = u^2\) has \(g'(u) = 2u\) by Lesson 4, Example 1.2. Proposition 2.1 gives \((g \circ f)'(a) = g'(f(a)) f'(a) = 2 f(a) f'(a)\). The composition \(g \circ f\) is \(f^2\), which is the claim.

  282 Lab 1 prints the absolute difference for the product as \(2.35 \times 10^{-7}\). Assuming Assumption 1.4, compare the printed size with the leading term \(h^2 p'''(x)/6\) of the central-difference error. Assumption 1.4 is not proved on this page, so the comparison is numerical.
  283 Solution

  288 E2 · The quotient value (from Lab 1)
  289 Lab 1 prints the analytic quotient derivative at 1.5 as 0.591836735. Check it as an exact fraction.
  290 Solution
  291 The quotient rule gives \((x^2 + 4x - 1)/(x+2)^2\), which at \(x = 1.5\) is \((2.25 + 6 - 1)/12.25 = 7.25/12.25 = 29/49\). Its decimal value is \(0.5918367346\ldots\), and the lab prints this rounded to nine decimal places, which is 0.591836735.
  295 E3 · The composite value (from Lab 1)
  296 Lab 1 prints the analytic chain-rule derivative at 1.5 as 95.062500000. Check it from \(6x(x^2+1)^2\).
  297 Solution
  298 At \(x = 1.5\), \(x^2 + 1 = 3.25\), so \(3.25^2 = 10.5625\), and \(6 \cdot 1.5 \cdot 10.5625 = 9 \cdot 10.5625 = 95.0625\). The printed value agrees with this exact value.
  304 Research
  306 R1 · The central difference of a cubic
  307 Let \(p(x) = ax^3 + bx^2 + mx + d\). Show that for every \(x\) and \(h \ne 0\), the central difference \((p(x+h) - p(x-h))/(2h)\) equals \(p'(x) + a h^2\).
  308 Solution
  309 For the linear term, the central difference is \(m\). For the quadratic term, \(((x+h)^2 - (x-h)^2)/(2h) = 4xh/(2h) = 2x\), so it contributes \(2bx\), which is the derivative of \(bx^2\). For the cubic term, \(((x+h)^3 - (x-h)^3)/(2h) = (6x^2 h + 2h^3)/(2h) = 3x^2 + h^2\), so it contributes \(a(3x^2 + h^2)\). Adding the parts gives \(3ax^2 + 2bx + m + a h^2 = p'(x) + a h^2\).
  313 R2 · Why the composition must be defined near the point
  314 Let \(f(x) = x\) on \(I = (-1, 1)\), and let \(g(u) = 1/u\) on \(J = (0, \infty)\). Show that \(g \circ f\) is not defined on \(I\), and explain what Proposition 2.1 then says.
  315 Solution
  316 The composition needs \(g(f(x))\) for every \(x\) in \(I\). For \(x \le 0\) in \(I\), \(f(x) = x \le 0\) is not in \(J\), so \(g(f(x))\) is not defined. The hypothesis \(f(I) \subset J\) fails, and Proposition 2.1 does not apply. Restricting \(I\) to \((0, 1)\) makes the hypothesis hold, and then the chain rule applies at each point of that interval.
  320 R3 · The quotient from the product and the reciprocal
  321 Using Proposition 1.2 and Problem P2, derive the quotient rule of Proposition 1.3 for \(f/g\) when \(g(a) \ne 0\).
  322 Solution
  323 Write \(f/g = f \cdot (1/g)\). By Proposition 1.2, \((f/g)'(a) = f'(a)(1/g)(a) + f(a)(1/g)'(a)\). Substituting \((1/g)(a) = 1/g(a)\) and Problem P2's \((1/g)'(a) = -g'(a)/g(a)^2\), the sum is \(f'(a)/g(a) - f(a)g'(a)/g(a)^2 = (f'(a)g(a) - f(a)g'(a))/g(a)^2\), which is the quotient rule.
