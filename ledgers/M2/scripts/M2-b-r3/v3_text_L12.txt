   52 Some quantities accumulate over an unbounded horizon, such as a continuous payoff that runs forever, and such a quantity is an integral over an unbounded interval. Such an integral is defined through a limit of ordinary integrals, and it may not have a finite value. This lesson defines the improper integral, evaluates the power integrals exactly, and proves that the integral of \(1/x\) diverges even though \(1/x\) tends to zero. It closes with a research problem that bounds the partial sums of the series \(\sum_k k^{-p}\) by an integral, for integers \(p \ge 2\). Each result is proved here from Lessons 1 to 10 and Assumptions 2.1 (Lesson 6) and 2.2 (stated where used); Lab 1 prints the truncation errors.
   54 1
   54 Definition
   57 Definition 1.1
   57 (improper integral over \([a, \infty)\))
   58 Let \(f\) be integrable on \([a, b]\) for every \(b \ge a\). The improper integral of \(f\) over \([a, \infty)\) is \[ \int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx, \] when the limit exists and is a finite number. The integral is then convergent. Otherwise it is divergent. The integrals \(\int_a^b f\) are the truncated integrals, and the limit is taken in the sense that for every \(\varepsilon > 0\) there is \(B\) with \(|\int_a^b f - \ell| \le \varepsilon\) for all \(b \ge B\).
   64 Remark 1.2
   64 (the limit along a sequence)
   65 If the limit in Definition 1.1 exists and equals \(\ell\), then \(\int_a^{b_m} f \to \ell\) for every sequence \(b_m \to \infty\). This follows from the definition, because the sequence eventually lies beyond any \(B\). Theorem 3.3 uses this direction: a sequence \(b_m \to \infty\) along which the truncated integrals are unbounded rules out a finite limit.
   68 2
   68 The power integrals
   71 Lemma 2.1
   71 (inverse powers tend to zero)
   72 For every integer \(m \ge 1\), \(b^{-m} \to 0\) as \(b \to \infty\).
   76 Proof
   77 For \(b \ge 1\), \(b^m \ge b\), so \(0 < b^{-m} \le b^{-1}\). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\), and put \(B = \max(1, 1/\varepsilon)\). For \(b \ge B\), \(0 < b^{-1} \le \varepsilon\), so \(0 < b^{-m} \le b^{-1} \le \varepsilon\). Hence \(b^{-m} \to 0\) as \(b \to \infty\).
   81 Theorem 2.2
   81 (power integrals)
   82 Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(p \ge 2\) be an integer and \(b > 1\) (for \(b = 1\) both sides are 0 by the convention of Lesson 9, Definition 1.3). Then \[ \int_1^b x^{-p}\,dx = \frac{1 - b^{1-p}}{p-1}. \] Hence \(\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx\) converges to \(1/(p-1)\), and the truncation error at \(b\) is exactly \(b^{1-p}/(p-1)\).
   88 Proof
   89 Let \(w(x) = (p-1)x^{p-1}\) and \(F = -1/w\) on \((0, \infty)\). The power rule gives \(w'(x) = (p-1)^2 x^{p-2}\), and the quotient rule for \(-1/w\) gives \(F' = w'/w^2 = (p-1)^2 x^{p-2}/\bigl((p-1)^2 x^{2p-2}\bigr) = x^{-p}\). On \([1, b]\), \(f(x) = x^{-p}\) is positive and nonincreasing, so it is integrable by Theorem 2.2 of Lesson 9, and \(F\) is continuous there. Theorem 2.1 of Lesson 10 gives \[ \int_1^b x^{-p}\,dx = F(b) - F(1) = -\frac{1}{(p-1)b^{p-1}} + \frac{1}{p-1} = \frac{1 - b^{1-p}}{p-1}. \] Since \(p - 1 \ge 1\), Lemma 2.1 gives \(b^{1-p} \to 0\), so the limit is \(1/(p-1)\). The error is \(1/(p-1) - (1 - b^{1-p})/(p-1) = b^{1-p}/(p-1)\).
   95 Example 2.3
   95 (\(p = 2\) and \(p = 3\))
   96 By Theorem 2.2 (under Assumption 2.1), for \(p = 2\), \(\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = 1\), and the truncation error at \(b\) is \(1/b\). For \(p = 3\), \(\int_1^\infty x^{-3}\,dx = 1/2\), and the error is \(1/(2b^2)\). Lab 1, parts A and B print these errors and their scaled values.

  200 3
  200 The integral of \(1/x\) diverges
  203 Lemma 3.1
  203 (harmonic blocks)
  204 Let \(H_N = \sum_{i=1}^{N} 1/i\). For every integer \(m \ge 0\), \(H_{2^m} \ge 1 + m/2\).
  208 Proof
  209 Induction on \(m\). For \(m = 0\), \(H_1 = 1 \ge 1\). Assume \(H_{2^m} \ge 1 + m/2\). Then \(H_{2^{m+1}} - H_{2^m} = \sum_{i = 2^m + 1}^{2^{m+1}} 1/i\). This sum has \(2^m\) terms, each at least \(1/2^{m+1}\), so it is at least \(2^m/2^{m+1} = 1/2\). Hence \(H_{2^{m+1}} \ge 1 + m/2 + 1/2 = 1 + (m+1)/2\).
  213 Lemma 3.2
  213 (a lower sum for \(1/x\))
  214 For every integer \(N \ge 2\), \(\int_1^N dx/x \ge H_N - 1\).
  218 Proof
  219 The function \(f(x) = 1/x\) is positive and nonincreasing on \([1, N]\), so it is integrable there. Take the partition of \([1, N]\) into the \(N - 1\) unit intervals \([k, k+1]\). On each, the infimum of \(f\) is at the right endpoint, \(f(k+1) = 1/(k+1)\). So the lower sum is \(L(f, P) = \sum_{k=1}^{N-1} 1/(k+1) = \sum_{j=2}^{N} 1/j = H_N - 1\). By Definition 1.3 of Lesson 9, \(L(f, P) \le \int_1^N f\).
  223 Theorem 3.3
  223 (divergence of \(\int_1^\infty dx/x\))
  224 The improper integral \(\int_1^\infty dx/x\) diverges. Its truncated integrals are at least \(m/2\) at \(b = 2^m\), so they are unbounded.
  228 Proof
  229 Let \(G(b) = \int_1^b dx/x\). By Lemma 3.2 and Lemma 3.1, \(G(2^m) \ge H_{2^m} - 1 \ge m/2\) for every \(m \ge 1\). Suppose the improper integral converged to a finite \(\ell\). Then \(G(b) \to \ell\) as \(b \to \infty\), and so \(G(2^m) \to \ell\) along the sequence \(b_m = 2^m\) (Remark 1.2). But \(G(2^m) \ge m/2\), and \(m/2 \to \infty\), so \(G(2^m)\) cannot converge. This contradiction shows that the improper integral diverges.
  233 Remark 3.4
  233 (the integrand tends to zero)
  234 The function \(1/x\) satisfies \(1/x \to 0\) as \(x \to \infty\), and Theorem 3.3 still shows divergence. The truncated integrals are unbounded because each block \(\{2^m + 1, \ldots, 2^{m+1}\}\), as used in the proof of Lemma 3.1, contributes at least \(1/2\) to \(H_N\), and Lemma 3.2 bounds the truncated integral below by \(H_N - 1\). Among integer exponents \(p \ge 1\), the integral converges for every integer \(p \ge 2\) (Theorem 2.2, under Assumption 2.1) and diverges at \(p = 1\) (Theorem 3.3); this lesson does not treat \(p \le 0\).
  238 4
  238 Where the canon breaks
  240 - A truncation is not the value. The truncated integral \(\int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx = 1 - 1/b\) is never equal to 1 at any finite \(b\). The error \(1/b\) falls by a factor of 10 each time \(b\) is multiplied by 10, and Lab 1, part A prints it for four values of \(b\).
  241 - Among integer exponents \(p \ge 1\), the exponent decides convergence. Theorem 2.2 and Theorem 3.3 together show that \(x^{-p}\) integrates to a finite value for integers \(p \ge 2\) and diverges at \(p = 1\). This lesson does not treat \(p \le 0\). Non-integer powers need real exponents, which are not established in this lesson, so no claim is made for them.
  242 - The definition needs integrability on every finite interval. The finite integrals used in this lesson are over an interval \([a,b]\) on which the integrand is monotone, which Lesson 9 covers (Theorem 2.2): \([1,b]\) in the theorems, and \([2,b]\), \([a,b]\) with \(a > 0\) and \([\varepsilon,1]\) in the problems. Problem R2 defines \(\int_0^1 f\) as a limit of integrals over \([\varepsilon,1]\) as \(\varepsilon \to 0^+\), an integral at a finite endpoint that Definition 1.1 does not cover; that limit is a separate definition given in Problem R2. An integrand that is not integrable on some \([a, b]\) is outside Definition 1.1, and this lesson makes no claim about it.
  247 5
  247 Misconceptions
  249 -
  250 “A large enough truncation gives the exact improper value.”
  251 The truncation error of \(\int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx\) is exactly \(1/b\), which is positive at every finite \(b\) (Theorem 2.2). At \(b = 10000\), the truncated value is \(0.9999\), not 1 (Lab 1, part A). A large \(b\) makes the error small, and the value is reached only in the limit.
  253 -
  254 “If \(f(x) \to 0\) as \(x \to \infty\), then \(\int_1^\infty f\) converges.”
  255 For \(f(x) = 1/x\), the integrand tends to 0, but the truncated integrals are at least \(m/2\) at \(b = 2^m\) (Theorem 3.3), so the improper integral diverges. Lab 1, part C prints \(H_{16384} - 1 = 9.28130671\), which is above the bound \(7\) at \(m = 14\).
  257 -
  258 “\(\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx\) converges for every \(p > 0\).”
  259 The case \(p = 1\) diverges (Theorem 3.3), and the case \(p \ge 2\) converges (Theorem 2.2, under Assumption 2.1), so the belief fails. This lesson proves nothing for \(0 < p < 1\).

  272 Evaluate \(\int_1^\infty x^{-4}\,dx\) by Theorem 2.2.
  273 Solution

  280 Solution

  285 M2-L12 · An integral starting at 2
  286 Evaluate \(\int_2^\infty x^{-3}\,dx\), using the antiderivative \(F(x) = -1/(2x^2)\).
  287 Solution
  288 The derivative of \(-1/(2x^2)\) is \(x^{-3}\), by the quotient rule and the power rule. By Lesson 10, Theorem 2.1 (under Assumption 2.1), with \(x^{-3}\) integrable on \([2, b]\) because it is positive and nonincreasing there (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2), the truncated integral from 2 to \(b\) is \(F(b) - F(2) = -1/(2b^2) + 1/8\). As \(b \to \infty\), \(1/(2b^2) \to 0\) by Lemma 2.1, so the improper integral is \(1/8\).

  296 M2-L12 · Inverse powers tend to zero by induction
  297 Prove Lemma 2.1 for every integer \(m \ge 1\) without the squeeze argument, by induction on \(m\).
  298 Solution
  299 For \(m = 1\), let \(\varepsilon > 0\), put \(B = \max(1, 1/\varepsilon)\), and let \(b \ge B\). Then \(b \ge 1\) gives \(0 < b^{-1}\), and \(b \ge 1/\varepsilon\) gives \(b^{-1} \le \varepsilon\), so \(b^{-1} \to 0\). Assume \(b^{-m} \to 0\), and let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Take \(B\) with \(b^{-m} \le \varepsilon\) for \(b \ge B\), and put \(B' = \max(B, 1)\). For \(b \ge B'\), \(0 < b^{-(m+1)} = b^{-m} \cdot b^{-1} \le b^{-m} \le \varepsilon\), because \(0 < b^{-1} \le 1\). So \(b^{-(m+1)} \to 0\).
  303 M2-L12 · The harmonic bound by induction
  304 Prove Lemma 3.1 by induction on \(m\), checking the step explicitly.
  305 Solution
  306 For \(m = 0\), \(H_1 = 1\). Assume \(H_{2^m} \ge 1 + m/2\). The increment \(H_{2^{m+1}} - H_{2^m}\) is the sum of the reciprocals of the integers from \(2^m + 1\) to \(2^{m+1}\), which are \(2^m\) numbers, each at least \(1/2^{m+1}\). So the increment is at least \(2^m/2^{m+1} = 1/2\), and \(H_{2^{m+1}} \ge 1 + m/2 + 1/2\).
  310 M2-L12 · Two sequences, one limit
  311 Suppose \(\int_1^b f\) tends to \(\ell\) as \(b \to \infty\). Prove that \(\int_1^{2^m} f \to \ell\) as \(m \to \infty\), and explain why this is used in Theorem 3.3.
  312 Solution
  313 Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), there is \(B\) with \(|\int_1^b f - \ell| \le \varepsilon\) for \(b \ge B\). Since \(2^m \to \infty\), there is \(m_0\) with \(2^m \ge B\) for \(m \ge m_0\), and then \(|\int_1^{2^m} f - \ell| \le \varepsilon\). So the subsequence converges to \(\ell\). Theorem 3.3 uses this with \(f = 1/x\): the subsequence values are at least \(m/2\), which cannot converge, so the full limit cannot exist.

  346 M2-L12 · The integral from \(a\) to infinity in general
  347 For integer \(p \ge 2\) and \(a > 0\), prove \(\int_a^\infty x^{-p}\,dx = a^{1-p}/(p-1)\).
  348 Solution
  349 Use the antiderivative \(F(x) = -1/((p-1)x^{p-1})\) from the proof of Theorem 2.2, which is valid on \((0, \infty)\), so it covers \([a, b]\) for every \(a > 0\). By Lesson 10, Theorem 2.1 (under Assumption 2.1), with \(x^{-p}\) integrable on \([a, b]\) because it is positive and nonincreasing there (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2), the truncated integral is \(F(b) - F(a) = -1/((p-1)b^{p-1}) + 1/((p-1)a^{p-1})\). By Lemma 2.1 the first term tends to 0, so the limit is \(1/((p-1)a^{p-1}) = a^{1-p}/(p-1)\).
  353 M2-L12 · An integral at a finite endpoint
  354 Define \(\int_0^1 f = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0^+} \int_\varepsilon^1 f\), when the limit exists. Show that \(\int_0^1 x^{-2}\,dx\) diverges.
  355 Solution
  356 For \(0 < \varepsilon < 1\), Lesson 10, Theorem 2.1 (under Assumption 2.1) with \(x^{-2}\) integrable on \([\varepsilon, 1]\) because it is positive and nonincreasing there (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2), gives \(\int_\varepsilon^1 x^{-2}\,dx = F(1) - F(\varepsilon)\) with \(F(x) = -1/x\), which is \(-1 + 1/\varepsilon\). As \(\varepsilon \to 0^+\), \(1/\varepsilon\) grows without bound, so the limit does not exist as a finite number. The integral diverges.
  360 M2-L12 · A series tested by an integral
  361 For integer \(p \ge 2\), prove that the partial sums of \(\sum_{k=1}^\infty k^{-p}\) are bounded. Convergence uses the following assumption, which this course does not prove.
  362 Assumption 2.2 (monotone convergence property). Every nondecreasing sequence of real numbers that is bounded above has a finite limit.
  363 Solution
  364 Let \(f(x) = x^{-p}\), which is decreasing on \([1, N]\). The lower sum for the unit partition is \(L(f, P) = \sum_{k=2}^{N} k^{-p}\), since the infimum on \([k-1, k]\) is \(k^{-p}\). By Definition 1.3 of Lesson 9, \(L(f, P) \le \int_1^N x^{-p}\,dx\). Theorem 2.2 (under Assumption 2.1) gives \(\int_1^N x^{-p}\,dx \le 1/(p-1)\). So \(1 + \sum_{k=2}^{N} k^{-p} \le 1 + 1/(p-1)\), a bound that does not depend on \(N\). The partial sums are nondecreasing and bounded by \(1 + 1/(p-1)\), so, assuming Assumption 2.2, they converge and the series converges.
