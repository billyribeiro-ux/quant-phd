   51 If a differentiable function has a maximum or a minimum at a point inside an interval, such as a maximal payoff, its derivative vanishes there (§1 proves this and defines the terms). This lesson states what a zero derivative and the sign of a derivative establish, and what they do not. It derives the mean value theorem from a result about extremes that this lesson takes as Assumption 2.1, and from the mean value theorem it derives monotonicity from the sign of the derivative.
   53 1
   53 Local extrema and the zero derivative
   55 Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), and let \(c \in I\). The function has a local maximum at \(c\) when there is \(\delta > 0\) with \(f(x) \le f(c)\) for every \(x \in I\) with \(|x - c| < \delta\). A local minimum is defined the same way with \(f(x) \ge f(c)\). A point that is a local maximum or a local minimum is a local extremum. A critical point of a differentiable function is a point \(c\) with \(f'(c) = 0\).
   58 Proposition 1.1
   58 (a differentiable local extremum has zero derivative)
   59 Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), let \(c \in I\), and suppose \(f\) is differentiable at \(c\) and has a local extremum at \(c\). Hypotheses: the derivative exists in the sense of Lesson 4, Definition 1.1, and the extremum is local in the sense above. Then \(f'(c) = 0\).
   63 Proof
   64 Suppose \(f\) has a local maximum at \(c\), with \(\delta\) as in the definition. Let \(Q(h) = (f(c+h) - f(c))/h\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta\). For \(0 < h < \delta\), the numerator is at most 0, so \(Q(h) \le 0\). For \(-\delta < h < 0\), the numerator is at most 0 and the denominator is negative, so \(Q(h) \ge 0\). Since \(Q(h) \to f'(c)\) as \(h \to 0\), suppose \(f'(c) > 0\). Then for \(h\) small, \(Q(h) > f'(c)/2 > 0\) by the definition of the limit with tolerance \(f'(c)/2\), which contradicts \(Q(h) \le 0\) for \(0 < h < \delta\). So \(f'(c) \le 0\). The same argument from the other side gives \(f'(c) \ge 0\). Hence \(f'(c) = 0\). A local minimum is handled by the same argument with the inequalities reversed. \(\blacksquare\)
   67 Proposition 1.1 gives a necessary condition. It does not say that a zero derivative produces an extremum, and Misconception 1 shows this.

   69 Rolle's theorem and the mean value theorem
   72 Assumption 2.1
   72 (extreme value theorem, assumed here and not proved)
   73 For every function \(f\) that is continuous at each point of a closed interval \([a, b]\) with \(a < b\), \(f\) attains a maximum value and a minimum value on \([a, b]\). This is a stated assumption of this lesson. No proof is given here, and no grade is given to it. This page does not state which property of the real numbers a proof would use. Every result below that uses it begins its statement with "Assuming Assumption 2.1".
   77 Theorem 2.2
   77 (Rolle's theorem)
   78 Assuming Assumption 2.1, let \(f\) be continuous at each point of \([a, b]\), with \(a < b\), and differentiable at each point of \((a, b)\), with \(f(a) = f(b)\). Hypotheses: the continuity and differentiability are as stated, and Assumption 2.1 applies to \(f\) on \([a, b]\). Then there is \(c\) with \(a < c < b\) and \(f'(c) = 0\).
   82 Proof
   83 If \(f\) is constant on \([a, b]\), every \(c\) in \((a, b)\) works, because the derivative of a constant is 0 (Lesson 4, Problem R3). Otherwise, \(f\) takes a value different from \(f(a)\). Assumption 2.1 gives a maximum value \(M\) and a minimum value \(m\) on \([a, b]\). Since \(f\) is not constant, \(M > m\), and at least one of \(M\) and \(m\) differs from \(f(a)\). Say \(M \ne f(a)\), so \(M\) is attained at some \(c\) with \(f(c) = M\). Because \(f(a) = f(b)\) and \(M \ne f(a)\), \(c\) is neither \(a\) nor \(b\), so \(c \in (a, b)\). Then \(c\) is a local maximum of \(f\) on \((a, b)\), where \(f\) is differentiable. By Proposition 1.1, \(f'(c) = 0\). If instead \(m \ne f(a)\), the same argument applies to the minimum. \(\blacksquare\)
   87 Theorem 2.3
   87 (mean value theorem)
   88 Assuming Assumption 2.1, let \(f\) be continuous at each point of \([a, b]\), with \(a < b\), and differentiable at each point of \((a, b)\). Hypotheses: the continuity and differentiability are as in Theorem 2.2 without the condition \(f(a) = f(b)\), and Assumption 2.1 applies to \(f\) on \([a, b]\). Then there is \(c\) with \(a < c < b\) and
   89 \[f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.\]
   93 Proof
   94 Let \(s = (f(b) - f(a))/(b - a)\), and define \(g(x) = f(x) - f(a) - s(x - a)\) on \([a, b]\). The function \(x \mapsto s(x - a)\) is continuous, and it is differentiable with derivative \(s\), by the derivative of a linear function (Lesson 4, Definition 1.1 gives the quotient \(s h/h = s\)). So \(g\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), differentiable on \((a, b)\), with \(g'(x) = f'(x) - s\) there. Also \(g(a) = 0\) and \(g(b) = f(b) - f(a) - s(b - a) = 0\). Theorem 2.2 applied to \(g\) gives \(c\) in \((a, b)\) with \(g'(c) = 0\), that is, \(f'(c) = s\). \(\blacksquare\)
   97 3
   97 Monotonicity from the sign of the derivative
   99 A function \(f\) on an interval is nondecreasing when \(f(x) \le f(y)\) for \(x < y\), and increasing when \(f(x) < f(y)\) for \(x < y\). The words nonincreasing and decreasing are defined in the same way with the inequalities reversed.
  102 Corollary 3.1
  102 (sign of the derivative and monotonicity)
  103 Assuming Assumption 2.1, let \(f\) be continuous at each point of \([a, b]\), with \(a < b\), and differentiable at each point of \((a, b)\). Hypotheses: as in Theorem 2.3. (i) If \(f'(x) \ge 0\) on \((a, b)\), then \(f\) is nondecreasing on \([a, b]\). (ii) If \(f'(x) > 0\) on \((a, b)\), then \(f\) is increasing on \([a, b]\). (iii) If \(f'(x) = 0\) on \((a, b)\), then \(f\) is constant on \([a, b]\).
  107 Proof
  108 Let \(x < y\) in \([a, b]\). The function \(f\) is continuous on \([x, y]\) and differentiable on \((x, y)\), which lies in \((a, b)\). Theorem 2.3 gives \(c\) in \((x, y)\) with \(f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x)\), and \(y - x > 0\). In (i), \(f'(c) \ge 0\), so \(f(y) - f(x) \ge 0\). In (ii), \(f'(c) > 0\), so \(f(y) - f(x) > 0\). In (iii), \(f'(c) = 0\), so \(f(y) = f(x)\) for all \(x, y\), and \(f\) is constant. \(\blacksquare\)
  111 Assuming Assumption 2.1, Corollary 3.1 (ii) gives a sufficient condition for increase, not a necessary one. Misconception 2 shows that a strictly increasing function can have a zero derivative at a point.

  183 Where the canon breaks
  184 Two dropped hypotheses break Rolle's conclusion, and a third case shows that the converse of Proposition 1.1 fails.
  186 - Continuity on the closed interval is needed for Rolle's theorem. Let \(f(x) = x\) on \([0, 1)\) and \(f(1) = 0\). Then \(f(0) = f(1) = 0\), but \(f\) is not continuous at 1, because its left-hand limit there is 1. Its derivative on \((0, 1)\) is 1 and never 0, so Rolle's conclusion fails. Problem R1 gives the full check.
  187 - Differentiability on the open interval is needed for Rolle's theorem. Let \(f(x) = |x|\) on \([-1, 1]\). Then \(f(-1) = f(1) = 1\), and \(f\) is continuous. Where \(f\) is differentiable, \(f'(c) = \pm 1\), and \(f\) is not differentiable at \(c = 0\). So no point of \((-1, 1)\) has \(f'(c) = 0\), and Rolle's conclusion fails.
  188 - A zero derivative at a point is not an extremum. For \(x^3\) at 0, \(f'(0) = 0\), yet \(f\) has no local extremum there. Misconception 1 gives the argument.
  193 5
  193 Misconceptions
  195 -
  196 “A zero derivative at a point produces a local extremum there.”
  197 Let \(f(x) = x^3\). Then \(f'(0) = 0\). But \(f(x) = x^3 > 0 = f(0)\) for every \(x > 0\), and \(f(x) < 0 = f(0)\) for \(x < 0\), so 0 is not a local extremum. Proposition 1.1 gives a necessary condition, and this example shows that it is not sufficient.
  199 -
  200 “A strictly increasing function must have a positive derivative everywhere.”
  201 For \(f(x) = x^3\), \(f\) is strictly increasing on \(\R\). For \(x < y\), \(y^3 - x^3 = (y - x)(y^2 + xy + x^2)\), and \(y^2 + xy + x^2 = (x + y/2)^2 + 3y^2/4\) is positive, because \(x < y\) excludes \(x = y = 0\). So \(x^3 < y^3\). Its derivative at 0 is 0. Assuming Assumption 2.1, Corollary 3.1 (ii) asks for a positive derivative and gives a sufficient condition, and the cubic shows it is not necessary.
  203 -
  204 “The point \(c\) of the mean value theorem is the midpoint of \([a, b]\).”
  205 Take \(f(x) = x^3\) on \([0, 1]\). The slope is \((1 - 0)/1 = 1\), so \(3c^2 = 1\) and \(c = 1/\sqrt{3} \approx 0.577\), which is not the midpoint \(0.5\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, Theorem 2.3 gives a point of the mean slope, and the point computed here need not be central.

  215 Mechanics
  217 M1 · A quadratic and its minimum
  218 Let \(f(x) = x^2 - 4x + 1\). Find the critical point, and, under Assumption 2.1, the intervals on which \(f\) is decreasing and increasing.
  219 Solution
  220 We have \(f'(x) = 2x - 4\), which is zero only at \(x = 2\). It is negative for \(x < 2\) and positive for \(x > 2\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, Corollary 3.1 (ii) applied to \(-f\) on each \([x, 2]\) with \(x < 2\), and Corollary 3.1 (ii) applied to \(f\) on each \([2, y]\) with \(y > 2\), give that \(f\) is decreasing on \((-\infty, 2]\) and increasing on \([2, \infty)\). The critical point 2 is the minimum, with \(f(2) = -3\).
  224 M2 · The mean value point for a cube
  225 Assuming Assumption 2.1, for \(f(x) = x^3\) on \([0, 2]\), find the point \(c\) of Theorem 2.3.
  226 Solution
  227 The slope is \((8 - 0)/2 = 4\). We need \(3c^2 = 4\), so \(c = 2/\sqrt{3} \approx 1.1547\), which lies in \((0, 2)\). This is the point the theorem promises.
  231 M3 · Monotone on a grid interval
  232 For \(f(x) = x^3 - 3x\), decide from its derivative, under Assumption 2.1, whether \(f\) is increasing on \([-2, -1]\), and check with the values at the ends.
  233 Solution
  234 On \((-2, -1)\), \(f'(x) = 3(x-1)(x+1)\) has both factors negative, so \(f'(x) > 0\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, by Corollary 3.1 (ii), \(f\) is increasing on \([-2, -1]\). The end values are consistent with this: \(f(-2) = -2 < 2 = f(-1)\).

  242 P1 · Constant functions
  243 Assuming Assumption 2.1, prove Corollary 3.1 (iii) directly from Theorem 2.3, without citing (i) or (ii).
  244 Solution
  245 Let \(x < y\) in \([a, b]\). By Theorem 2.3 there is \(c\) in \((x, y)\) with \(f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x)\). Since \(f'(c) = 0\) by hypothesis, \(f(y) - f(x) = 0\). So \(f(x) = f(y)\) for every pair, and \(f\) is constant.
  249 P2 · Injectivity from a nonzero derivative
  250 Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\), with \(f'(x) \ne 0\) for every \(x\) in \((a, b)\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, prove that \(f\) is one-to-one on \([a, b]\), meaning that \(f(x) = f(y)\) forces \(x = y\).
  251 Solution
  252 Suppose \(f(x) = f(y)\) with \(a \le x < y \le b\) and \(x \ne y\). Then \(f\) is continuous on \([x, y]\) and differentiable on \((x, y)\), and \(f(x) = f(y)\). Rolle's theorem (Theorem 2.2) gives \(c\) in \((x, y) \subset (a, b)\) with \(f'(c) = 0\), which contradicts the hypothesis. So \(f\) is one-to-one.
  256 P3 · A nonpositive derivative
  257 Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\), with \(f'(x) \le 0\) there. Assuming Assumption 2.1, prove that \(f\) is nonincreasing on \([a, b]\).
  258 Solution
  259 Let \(g = -f\). Then \(g\) is continuous on \([a, b]\), differentiable on \((a, b)\), and \(g'(x) = -f'(x) \ge 0\). By Corollary 3.1 (i), \(g\) is nondecreasing. For \(x < y\), \(-f(x) \le -f(y)\), so \(f(y) \le f(x)\), which means \(f\) is nonincreasing.

  267 E1 · Counting the increasing intervals (from Lab 1)
  268 Lab 1 prints 8 of 16 grid intervals on which \(f\) increases. Count them from the monotone intervals, and say which grid intervals they are.
  269 Solution
  270 The increasing intervals of the cubic are \((-\infty, -1]\) and \([1, \infty)\). On the grid from \(-2\) to \(2\) with step \(1/4\), the intervals inside \([-2, -1]\) are four, and those inside \([1, 2]\) are four. Together that is 8, which agrees with the printed count.
  274 E2 · The end values (from Lab 1)
  275 Lab 1 prints \(f(-1) = 2\) and \(f(1) = -2\). Check both from \(x^3 - 3x\), and say what they are in terms of the extrema.
  276 Solution
  277 We have \(f(-1) = -1 + 3 = 2\) and \(f(1) = 1 - 3 = -2\). The point \(-1\) is where \(f\) changes from increasing to decreasing, so it is a local maximum with value 2. The point 1 is where \(f\) changes from decreasing to increasing, so it is a local minimum with value \(-2\).
  281 E3 · Why every midpoint has a nonzero sign (from Lab 1)
  282 Lab 1 reports that the sign of \(f'\) at each interval midpoint agrees with the direction on all 16 intervals. Explain why no midpoint can be a critical point.
  283 Solution
  284 The grid endpoints are multiples of \(1/4\), so each interval's midpoint is an odd multiple of \(1/8\). The critical points are \(\pm 1\), which are even multiples of \(1/8\). So no midpoint equals \(\pm 1\), and \(f'\) is nonzero at each midpoint, which makes the comparison well defined.
  290 Research
  292 R1 · Rolle's conclusion fails without continuity
  293 Let \(f(x) = x\) on \([0, 1)\) and \(f(1) = 0\). Show that \(f(0) = f(1)\), that \(f\) is differentiable on \((0, 1)\) with derivative 1 there, and that no \(c\) in \((0,1)\) has \(f'(c) = 0\). Name the hypothesis of Theorem 2.2 that fails.
  294 Solution
  295 We have \(f(0) = 0 = f(1)\). On \((0, 1)\), \(f(x) = x\), so \(f'(x) = 1\) there, and no \(c\) has derivative 0. The hypothesis that fails is continuity at 1 on \([0, 1]\): the left-hand limit at 1 is 1, while \(f(1) = 0\). So \(f\) is not continuous at the endpoint 1, and Theorem 2.2 does not apply.
  299 R2 · The mean value theorem fails for a kink
  300 Let \(f(x) = |x - 1|\) on \([0, 2]\). Show that the slope of the chord is 0, and that \(f'(c) \ne 0\) at every \(c\) in \((0, 2)\) where \(f\) is differentiable. What hypothesis of Theorem 2.3 fails?
  301 Solution
  302 The chord slope is \((f(2) - f(0))/2 = (1 - 1)/2 = 0\). For \(c\) in \((0, 1)\), \(f(x) = 1 - x\) near \(c\), so \(f'(c) = -1\). For \(c\) in \((1, 2)\), \(f(x) = x - 1\), so \(f'(c) = 1\). At \(c = 1\), \(f\) is not differentiable. So no point of \((0, 2)\) has \(f'(c) = 0\). The hypothesis that fails is differentiability at every point of \((0, 2)\).
  306 R3 · A bounded derivative gives a Lipschitz bound
  307 Let \(f\) be differentiable at every point of \(\R\), with \(|f'(x)| \le M\) for every \(x\). Assuming Assumption 2.1, prove that \(|f(x) - f(y)| \le M|x - y|\) for all \(x, y\). A function with this property is called Lipschitz with constant \(M\), and the name is given here in prose.
  308 Solution
  309 Let \(x < y\). Then \(f\) is continuous on \([x, y]\) (Lesson 4, Proposition 2.1) and differentiable on \((x, y)\). Theorem 2.3 gives \(c\) in \((x, y)\) with \(f(y) - f(x) = f'(c)(y - x)\). Then \(|f(y) - f(x)| = |f'(c)|\,(y - x) \le M(y - x)\). The case \(x = y\) is trivial, and the case \(x > y\) follows by exchanging the names.
