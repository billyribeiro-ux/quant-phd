   52 Lesson 9 defined the integral as the common value of the suprema of lower sums and infima of upper sums, and computing it from sums needs a limit of sums. The fundamental theorem removes the sums. Given Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (assumed there and not proved here), if \(F\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), differentiable on \((a,b)\) with \(F' = f\), and \(f\) is integrable on \([a,b]\), then the integral of \(f\) over \([a,b]\) is \(F(b) - F(a)\). Lessons 11 and 12 evaluate their closed-form integrals this way. Two hypotheses are easy to forget, the continuity of \(F\) on the closed interval and the integrability of \(f\), and this lesson states both and checks them on two examples.
   54 1
   54 Antiderivatives and the mean value theorem
   57 Definition 1.1
   57 (antiderivative)
   58 Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\). An antiderivative of \(f\) on \(I\) is a function \(F\) that is differentiable on \(I\) with \(F' = f\) at every point of \(I\).
   62 Theorem 1.2
   62 (mean value theorem, Theorem 2.3 of Lesson 6)
   63 Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(p < q\). If \(\varphi\) is continuous on \([p, q]\) and differentiable on \((p, q)\), then there is a point \(c\) with \(p < c < q\) such that \(\varphi(q) - \varphi(p) = \varphi'(c)\,(q - p)\).
   67 Remark 1.3
   68 Theorem 1.2 is used here in the form stated above. It is the mean value theorem of Lesson 6, whose proof there takes the extreme value theorem as an assumed fact; this lesson relies on that statement and does not reprove it.
   71 2
   71 The evaluation form
   73 For a function \(F\) and an interval \([a, b]\), the increment of \(F\) over \([a, b]\) is \(F(b) - F(a)\).
   76 Theorem 2.1
   76 (fundamental theorem of calculus, evaluation form)
   77 Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(a < b\). Let \(F\) be continuous on \([a, b]\) and differentiable on \((a, b)\), with \(F' = f\) on \((a, b)\). Suppose that \(f\) is integrable on \([a, b]\) in the sense of Definition 1.3 of Lesson 9. Then \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a). \] The function \(f\) need not be continuous. The integrability of \(f\) is stated as a hypothesis, and this lesson does not derive it from the differentiability of \(F\).
   83 Proof
   84 Let \(P\) be any partition \(a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b\). On each subinterval, \(F\) is continuous on \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\) and differentiable on \((x_{i-1}, x_i)\), so Theorem 1.2 gives a point \(c_i\) with \(x_{i-1} < c_i < x_i\) and \[ F(x_i) - F(x_{i-1}) = F'(c_i)\,\Delta x_i = f(c_i)\,\Delta x_i. \] Summing over \(i\), the left sides telescope to \(F(b) - F(a)\), so \(F(b) - F(a) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(c_i)\,\Delta x_i\). Since \(c_i\) lies in \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\), the number \(f(c_i)\) lies between the infimum \(m_i\) and the supremum \(M_i\) of \(f\) on that subinterval. Multiplying by \(\Delta x_i\) and summing, \[ L(f, P) \le F(b) - F(a) \le U(f, P). \] This holds for every partition \(P\). Taking the supremum of the left inequality over \(P\) gives \(I_* \le F(b) - F(a)\), and taking the infimum of the right inequality gives \(F(b) - F(a) \le I^*\). Since \(f\) is integrable, \(I_* = I^* = \int_a^b f\), so \(\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)\).
   92 Example 2.2
   92 (a rational antiderivative)
   93 Let \(F(x) = x/(1 + x)\). By the quotient rule, \(F'(x) = \bigl((1+x) - x\bigr)/(1+x)^2 = 1/(1+x)^2\) for \(x > -1\). The function \(f(x) = 1/(1+x)^2\) is nonincreasing on \([0, 1]\), so it is integrable there by Theorem 2.2 of Lesson 9. Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 2.1 gives \[ \int_0^1 \frac{dx}{(1+x)^2} = F(1) - F(0) = \frac{1}{2} - 0 = \frac{1}{2}. \] The quotient \(F\) is continuous on \([0, 1]\), since its denominator does not vanish there.

   98 Polynomials
  101 Corollary 3.1
  101 (powers)
  102 Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), for every integer \(k \ge 0\) and every \(0 \le a < b\) (for \(a = b\), both sides are 0 by the convention \(\int_a^a f = 0\) of Lesson 9, Definition 1.3), \(\displaystyle \int_a^b x^k\,dx = \frac{b^{k+1} - a^{k+1}}{k+1}\).
  106 Proof
  107 Let \(F(x) = x^{k+1}/(k+1)\). Then \(F\) is a polynomial, hence continuous, and \(F'(x) = x^k\) by the power rule for positive integer exponents (Lesson 7, Example 1.3, which uses the product rule of Lesson 5 by induction), and for \(k = 0\) directly, since \(F(x) = x\) and \(F' = 1\). For \(k = 0\) the integrand is constant, and for \(k \ge 1\) the function \(x^k\) is nondecreasing on \([0, \infty)\), because for \(0 \le s \le t\) we have \(t^k - s^k = (t - s)\sum_{j=0}^{k-1} t^{k-1-j}s^j \ge 0\). So \(x^k\) is integrable on \([a, b]\) by Theorem 2.2 of Lesson 9, and Theorem 2.1 gives the formula.
  111 Example 3.2
  111 (the cubic of Lab 1)
  112 Let \(F(x) = x^3 - 2x\), so \(F' = f = 3x^2 - 2\). The function \(f\) is nondecreasing on \([0, 1]\), because \(f'(x) = 6x \ge 0\) there (Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 6, under Assumption 2.1), so it is integrable by Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2. Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 2.1 gives \(\int_0^1 (3x^2 - 2)\,dx = F(1) - F(0) = (1 - 2) - 0 = -1\), which is the value that the sums of Lab 1, part A bracket.

  235 4
  235 Two antiderivatives differ by a constant
  238 Proposition 4.1
  238 (constant difference)
  239 Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6 (the extreme value theorem, assumed there and not proved here), let \(F\) and \(G\) be antiderivatives of the same \(f\) on an open interval \(I\). Then \(F - G\) is constant on \(I\).
  243 Proof
  244 Let \(H = F - G\). Then \(H' = F' - G' = f - f = 0\) on \(I\). For \(p < q\) in \(I\), Theorem 1.2 applied to \(H\) on \([p, q]\) gives \(H(q) - H(p) = H'(c)(q - p) = 0\). So \(H\) takes the same value at any two points of \(I\).
  247 5
  247 Where the canon breaks
  251 - Integrability is a hypothesis. The proof uses it in the last step, where \(I_* = I^*\) turns two inequalities into an equality. Without it, Theorem 2.1 gives only the bracket \(L(f,P) \le F(b) - F(a) \le U(f,P)\) for each partition, and no value for the integral. The integrands this lesson uses are integrable by monotonicity (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2), and it proves integrability for no other class.
  252 - Continuity of \(F\) is needed on the closed interval. The mean value theorem is applied on each closed subinterval \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\), whose endpoints include the interior partition points \(x_1\) to \(x_{n-1}\), so \(F\) must be continuous at every point of \([a, b]\). A condition only on the open interval would not give \(F(b) - F(a)\): \(F(x) = x\) on \((0, 1)\) with \(F(0) = 5\) and \(F(1) = 1\) is differentiable on \((0,1)\) with \(F' = 1\), and \(\int_0^1 F' = 1\), but \(F(1) - F(0) = -4\).
  253 - The formula uses differences, not values. Adding a constant to \(F\) changes \(F(b)\) and \(F(a)\) equally, so the integral is unchanged. Example 3.2 and the lab use \(F(1) - F(0)\), not \(F(1)\).
  258 6
  258 Misconceptions
  260 -
  261 “Differentiability of \(F\) on \((a, b)\) is enough; nothing is needed about \(F'\).”
  262 Theorem 2.1 lists the integrability of \(f = F'\) as a hypothesis, and the proof uses it at the last step, where the sums \(L(f,P)\) and \(U(f,P)\) must have the common value \(I_* = I^*\). The proof therefore needs that step, and the value of the integral is not established without it. The correction is to check integrability, which Lesson 9 does for monotone derivatives.
  264 -
  265 “The integral \(\int_a^x f\) equals \(F(x)\), with no constant to subtract.”
  266 The correct value is \(F(x) - F(a)\). Take \(G(x) = x/(1+x) + 5\), which has the same derivative as \(F(x) = x/(1+x)\). Then \(G(1) - G(0) = 1/2\), the same as \(F(1) - F(0)\), while \(G(1) = 11/2\) differs from \(F(1) = 1/2\). The integral depends on \(F\) through the difference \(F(b) - F(a)\), so the added constant 5 cancels, and Proposition 4.1 states that two antiderivatives of the same \(f\) differ by a constant.
  268 -
  269 “The fundamental theorem applies only to polynomials.”
  270 Example 2.2 applies Theorem 2.1 to \(F(x) = x/(1+x)\), which is not a polynomial, and Lab 1, part B checks the bracket for it at \(n = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128\). Each hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 holds for it: \(F\) is continuous on \([0, 1]\) and differentiable on \((0, 1)\), and \(f = 1/(1+x)^2\) is nonincreasing on \([0, 1]\), hence integrable (Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2).

  282 M2-L10 · A cubic over \([0, 2]\)
  283 Evaluate \(\int_0^2 (3x^2 - 2)\,dx\) by Theorem 2.1, and check that the derivative is nondecreasing on \([0, 2]\).
  284 Solution
  285 An antiderivative is \(F(x) = x^3 - 2x\). Then \(F(2) - F(0) = (8 - 4) - 0 = 4\). The integrand \(3x^2 - 2\) has derivative \(6x \ge 0\) on \([0, 2]\), so it is nondecreasing there (Corollary 3.1 of Lesson 6, under Assumption 2.1) and integrable by Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2. So, under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 2.1 gives the integral 4.
  289 M2-L10 · Powers over \([1, 3]\)
  290 Evaluate \(\int_1^3 x^2\,dx\) by Corollary 3.1.
  291 Solution
  292 Corollary 3.1 with \(k = 2\), \(a = 1\), \(b = 3\) gives \((3^3 - 1^3)/3 = (27 - 1)/3 = 26/3\). The interval lies in \([0, \infty)\), which is where the power is nondecreasing, so the corollary applies.
  296 M2-L10 · A decreasing integrand on \([1, 2]\)
  297 Evaluate \(\int_1^2 dx/(1+x)^2\) using the antiderivative \(F(x) = -1/(1+x)\).
  298 Solution
  299 The derivative of \(-1/(1+x)\) is \(1/(1+x)^2\) by the quotient rule, since \(\frac{d}{dx}(-1) = 0\) and \(\frac{d}{dx}(1+x) = 1\). The function \(1/(1+x)^2\) is decreasing on \([1, 2]\), hence integrable. By Theorem 2.1 (under Assumption 2.1), the integral is \(F(2) - F(1) = -1/3 + 1/2 = 1/6\).

  307 M2-L10 · The power rule from the product rule
  308 Prove that \((x^{j})' = j\,x^{j-1}\) for every integer \(j \ge 1\), by induction, using only the product rule.
  309 Solution
  310 For \(j = 1\), the derivative of \(x\) is 1. Assume \((x^j)' = j x^{j-1}\). Then \((x^{j+1})' = (x \cdot x^j)' = x\,(x^j)' + x^j \cdot 1 = x \cdot j x^{j-1} + x^j = (j+1)x^j\). This is the claim for \(j + 1\).
  314 M2-L10 · The sandwich for one partition
  315 Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2.1, prove that \(F(b) - F(a)\) lies between \(L(f,P)\) and \(U(f,P)\) for every partition \(P\), without using the integral.
  316 Solution
  317 On each \([x_{i-1}, x_i]\), Theorem 1.2 gives \(c_i\) in \((x_{i-1}, x_i)\) with \(F(x_i) - F(x_{i-1}) = f(c_i)\Delta x_i\). Since \(m_i \le f(c_i) \le M_i\), summing and telescoping gives \(L(f,P) \le F(b) - F(a) \le U(f,P)\). No integral appears in this argument.
  321 M2-L10 · Constant differences on an interval
  322 Let \(F\) and \(G\) be antiderivatives of \(f\) on \((a,b)\). Show that \(F(x) - G(x) = F(y) - G(y)\) for all \(x, y\) in \((a,b)\), and deduce that \(F(b) - F(a) = G(b) - G(a)\) when \(F\) and \(G\) are both continuous on \([a,b]\).
  323 Solution
  324 Under Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Proposition 4.1 gives that \(F - G\) is constant on \((a,b)\). So \(F(x) - G(x) = F(y) - G(y)\) for \(x, y\) in \((a,b)\). Then \(F(b) - G(b)\) and \(F(a) - G(a)\) are equal when \(F\) and \(G\) are continuous on \([a,b]\), because the constant on \((a,b)\) extends to the endpoints by continuity. Subtracting gives \(F(b) - F(a) = G(b) - G(a)\).

  346 M2-L10 · Expected size of the central-difference error
  347 Lab 1, part C uses a central difference with step \(h = 10^{-6}\) at \(x = 0.3\). Estimate its truncation error, using \(F''' (x) = 6/(1+x)^4\) for \(F(x) = x/(1+x)\).
  348 Solution
  349 The truncation error of a central difference is about \(h^2 F'''(x)/6\). At \(x = 0.3\), \(F'''(0.3) = 6/1.3^4 = 6/2.8561 \approx 2.1\), so the truncation error is about \(10^{-12} \cdot 2.1/6 \approx 3.5 \times 10^{-13}\). Each value \(F(0.3 \pm h)\) is near \(0.3/1.3 \approx 0.23\), and the rounding of each value is counted as follows, each error at most half an ulp. Forming \(x \pm h\) (half an ulp \(2.8 \times 10^{-17}\) at 0.3) moves \(F\) by \(1/(1+y)^2 \approx 0.59\) times that, about \(1.6 \times 10^{-17}\). Forming \(1 + x \pm h\) (half an ulp \(1.1 \times 10^{-16}\) at 1.3) moves \(F\) by \(y/(1+y)^2 \approx 0.18\) times that, about \(2.0 \times 10^{-17}\). The quotient (half an ulp \(1.4 \times 10^{-17}\) at 0.23) adds \(1.4 \times 10^{-17}\). Each value therefore carries at most about \(5.0 \times 10^{-17}\), and the difference of two values, divided by \(2h = 2 \times 10^{-6}\), carries rounding error at most about \(5 \times 10^{-11}\) by this count. Both are far below the \(10^{-7}\) tolerance of part C.

  357 M2-L10 · A two-sided bound from the mean value theorem
  358 Let \(F\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), with \(F' = f\) on \((a,b)\), where \(f\) is defined and nondecreasing on \([a,b]\). Prove that \((b-a)f(a) \le F(b) - F(a) \le (b-a)f(b)\).
  359 Solution
  360 Assuming Assumption 2.1 of Lesson 6, Theorem 1.2 gives a point \(c\) in \((a,b)\) with \(F(b) - F(a) = f(c)(b-a)\). Since \(f\) is nondecreasing and \(a < c < b\), \(f(a) \le f(c) \le f(b)\), and multiplying by \(b - a > 0\) gives the claim. The bound needs no integral.
  364 M2-L10 · Monotone in the upper limit
  365 For integer \(k \ge 1\) and \(0 \le a\), show that \(b \mapsto \int_a^b x^k\,dx\) is strictly increasing on \([a, \infty)\).
  366 Solution
  367 By Corollary 3.1 (under Assumption 2.1) the integral equals \((b^{k+1} - a^{k+1})/(k+1)\). For \(b_1 < b_2\) with both at least \(a \ge 0\), the map \(t \mapsto t^{k+1}\) is strictly increasing on \([0, \infty)\), so \(b_2^{k+1} > b_1^{k+1}\), and the difference of the integrals is positive.
  371 M2-L10 · The gap bound for the cubic
  372 For \(f = 3x^2 - 2\) on \([0,1]\) and the uniform partition into \(n\) parts, show from Lesson 9, Theorem 2.2 that \(U_n - L_n \le 3/n\), and that equality holds.
  373 Solution
  374 The mesh is \(1/n\), and \(f(1) - f(0) = 1 - (-2) = 3\). Theorem 2.2 gives \(U_n - L_n \le (1/n)\cdot 3 = 3/n\). For the uniform partition, every subinterval has width \(1/n\), and \(f\) is nondecreasing, so \(U_n - L_n = \sum_i (f(x_i) - f(x_{i-1}))/n = (f(1) - f(0))/n = 3/n\). Equality holds.
