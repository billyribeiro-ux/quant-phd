   51 A sensitivity measures how a value moves when an input moves a little. The slope of a price against a yield is the simplest case. That slope is a limit of a ratio of changes, and this lesson defines it exactly. It proves that a function with a derivative at a point is continuous there, and it shows, with \(|x|\) at 0, that a continuous function can fail to have a derivative.
   53 1
   53 The derivative as a limit
   56 Definition 1.1
   56 (derivative at a point)
   57 Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), and let \(a \in I\). For each \(h \ne 0\) with \(a + h \in I\), put \(Q(h) = \dfrac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}\), the difference quotient at \(a\). If \(\lim_{h \to 0} Q(h)\) exists, in the sense of Lesson 3, Definition 1.1 with \(h\) in place of \(x\) and \(0\) in place of \(a\), then \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\). Its derivative at \(a\) is that limit, written \(f'(a)\).
   60 The derivative is a limit of ratios of changes. The numerator \(f(a+h) - f(a)\) is the change in the value, the denominator \(h\) is the change in the input, and \(f'(a)\) is what the ratio approaches as the input change shrinks. This is the meaning of derivative used in this lesson.
   63 Example 1.2
   63 (the square)
   64 Let \(f(x) = x^2\) on \(\R\). For \(h \ne 0\), \(Q(h) = \dfrac{(a + h)^2 - a^2}{h} = \dfrac{2ah + h^2}{h} = 2a + h\). Then \(|Q(h) - 2a| = |h|\), so for \(\varepsilon > 0\) the choice \(\delta = \varepsilon\) gives \(|Q(h) - 2a| < \varepsilon\) whenever \(0 < |h| < \delta\). Hence \(f\) is differentiable at every \(a\) with \(f'(a) = 2a\).

  125 Differentiable implies continuous
  128 Proposition 2.1
  128 (differentiability implies continuity)
  129 Let \(f\) be defined on an open interval \(I\), let \(a \in I\), and suppose \(f\) is differentiable at \(a\). Hypotheses: the derivative \(f'(a)\) exists in the sense of Definition 1.1. Then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) in the sequential sense of Lesson 2, §2.
  133 Proof
  134 Let \(\varepsilon > 0\). Apply Definition 1.1 with tolerance 1 in place of \(\varepsilon\): there is \(\delta_1 > 0\) such that \(|Q(h) - f'(a)| < 1\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta_1\), with \(a + h \in I\). For such \(h\), \(|Q(h)| < |f'(a)| + 1\). Put \(\delta = \min\bigl(\delta_1, \varepsilon/(|f'(a)| + 1)\bigr)\). For \(x \in I\) with \(0 < |x - a| < \delta\), \(f(x) - f(a) = (x - a)\,Q(x - a)\), so
  135 \[|f(x) - f(a)| = |x - a|\,|Q(x - a)| < \delta\bigl(|f'(a)| + 1\bigr) \le \varepsilon.\]
  136 For \(x = a\) the difference is 0. So \(|f(x) - f(a)| < \varepsilon\) for every \(x \in I\) with \(|x - a| < \delta\). Now let \((x_n)\) be a sequence in \(I\) with \(x_n \to a\). Choose \(N\) with \(|x_n - a| < \delta\) for \(n \ge N\); then \(|f(x_n) - f(a)| < \varepsilon\) for these \(n\). Since \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary, \(f(x_n) \to f(a)\). \(\blacksquare\)
  139 Proposition 2.1 gives one direction only. A continuous function need not be differentiable, and Example 3.1 gives the case.
  141 3
  141 Where the derivative fails
  144 Example 3.1
  144 (the absolute value at its kink)
  145 Let \(f(x) = |x|\) on \(\R\). At \(a = 0\), \(Q(h) = |h|/h\), which is 1 for \(h > 0\) and \(-1\) for \(h < 0\). The right-hand limit of \(Q\) at 0 is 1 and the left-hand limit is \(-1\). By Lesson 3, Proposition 2.2, the two-sided limit \(\lim_{h \to 0} Q(h)\) does not exist. So \(f\) is not differentiable at 0. The function is continuous at 0, because \(|x_n - 0| = |x_n| \to 0\) whenever \(x_n \to 0\), by Definition 2.1 of Lesson 1.
  148 Example 3.1 is the converse failure promised after Proposition 2.1: a function can be continuous at a point and have no derivative there. The derivative needs the difference quotient to have one limit as \(h \to 0\), approached from both sides. Continuity at 0 requires \(f(x) \to f(0)\), and it does not examine the quotient.
  151 4
  151 Where the canon breaks
  152 The definition is a statement about one point, and it depends on the limit being the same from both sides. Three points of practice need care.
  154 - A formula is not a derivative. A function given by a formula can have no derivative at a point where the formula changes, as Example 3.1 shows for \(|x|\). The derivative is checked at each point, not inferred from the formula elsewhere.
  155 - The quotient is an approximation, not the derivative. Lab 1 gives \(Q(0.001) = 3.003001\), which differs from \(f'(1) = 3\). The derivative is the limit, and the quotient at a single step is an approximation to it.
  156 - Floating-point arithmetic is a further source of error. The lab computes in exact rational arithmetic, so its quotients carry no floating-point rounding; only its printed decimals are rounded, for display. Lesson 13 treats the effect of rounding on the quotient when it is computed in floating-point arithmetic.
  161 5
  161 Misconceptions
  163 -
  164 “Every function given by a formula has a derivative at every point.”
  165 Example 3.1 gives \(f(x) = |x|\), a formula, with no derivative at 0. The one-sided quotients differ, so the limit in Definition 1.1 does not exist.
  167 -
  168 “A continuous function is differentiable.”
  169 Example 3.1 is continuous at 0 and not differentiable there. Proposition 2.1 proves the direction from differentiable to continuous, and says nothing about the converse.
  171 -
  172 “The quotient at \(h = 0.001\) is the derivative.”
  173 For \(x^3\) at \(a = 1\), Lab 1 gives \(Q(0.001) = 3.003001\), while \(f'(1) = 3\). The quotient differs from the derivative by \(3h + h^2 = 0.003001\) at that step. The derivative is the limit, which the quotient approaches but does not equal.

  183 Mechanics
  185 M1 · A linear function
  186 Let \(f(x) = 5x - 2\). Find \(f'(3)\) from Definition 1.1.
  187 Solution
  188 The difference quotient is \(Q(h) = (5(3 + h) - 2 - (5 \cdot 3 - 2))/h = 5h/h = 5\) for \(h \ne 0\). The constant sequence has limit 5, so \(f'(3) = 5\).
  192 M2 · The square at a negative point
  193 Let \(f(x) = x^2\). Find \(f'(-1)\) from Example 1.2, and check it with the quotient at \(h = 0.01\).
  194 Solution
  195 Example 1.2 gives \(f'(a) = 2a\), so \(f'(-1) = -2\). At \(h = 0.01\), \(Q(h) = 2(-1) + 0.01 = -1.99\), which is \(-2\) plus the step, so the check agrees with the derivative to within the step.
  199 M3 · The reciprocal
  200 Let \(f(x) = 1/x\) for \(x \ne 0\). Find \(f'(2)\) from Definition 1.1.
  201 Solution
  202 For \(h \ne 0\) with \(2 + h \ne 0\), \(Q(h) = \dfrac{1/(2+h) - 1/2}{h} = \dfrac{2 - (2 + h)}{2(2+h)h} = \dfrac{-1}{2(2+h)}\). As \(h \to 0\) this tends to \(-1/4\), by the quotient law of Lesson 2, Proposition 1.1 (iii), applied to sequences \(h_n \to 0\), since the denominator \(2(2+h_n)\) tends to \(4 \ne 0\). So \(f'(2) = -1/4\).
  208 Proof
  210 P1 · The cube at any point
  211 Let \(f(x) = x^3\). Prove from Definition 1.1 that \(f'(a) = 3a^2\) for every real \(a\).
  212 Solution
  213 We have \(Q(h) = ((a+h)^3 - a^3)/h = 3a^2 + 3ah + h^2\). So \(|Q(h) - 3a^2| = |h|\,|3a + h| \le |h|(3|a| + |h|)\). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\) and put \(\delta = \min\bigl(1, \varepsilon/(3|a| + 1)\bigr)\). For \(0 < |h| < \delta\), \(|h| < 1\), so \(|Q(h) - 3a^2| < \delta(3|a| + 1) \le \varepsilon\). Hence \(f'(a) = 3a^2\).
  217 P2 · The sum rule
  218 Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable at \(a\). Prove that \(f + g\) is differentiable at \(a\) with \((f+g)'(a) = f'(a) + g'(a)\).
  219 Solution
  220 For \(h \ne 0\), the difference quotient of \(f + g\) is \(Q_f(h) + Q_g(h)\). Given \(\varepsilon > 0\), choose \(\delta_1\) with \(|Q_f(h) - f'(a)| < \varepsilon/2\) and \(\delta_2\) with \(|Q_g(h) - g'(a)| < \varepsilon/2\) for \(0 < |h| < \min(\delta_1, \delta_2)\), with \(\delta = \min(\delta_1,\delta_2)\). Then \(|(Q_f + Q_g)(h) - (f'(a) + g'(a))| < \varepsilon\). So the sum is differentiable at \(a\) with the stated derivative.
  224 P3 · A constant multiple
  225 Let \(f\) be differentiable at \(a\) and let \(c\) be a real constant. Prove that \(cf\) is differentiable at \(a\) with \((cf)'(a) = c\,f'(a)\).
  226 Solution
  227 The difference quotient of \(cf\) is \(cQ(h)\). Let \(\varepsilon > 0\), and choose \(\delta\) with \(|Q(h) - f'(a)| < \varepsilon/(|c| + 1)\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta\). Then \(|cQ(h) - cf'(a)| = |c|\,|Q(h) - f'(a)| < |c|\varepsilon/(|c| + 1) < \varepsilon\). So \((cf)'(a) = c f'(a)\).
  233 Empirical
  235 E1 · The quotient at h = 0.1 (from Lab 1)
  236 Lab 1 prints the quotient and its error at \(h = 0.1\). Check both from the expansion \(3 + 3h + h^2\).
  237 Solution
  238 At \(h = 0.1\), \(3 + 0.3 + 0.01 = 3.31\), and the error is \(0.31\), which the lab prints as 3.310000 and 3.100000e-01 at that step. The expansion matches the printed values.
  242 E2 · The error at h = 10^-5 (from Lab 1)
  243 Lab 1 prints the error at \(h = 10^{-5}\) as \(3.000010 \times 10^{-5}\). Compute the exact error and check the printed value.
  244 Solution
  245 The exact error is \(3h + h^2 = 3 \times 10^{-5} + 10^{-10} = 3.00001 \times 10^{-5}\), since \(10^{-10} = 10^{-5} \cdot 10^{-5}\). The printed value \(3.000010 \times 10^{-5}\) is this number with a trailing zero, so it agrees with the output.
  249 E3 · The ratio of error to step (from Lab 1)
  250 Lab 1 prints the error divided by \(h\) at \(h = 10^{-6}\) as 3.000001. Explain from the expansion why this ratio is \(3 + h\), and why it does not depend on the printed rounding.
  251 Solution
  252 The error is \(3h + h^2\), so dividing by \(h\) gives \(3 + h\) exactly for \(h > 0\). At \(h = 10^{-6}\) this is \(3.000001\) exactly, and the printed value is the same number. The ratio is the coefficient 3 plus the step, which is why it approaches 3 as \(h\) falls.
  258 Research
  260 R1 · A derivative at 0 for x|x|
  261 Let \(f(x) = x|x|\). Show that \(f\) is differentiable at 0 with \(f'(0) = 0\), and say what this shows about the one-sided quotients that a derivative needs.
  262 Solution
  263 At \(a = 0\), \(Q(h) = h|h|/h = |h|\) for \(h \ne 0\). Since \(|Q(h) - 0| = |h|\), the choice \(\delta = \varepsilon\) gives \(|Q(h)| < \varepsilon\) for \(0 < |h| < \delta\). So \(f'(0) = 0\). Unlike \(|x|\) in Example 3.1, the quotient here has a single limit from both sides, because the sign of \(h\) cancels.
  267 R2 · The quotient along a sequence of steps
  268 Let \(f\) be differentiable at \(a\). Show that \(Q(1/n) \to f'(a)\), where \(Q\) is the difference quotient at \(a\), for the sequence of steps \(h_n = 1/n\).
  269 Solution
  270 The function \(Q\) has limit \(f'(a)\) at 0, by Definition 1.1. The sequence \(h_n = 1/n\) avoids 0 and tends to 0. By Lesson 3, Proposition 1.3, applied to \(Q\) at the point 0, \(Q(h_n) \to f'(a)\). The argument uses the limit of \(Q\) and the differentiability of \(f\) at \(a\), and nothing else about \(f\).
  274 R3 · The derivative of a constant
  275 Let \(f(x) = c\) for every \(x\), with \(c\) constant. Find \(f'(a)\) for every \(a\), and say which step of Definition 1.1 uses that \(f\) is constant.
  276 Solution
  277 For \(h \ne 0\), \(Q(h) = (c - c)/h = 0\). The quotient is the constant sequence 0, so its limit is 0, and \(f'(a) = 0\) at every \(a\). The step that uses constancy is the equality \(f(a + h) = f(a)\), which makes the numerator zero for every \(h\).
