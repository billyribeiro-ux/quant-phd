Expected utility weights each outcome by its probability. This lesson keeps the outcome values, replaces each probability by a decision weight (Definition 1.1), and shows that the replacement can change which lottery ranks higher. This lesson defines a weighting function, applies it to the outcomes of a finite lottery one at a time, and gives a pair of lotteries on which the weighted value of Definition 1.2 ranks a first-order-dominated lottery above its dominator. Expected utility with a nondecreasing utility cannot do this on a finite set of outcomes (Lesson 4, Theorem 1.5). The lesson does not claim how people weight probabilities, and it quotes no empirical magnitude; its weights are values of Example 1.4.
1Weighting functions
Expected utility weights each outcome by its probability. Here the probability of an outcome is first passed through a function, and only then multiplied by the value of the outcome. A finite lottery is a list of pairs \((x_i, p_i)\), with \(p_i \ge 0\) and \(\sum_i p_i = 1\). Outcomes are in currency units (CU). This lesson uses gains only, \(x_i \ge 0\), so the value function of Lesson 6 is \(v(x) = x^{\alpha}\) with \(v(0) = 0\).
Definition 1.1 (weighting function)
A weighting function is a map \(\omega : [0, 1] \to [0, 1]\) that is nondecreasing, with \(\omega(0) = 0\) and \(\omega(1) = 1\). The number \(\omega(p)\) is the decision weight of an outcome whose probability is \(p\).
Definition 1.2 (non-cumulative weighted value)
Let \(\omega\) be a weighting function and \(v\) a value function with \(v(0) = 0\). For a finite lottery \(L = \{(x_i, p_i)\}\) on gains, the probability weighting of \(L\) is
\[ V_\omega(L) = \sum_i \omega(p_i)\, v(x_i). \]
The word "non-cumulative" refers to the weights: each outcome's own probability is weighted, not the probability of reaching it or of doing at least as well. Expected utility is the case \(\omega(p) = p\) and \(v = u\), because then \(V_\omega(L) = \sum_i p_i u(x_i) = \E\,u(X)\).
Remark 1.3 (the identity weighting)
If \(\omega(p) = p\) for every \(p\), then \(V_\omega(L) = \sum_i p_i v(x_i)\) for every finite lottery \(L\). This is the expected value of \(v(X)\).
Proof
Substitute \(\omega(p_i) = p_i\) into Definition 1.2. The sum is the definition of the expected value of \(v(X)\), where \(X\) takes the value \(x_i\) with probability \(p_i\).
The name prospect theory appears in the title of the 1979 paper by Kahneman and Tversky. This lesson states no weighting function, estimate or functional form from that paper, which is not read here, and it uses the non-cumulative value of Definition 1.2 as a mathematical object.
Example 1.4 (a weighting function with \(\eta = 1/2\))
Take the family \(\omega_\eta(p) = p^{\eta}/\bigl(p^{\eta} + (1 - p)^{\eta}\bigr)^{1/\eta}\) with \(\eta = 1/2\). Then \(\omega_{1/2}(0) = 0\) and \(\omega_{1/2}(1) = 1\). Because \((\sqrt{p} + \sqrt{1 - p})^2 = p + (1 - p) + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)} = 1 + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)}\), the closed form is
\[ \omega(p) = \frac{\sqrt{p}}{1 + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)}}. \]
This closed form shows that \(0 \le \omega(p) \le \sqrt{p} \le 1\) on \([0, 1]\). It is also nondecreasing there. Write \(q = \sqrt{p(1 - p)}\) and \(g = 1 + 2q\), so that \(\omega = \sqrt{p}/g\). Then \(q' = (1 - 2p)/(2q)\) and \(g' = (1 - 2p)/q\) on \((0, 1)\), and the quotient rule gives \(\omega' = \bigl(g/(2\sqrt{p}) - \sqrt{p}\, g'\bigr)/g^2\). Multiplying the numerator by \(2\sqrt{p}\, q\) gives \(qg - 2p(1 - 2p) = q + 2q^2 - 2p + 4p^2\). Since \(q^2 = p - p^2\), this equals \(q + 2p^2\), so
\[ \omega'(p) = \frac{q + 2p^2}{2\sqrt{p}\, q\, (1 + 2q)^2} > 0 \qquad (0 < p < 1). \]
The function is continuous on \([0, 1]\) and has a positive derivative on \((0, 1)\), so by the mean value theorem it is nondecreasing on \([0, 1]\). It is therefore a weighting function in the sense of Definition 1.1.
The values used below, rounded to eight decimals, are \(\omega(3/10) = 0.28579088\), \(\omega(3/5) = 0.39125077\) and \(\omega(1/2) = 1/(2\sqrt{2}) = 0.35355339\). Their unrounded values, to ten decimals, are 0.2857908849, 0.3912507700 and 0.3535533906. Lab 1, part A, evaluates the general formula and the closed form on 1001 probabilities and reports a largest gap of \(3.33 \times 10^{-16}\). Values quoted to eight decimals on this page are rounded from unrounded values, so a quotient of the printed digits can differ in the last digit.
Observation 1.5 (the shape of this example, on a grid)
For \(\omega = \omega_{1/2}\), the values lie in \([0, 1]\) and \(\omega\) is nondecreasing on \([0, 1]\); Example 1.4 proves both by the closed form and its derivative. On the 1001 equally spaced probabilities \(0, 0.001, \ldots, 1\), the difference \(\omega(p) - p\) changes sign once between the interior grid points, and the last grid point with \(\omega(p) > p\) is \(0.278\). These two sign facts are finite checks on the grid, not theorems, and Lab 1, part A, reports them.
Under this example the weight exceeds the probability at 0.01 and at 0.05, and it falls below the probability at 0.3, 0.5, 0.6, 0.95 and 0.99. The printed values from Lab 1, part A, are \(\omega(0.01) = 0.0834 > 0.01\), \(\omega(0.05) = 0.1557 > 0.05\), \(\omega(0.3) = 0.2858 < 0.3\), \(\omega(0.5) = 0.3536 < 0.5\), \(\omega(0.6) = 0.3913 < 0.6\), \(\omega(0.95) = 0.6788 < 0.95\), \(\omega(0.99) = 0.8298 < 0.99\). The rounding is to four decimals. Each comparison also holds for the unrounded values.
Remark 1.6 (the weights of a lottery need not add up to one)
Under the identity weighting the weights of a lottery's outcomes sum to \(\sum_i p_i = 1\). Under \(\omega_{1/2}\) they do not. Lottery \(A\) of Example 2.1 (Section 2) has three outcomes: 0 with probability \(2/5\), and 50 CU and 100 CU with probability \(3/10\) each. Its weights are \(\omega(2/5)\), \(\omega(3/10)\) and \(\omega(3/10)\), which sum to \(\omega(2/5) + 2\,\omega(3/10) = 0.31945492 + 0.57158177 = 0.89103669\) from unrounded values, not one. The two positive outcomes of \(A\) alone carry \(2\,\omega(3/10) = 0.57158177\). The single positive outcome of the lottery \(B\) of Example 2.1 has probability \(3/5\), and its weight is \(\omega(3/5) = 0.39125077\). This is the arithmetic behind the exhibit.
2Dominance, and a weighted ranking that reverses it
Lesson 4 defines first-order stochastic dominance by distribution functions. For finite lotteries on the same outcomes, \(B\) first-order dominates \(A\) when \(F_B(t) \le F_A(t)\) for every outcome \(t\), with \(F(t) = \Prob(X \le t)\). For lotteries on one finite set of outcomes, Lesson 4 (Theorem 1.5) proves that expected utility with any nondecreasing \(u\) ranks \(B\) at or above \(A\) when \(B\) first-order dominates \(A\).
Example 2.1 (the pair A and B)
Lottery \(A\) pays 0 with probability \(2/5\), 50 CU with probability \(3/10\), and 100 CU with probability \(3/10\). Lottery \(B\) pays 0 with probability \(2/5\) and 100 CU with probability \(3/5\). The distribution functions are exact fractions:
| \(t\) (CU) | \(F_A(t)\) | \(F_B(t)\) | \(F_B(t) \le F_A(t)\)
| 0 | \(2/5 = 0.4000\) | \(2/5 = 0.4000\) | yes, with equality
| 50 | \(7/10 = 0.7000\) | \(2/5 = 0.4000\) | yes, strictly
| 100 | 1 | 1 | yes, with equality
So \(B\) first-order dominates \(A\), and the inequality is strict at \(t = 50\).
Proposition 2.2 (a weighted ranking that reverses dominance)
Assume:
A1. the lotteries \(A\) and \(B\) of Example 2.1, with finite support on gains;
A2. the weighting function \(\omega = \omega_{1/2}\) of Example 1.4;
A3. the value function \(v(x) = x^{\alpha}\) for \(x \ge 0\), with \(\alpha > 0\) and \(v(0) = 0\).
Then \(B\) first-order dominates \(A\), and \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) if and only if
\[ \frac{\omega(3/10)}{\omega(3/5)} > \frac{1}{1 + 2^{-\alpha}}. \]
The threshold is \(2/3\) at \(\alpha = 1\) and \(0.58578644\) at \(\alpha = 1/2\). The ratio is \(0.73045450\) from unrounded values, so \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) at both values of \(\alpha\).
Proof
Dominance is Example 2.1. For the weighted values, the outcome 0 contributes \(\omega(2/5)\,v(0) = 0\). So \(V_\omega(A) = \omega(3/10)\,(50^{\alpha} + 100^{\alpha})\) and \(V_\omega(B) = \omega(3/5)\,100^{\alpha}\). Since \(50^{\alpha} = 100^{\alpha} 2^{-\alpha}\), dividing both sides by \(100^{\alpha} > 0\) gives \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) if and only if \(\omega(3/10)(1 + 2^{-\alpha}) > \omega(3/5)\). Dividing by \(\omega(3/5) > 0\) gives the stated condition. At \(\alpha = 1\) the threshold is \(1/(1 + 1/2) = 2/3\). At \(\alpha = 1/2\) it is \(1/(1 + 2^{-1/2}) = 0.58578644\). The ratio \(\omega(3/10)/\omega(3/5)\) is computed from the unrounded closed form of Example 1.4, and it is \(0.73045450\) to eight decimals. The quotient of the eight-decimal values printed above, \(0.28579088 / 0.39125077\), is \(0.73045449\), so the last digit depends on the basis.
Remark 2.3 (what the reversal does and does not show)
Under the identity weighting, the same pair gives \(V(A) = 0.3 \times 150 = 45\) and \(V(B) = 0.6 \times 100 = 60\) at \(\alpha = 1\), so \(B\) ranks higher, as dominance requires. Holding the outcomes and the value function fixed, the change from the identity weighting to \(\omega\) reverses the ranking at \(\alpha = 1\), so at that value of \(\alpha\) the reversal comes from the weights. It is a property of this form of the weighted value. It is not evidence about how people choose. The lesson takes no position on that question.
Lab 1 · The weighting function, and the pair that the weighted value ranks against dominance
Stated before running: the closed form of Example 1.4 equals the general formula on 1001 probabilities to floating-point accuracy, and, on the 1001-point grid, \(\omega(p) > p\) below a crossing near \(0.278\) and not above it. For the pair \(A\) and \(B\) of Example 2.1, \(B\) first-order dominates \(A\), yet the non-cumulative weighted value ranks \(A\) above \(B\) at \(\alpha = 1\) (about 42.87 against 39.13) and at \(\alpha = 1/2\) (about 4.88 against 3.91). Every nondecreasing utility in the checked family ranks \(B\) at or above \(A\).
"""E3 L07 lab 1: a probability weighting function, and a dominated pair that the weighted value ranks higher.
Amounts are in currency units (CU). Probabilities are exact fractions. The weighting function, the value of an
outcome and the weighted value are floats, printed to four decimals.
Part A: the weighting function omega(p) = p**eta / (p**eta + (1 - p)**eta)**(1/eta) at eta = 0.5. It prints the
endpoints omega(0) and omega(1) and the values at small and large probabilities beside p itself. On a grid of 1001
equally spaced probabilities it checks that omega is nondecreasing and lies in [0, 1], checks the closed form
sqrt(p) / (1 + 2 sqrt(p (1 - p))) against the formula, and prints the number of sign changes of omega(p) - p and the
last grid point with omega(p) > p.
Part B: two finite lotteries on gains, A and B. The distribution-function test of first-order dominance (the
test of Lesson 4) shows that B dominates A: its distribution function is at or below A's at every outcome,
with strict inequality somewhere.
Part C: the non-cumulative weighted value Gamma(L) = sum over outcomes of omega(p_i) * v(x_i), with v(x) = x**alpha
for gains, at alpha = 1 and alpha = 0.5. It prints Gamma(A) and Gamma(B) and which lottery is ranked higher.
Part D: a finite family of nondecreasing utilities (power functions and step functions). For each one it checks
whether the expected utility of B is at least that of A, prints any utility that ranks A above B, and prints the count.
Standard library only; no randomness and no network access.
"""
from __future__ import annotations
import itertools
import math
from collections.abc import Callable
from fractions import Fraction
ETA = 0.5
ALPHAS: tuple[float, ...] = (1.0, 0.5)
LOTTERY_A: tuple[tuple[int, Fraction], ...] = ((0, Fraction(2, 5)), (50, Fraction(3, 10)), (100, Fraction(3, 10)))
LOTTERY_B: tuple[tuple[int, Fraction], ...] = ((0, Fraction(2, 5)), (100, Fraction(3, 5)))
GRID_POINTS = 1001
def omega(p: float, eta: float = ETA) -> float:
"""Weight of a probability p in [0, 1]: p**eta / (p**eta + (1 - p)**eta)**(1/eta). Dimensionless."""
if not 0.0 <= p <= 1.0:
raise ValueError("a probability must lie in [0, 1]")
return float(p**eta / (p**eta + (1.0 - p) ** eta) ** (1.0 / eta))
def distribution_at(lottery: tuple[tuple[int, Fraction], ...], t: int) -> Fraction:
"""P(X <= t) for a finite lottery given as (outcome in CU, probability) pairs."""
return sum((p for x, p in lottery if x <= t), Fraction(0))
def dominates_fosd(b: tuple[tuple[int, Fraction], ...], a: tuple[tuple[int, Fraction], ...]) -> tuple[bool, bool]:
"""Return (B first-order dominates A, strictly somewhere): F_B(t) <= F_A(t) at every outcome t of either lottery."""
points = sorted({x for x, _ in a} | {x for x, _ in b})
at_or_below = all(distribution_at(b, t) <= distribution_at(a, t) for t in points)
strict = any(distribution_at(b, t) < distribution_at(a, t) for t in points)
return at_or_below, strict
def weighted_value(lottery: tuple[tuple[int, Fraction], ...], alpha: float) -> float:
"""Non-cumulative weighted value: sum of omega(p_i) * v(x_i), with v(x) = x**alpha for gains (x >= 0)."""
return float(sum(omega(float(p)) * float(x) ** alpha for x, p in lottery))
def expected_utility(lottery: tuple[tuple[int, Fraction], ...], u: Callable[[float], float]) -> float:
"""Expected utility sum of p_i * u(x_i) for a utility function u on outcomes in CU."""
return float(sum(float(p) * u(float(x)) for x, p in lottery))
def part_a() -> None:
print(f"part A: eta = {ETA:g}; omega(0) = {omega(0.0):.4f}; omega(1) = {omega(1.0):.4f}")
for p in (0.01, 0.05, 0.3, 0.5, 0.6, 0.95, 0.99):
side = "above p" if omega(p) > p else "below p"
print(f"part A: p = {p:<5g} omega(p) = {omega(p):.4f} ({side})")
grid = [i / (GRID_POINTS - 1) for i in range(GRID_POINTS)]
values = [omega(p) for p in grid]
nondecreasing = all(values[i] <= values[i + 1] for i in range(len(values) - 1))
in_unit = all(0.0 <= v <= 1.0 for v in values)
print(
f"part A: on {GRID_POINTS} equally spaced probabilities: nondecreasing = {nondecreasing}, in [0, 1] = {in_unit}"
)
closed_gap = max(abs(omega(p) - math.sqrt(p) / (1 + 2 * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p)))) for p in grid)
agrees = "agrees with" if closed_gap < 1e-12 else "differs from"
print(f"part A: closed form sqrt(p) / (1 + 2 sqrt(p (1 - p))) {agrees} the formula, largest gap {closed_gap:.2e}")
interior = grid[1:-1]
above = [omega(p) > p for p in interior]
changes = sum(1 for a, b in itertools.pairwise(above) if a != b)
above_points = [p for p in interior if omega(p) > p]
last_text = "none" if not above_points else f"{max(above_points):.3f}"
print(
f"part A: interior grid points: sign changes of omega(p) - p = {changes}; last point with omega(p) > p = "
f"{last_text}"
)
def part_b() -> None:
print("part B: distribution functions F(t) = P(X <= t) at the outcomes")
for t in (0, 50, 100):
print(
f"part B: t = {t:3d} F_A(t) = {float(distribution_at(LOTTERY_A, t)):.4f} "
f"F_B(t) = {float(distribution_at(LOTTERY_B, t)):.4f}"
)
at_or_below, strict = dominates_fosd(LOTTERY_B, LOTTERY_A)
print(f"part B: B first-order dominates A (F_B <= F_A everywhere) = {at_or_below}; strict somewhere = {strict}")
def part_c() -> None:
for alpha in ALPHAS:
va = weighted_value(LOTTERY_A, alpha)
vb = weighted_value(LOTTERY_B, alpha)
ranked = "A above B" if va > vb else "B at or above A"
print(f"part C: alpha = {alpha:g} Gamma(A) = {va:.4f} Gamma(B) = {vb:.4f} weighted ranking: {ranked}")
def power_utility(a: float) -> Callable[[float], float]:
"""Utility x**a on outcomes x >= 0; nondecreasing for a > 0."""
def u(x: float) -> float:
return float(x**a)
return u
def step_utility(t: float) -> Callable[[float], float]:
"""Indicator utility 1 if x >= t, else 0; nondecreasing in x."""
def u(x: float) -> float:
return 1.0 if x >= t else 0.0
return u
def part_d() -> None:
utilities: list[tuple[str, Callable[[float], float]]] = []
for a in (0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0):
utilities.append((f"x**{a:g}", power_utility(a)))
for t in (1.0, 50.0, 51.0, 100.0, 101.0):
utilities.append((f"indicator of x >= {t:g}", step_utility(t)))
agree = 0
for name, u in utilities:
eu_a = expected_utility(LOTTERY_A, u)
eu_b = expected_utility(LOTTERY_B, u)
if eu_b >= eu_a:
agree += 1
else:
print(f"part D: utility {name} ranks A above B")
print(f"part D: nondecreasing utilities with E u(B) >= E u(A): {agree} of {len(utilities)}")
def main() -> None:
part_a()
part_b()
part_c()
part_d()
main()

3Where the canon breaks
Expected utility is linear in the probabilities. The weighted value of Definition 1.2 is not. Take \(\omega = \omega_{1/2}\). The value of a single probability is \(\omega(p)\), and an affine function (a function \(a + bp\) for constants \(a\) and \(b\)) with \(\omega(0) = 0\) and \(\omega(1) = 1\) must be the identity (Problem R1). The example has \(\omega(1/2) = 0.35355339 \ne 1/2\). The reversal needs this non-linearity: Remark 1.3 gives expected utility as the identity weighting, and Lesson 4 (Theorem 1.5) shows that expected utility with a nondecreasing utility, on a finite set of outcomes, cannot reverse dominance.
One repair is cumulative: the weight is applied to the probability that the outcome is at least a level, and not to the probability of that outcome alone. This lesson does not derive that repair. Problem R2 shows only the step that a nondecreasing weight preserves the ordering of tail probabilities, which is what a cumulative construction needs. No cumulative value is computed here, and no magnitude of any weighting function is quoted for people. The one source the lesson cites, Kahneman and Tversky (1979), is used for its title only.
The non-cumulative value is a convenient form. It is not the only way to write a weighting, and nothing in the exhibit shows that people use it.
4Misconceptions
“A nondecreasing weighting function cannot rank a dominated lottery higher, because the weights respect the order of the probabilities.”
The weights respect the order of single probabilities, but not the order of lotteries. Proposition 2.2 gives a pair where \(\omega\) is nondecreasing, \(B\) dominates \(A\), and \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\). The two positive outcomes of \(A\) carry the weight \(2\,\omega(3/10) = 0.57158177\) (Remark 1.6), which exceeds the weight \(\omega(3/5) = 0.39125077\) of the single positive outcome of \(B\).
“The decision weights of a lottery's outcomes add up to one, so a weighted value is an average.”
Under \(\omega_{1/2}\) the two positive outcomes of \(A\) carry the weight \(2\,\omega(3/10) = 0.57158177\), and the identity weighting would give \(0.6\). The weights are not probabilities: over all three outcomes of \(A\) they sum to \(0.89103669\), not one (Remark 1.6).
“Overweighting small probabilities means every lottery with a small chance of a prize is valued above its expected value.”
The weight of a small probability is above the probability itself: \(\omega(0.01) = 0.0834\). But the rest of the lottery is weighted too. For \(L\) paying 100 CU with probability \(0.01\) and 50 CU with probability \(0.99\), the weighted value at \(\alpha = 1\) is \(\omega(0.01)\,100 + \omega(0.99)\,50 = 8.3403 + 41.4925 = 49.8328\), with the weights taken unrounded (to ten decimals, \(\omega(0.01) = 0.0834030105\), \(\omega(0.99) = 0.8298494764\)), while the expected value is \(0.01 \times 100 + 0.99 \times 50 = 50.5\). The lottery is valued below its expected value. The overweighting of \(0.01\) adds \((0.0834030105 - 0.01) \times 100 = 7.34\) CU, and the underweighting of \(0.99\) (Lab 1, part A, prints \(\omega(0.99) = 0.8298 < 0.99\)) subtracts \((0.99 - 0.8298494764) \times 50 = 8.01\) CU, so the net effect is \(-0.67\) CU. The counterexample is a computation from the closed form, graded T.
5Problem set
Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it.
Mechanics
M1 · The weight of one quarter
Compute \(\omega_{1/2}(1/4)\) from the closed form of Example 1.4, exactly.
Solution
With \(p = 1/4\), \(\sqrt{p} = 1/2\) and \(\sqrt{p(1 - p)} = \sqrt{3}/4\). So \(\omega(1/4) = (1/2) / (1 + \sqrt{3}/2) = 1/(2 + \sqrt{3})\). Multiplying the numerator and denominator by \(2 - \sqrt{3}\) gives \(2 - \sqrt{3} = 0.26794919\). Since \(0.2679 > 0.25\) and \(1/4\) lies below the crossing near \(0.278\), the weight exceeds the probability there, as Observation 1.5 reports on the grid.
M2 · Weighted value against expected value
A lottery pays 100 CU with probability \(1/2\) and 0 otherwise. Take \(v(x) = x\). Compute \(V_\omega\) under \(\omega_{1/2}\), and compare it with the expected value.
Solution
The positive outcome carries weight \(\omega(1/2) = 1/(2\sqrt{2}) = 0.35355339\), so \(V_\omega = 100 \times 0.35355339 = 35.355339\) CU. The expected value is \(50\) CU. The weighted value is below the expected value because \(\omega(1/2) < 1/2\), which is the underweighting of a middle probability (Lab 1, part A, prints \(\omega(0.5) = 0.3536 < 0.5\)).
M3 · The endpoints of the closed form
Show from the closed form of Example 1.4 that \(\omega_{1/2}(0) = 0\) and \(\omega_{1/2}(1) = 1\), and check the general formula at \(p = 1\).
Solution
At \(p = 0\) the numerator is \(\sqrt{0} = 0\) and the denominator is \(1 + 2\sqrt{0} = 1\), so \(\omega(0) = 0\). At \(p = 1\) the numerator is \(1\) and the denominator is \(1 + 2\sqrt{1 \cdot 0} = 1\), so \(\omega(1) = 1\). In the general formula the same values come from \(0^{\eta} = 0\) and \(1^{\eta} = 1\) with \(1 - p = 0\), which is \(0^{\eta} = 0\) for \(\eta > 0\).
Proof
P1 · The closed form for \(\eta = 1/2\)
Starting from \(\omega_\eta(p) = p^{\eta}/\bigl(p^{\eta} + (1 - p)^{\eta}\bigr)^{1/\eta}\), show that \(\omega_{1/2}(p) = \sqrt{p}/\bigl(1 + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)}\bigr)\) for \(0 < p < 1\).
Solution
At \(\eta = 1/2\), \(p^{\eta} = \sqrt{p}\) and \(1/\eta = 2\). So the denominator is \(\bigl(\sqrt{p} + \sqrt{1 - p}\bigr)^2 = p + (1 - p) + 2\sqrt{p}\sqrt{1 - p} = 1 + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)}\). Dividing the numerator \(\sqrt{p}\) by this denominator gives the closed form. The identity uses only \(p + (1 - p) = 1\) and \(\sqrt{p}\sqrt{1 - p} = \sqrt{p(1 - p)}\), both valid for \(0 \le p \le 1\).
P2 · The identity weighting gives the expected value
Prove Remark 1.3 for a lottery with three outcomes, from Definition 1.2 and no other fact.
Solution
Let \(L = \{(x_1, p_1), (x_2, p_2), (x_3, p_3)\}\). With \(\omega(p) = p\), Definition 1.2 gives \(V_\omega(L) = p_1 v(x_1) + p_2 v(x_2) + p_3 v(x_3)\). The expected value of \(v(X)\), with \(\Prob(X = x_i) = p_i\), is the same sum by the definition of expectation for a finite distribution. The two expressions are identical term by term, so the claim holds.
P3 · The threshold for the reversal
Show that \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) if and only if \(\omega(3/10)/\omega(3/5) > 1/(1 + 2^{-\alpha})\), for the pair of Example 2.1 and \(\alpha > 0\).
Solution
The outcome 0 has value \(v(0) = 0\), so it contributes nothing. Then \(V_\omega(A) = \omega(3/10)(50^{\alpha} + 100^{\alpha})\) and \(V_\omega(B) = \omega(3/5)\,100^{\alpha}\). Write \(50^{\alpha} = 2^{-\alpha}\,100^{\alpha}\). Then \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) is \(\omega(3/10)(1 + 2^{-\alpha}) 100^{\alpha} > \omega(3/5)\,100^{\alpha}\). Dividing by \(100^{\alpha} > 0\) and then by \(\omega(3/5) > 0\) gives the stated condition. The equivalence holds in both directions because each step divides by a positive number.

E3 · Predict the ranking at identity weighting

Research
R1 · Why the example is not linear in the probability
Show that an affine function \(\omega(p) = a + bp\) with \(\omega(0) = 0\) and \(\omega(1) = 1\) is the identity, and conclude that \(\omega_{1/2}\) is not affine.
Solution
\(\omega(0) = a = 0\) and \(\omega(1) = a + b = 1\), so \(a = 0\) and \(b = 1\), and \(\omega(p) = p\). The example has \(\omega_{1/2}(1/2) = 0.35355339\), which differs from \(1/2\). So \(\omega_{1/2}\) is not affine, and \(V_\omega\) is not a linear function of the probability vector. This is the non-linearity that expected utility lacks, and the reversal in Proposition 2.2 needs it.
R2 · Tail probabilities keep their order
For the pair of Example 2.1, let \(\mathrm{Tail}_L(x) = \Prob(X \ge x)\) be the tail probability. Show that \(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x) \ge \mathrm{Tail}_A(x)\) at \(x = 50\) and \(x = 100\), and that any nondecreasing \(\omega\) gives \(\omega(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x)) \ge \omega(\mathrm{Tail}_A(x))\).
Solution
At \(x = 50\), \(\mathrm{Tail}_A = 1 - F_A(49) = 1 - 2/5 = 3/5\), and \(\mathrm{Tail}_B = 1 - F_B(49) = 3/5\), so they are equal. At \(x = 100\), \(\mathrm{Tail}_A = 3/10\) and \(\mathrm{Tail}_B = 3/5\), so \(\mathrm{Tail}_B > \mathrm{Tail}_A\). Hence \(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x) \ge \mathrm{Tail}_A(x)\) at both outcomes. For a nondecreasing \(\omega\), \(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x) \ge \mathrm{Tail}_A(x)\) implies \(\omega(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x)) \ge \omega(\mathrm{Tail}_A(x))\), which is the step a cumulative construction needs. A full cumulative value is not defined here, and this solution does not show that it respects dominance in general.
R3 · A choice that separates the two forms
Design a choice between \(A\) and \(B\) of Example 2.1 whose outcome distinguishes the non-cumulative value of Definition 1.2 from a form that respects dominance. State the prediction of each form. No data are reported in this lesson.
Solution
Ask a decision maker to choose between \(A\) and \(B\) as stated, with the outcomes paid by a stated mechanism. Under the non-cumulative form at \(\alpha = 1\) and \(\omega_{1/2}\), the prediction is a choice of \(A\), because \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) (Proposition 2.2). Under any form that respects dominance (one that ranks \(B\) at or above \(A\) whenever \(B\) first-order dominates \(A\)), including expected utility with a nondecreasing utility (Lesson 4, Theorem 1.5), the prediction is a choice of \(B\) or indifference. A majority choice of \(A\) matches the prediction of the non-cumulative form and not the prediction of the dominance-respecting forms; a majority choice of \(B\) matches the dominance-respecting forms and not the non-cumulative form. Which prediction a real sample matches is the only comparison the design supports. The design says nothing about the size of any effect, and a real study would need its own sample and controls.
