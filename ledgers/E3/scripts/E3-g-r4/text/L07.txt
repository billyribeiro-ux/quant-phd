0014| Expected utility weights each outcome by its probability. This lesson keeps the outcome values, replaces each probability by a decision weight (Definition 1.1), and shows that the replacement can change which lottery ranks higher. This lesson defines a weighting function, applies it to the outcomes of a finite lottery one at a time, and gives a pair of lotteries on which the weighted value of Definition 1.2 ranks a first-order-dominated lottery above its dominator. Expected utility with a nondecreasing utility cannot do this on a finite set of outcomes (Lesson 4, Theorem 1.5). The lesson does not claim how people weight probabilities, and it quotes no magnitude for the weights.
0015| 1Weighting functions
0016| Expected utility weights each outcome by its probability. Here the probability of an outcome is first passed through a function, and only then multiplied by the value of the outcome. A finite lottery is a list of pairs \((x_i, p_i)\), with \(p_i \ge 0\) and \(\sum_i p_i = 1\). Outcomes are in currency units (CU). This lesson uses gains only, \(x_i \ge 0\), so the value function of Lesson 6 is \(v(x) = x^{\alpha}\) with \(v(0) = 0\).
0017| Definition 1.1 (weighting function)
0018| A weighting function is a map \(\omega : [0, 1] \to [0, 1]\) that is nondecreasing, with \(\omega(0) = 0\) and \(\omega(1) = 1\). The number \(\omega(p)\) is the decision weight of an outcome whose probability is \(p\).
0019| Definition 1.2 (non-cumulative weighted value)
0020| Let \(\omega\) be a weighting function and \(v\) a value function with \(v(0) = 0\). For a finite lottery \(L = \{(x_i, p_i)\}\) on gains, the probability weighting of \(L\) is \[ V_\omega(L) = \sum_i \omega(p_i)\, v(x_i). \] The word "non-cumulative" refers to the weights: each outcome's own probability is weighted, not the probability of reaching it or of doing at least as well. Expected utility is the case \(\omega(p) = p\) and \(v = u\), because then \(V_\omega(L) = \sum_i p_i u(x_i) = \E\,u(X)\).
0021| Remark 1.3 (the identity weighting)
0022| If \(\omega(p) = p\) for every \(p\), then \(V_\omega(L) = \sum_i p_i v(x_i)\) for every finite lottery \(L\). This is the expected value of \(v(X)\).
0023| Proof
0024| Substitute \(\omega(p_i) = p_i\) into Definition 1.2. The sum is the definition of the expected value of \(v(X)\), where \(X\) takes the value \(x_i\) with probability \(p_i\).
0025| The name prospect theory appears in the title of the 1979 paper by Kahneman and Tversky. This lesson does not reproduce that paper's weighting function, its estimates or its functional form, and it uses the non-cumulative value of Definition 1.2 as a mathematical object.
0026| Example 1.4 (a weighting function with \(\eta = 1/2\))
0027| Take the family \(\omega_\eta(p) = p^{\eta}/\bigl(p^{\eta} + (1 - p)^{\eta}\bigr)^{1/\eta}\) with \(\eta = 1/2\). Then \(\omega_{1/2}(0) = 0\) and \(\omega_{1/2}(1) = 1\). Because \((\sqrt{p} + \sqrt{1 - p})^2 = p + (1 - p) + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)} = 1 + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)}\), the closed form is \[ \omega(p) = \frac{\sqrt{p}}{1 + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)}}. \] The unrounded values used below are \(\omega(3/10) = 0.28579088\), \(\omega(3/5) = 0.39125077\) and \(\omega(1/2) = 1/(2\sqrt{2}) = 0.35355339\). Lab 1, part A, evaluates the general formula and the closed form on 1001 probabilities and reports a largest gap of \(3.33 \times 10^{-16}\). Values quoted to eight decimals on this page are rounded from unrounded values, so a quotient of the printed digits can differ in the last digit.
0028| Observation 1.5 (the shape of this example, on a grid)
0029| For \(\omega = \omega_{1/2}\), the values lie in \([0, 1]\), and \(\omega\) is nondecreasing on the 1001 equally spaced probabilities \(0, 0.001, \ldots, 1\). The difference \(\omega(p) - p\) changes sign once between the interior grid points, and the last grid point with \(\omega(p) > p\) is \(0.278\). The observation is a finite check, not a theorem: it holds on the grid, and beyond the grid it is a hypothesis. Lab 1, part A, reports each fact.
0030| Under this example the weight exceeds the probability at 0.01 and at 0.05, and it falls below the probability at 0.3, 0.5, 0.6, 0.95 and 0.99. The printed values from Lab 1, part A, are \(\omega(0.01) = 0.0834 > 0.01\), \(\omega(0.05) = 0.1557 > 0.05\), \(\omega(0.3) = 0.2858 < 0.3\), \(\omega(0.5) = 0.3536 < 0.5\), \(\omega(0.6) = 0.3913 < 0.6\), \(\omega(0.95) = 0.6788 < 0.95\), \(\omega(0.99) = 0.8298 < 0.99\). The rounding is to four decimals. Each comparison also holds for the unrounded values.
0031| Remark 1.6 (the weights of a lottery need not add up to one)
0032| Under the identity weighting the weights of a lottery's outcomes sum to \(\sum_i p_i = 1\). Under \(\omega_{1/2}\) they do not. Lottery \(A\) of Section 2 has three outcomes: 0 with probability \(2/5\), and 50 CU and 100 CU with probability \(3/10\) each. Its weights are \(\omega(2/5)\), \(\omega(3/10)\) and \(\omega(3/10)\), which sum to \(\omega(2/5) + 2\,\omega(3/10) = 0.31945492 + 0.57158177 = 0.89103669\) from unrounded values, not one. The two positive outcomes of \(A\) alone carry \(2\,\omega(3/10) = 0.57158177\). The single positive outcome of the lottery \(B\) of Section 2 has probability \(3/5\), and its weight is \(\omega(3/5) = 0.39125077\). This is the arithmetic behind the exhibit.
0033| 2Dominance, and a weighted ranking that reverses it
0034| Lesson 4 defines first-order stochastic dominance by distribution functions. For finite lotteries on the same outcomes, \(B\) first-order dominates \(A\) when \(F_B(t) \le F_A(t)\) for every outcome \(t\), with \(F(t) = \Prob(X \le t)\). For lotteries on one finite set of outcomes, Lesson 4 (Theorem 1.5) proves that expected utility with any nondecreasing \(u\) ranks \(B\) at or above \(A\) when \(B\) first-order dominates \(A\).

0054| Proposition 2.2 (a weighted ranking that reverses dominance)
0055| Assume:
0056| A1. the lotteries \(A\) and \(B\) of Example 2.1, with finite support on gains;
0057| A2. the weighting function \(\omega = \omega_{1/2}\) of Example 1.4;
0058| A3. the value function \(v(x) = x^{\alpha}\) for \(x \ge 0\), with \(\alpha > 0\) and \(v(0) = 0\).
0059| Then \(B\) first-order dominates \(A\), and \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) if and only if \[ \frac{\omega(3/10)}{\omega(3/5)} > \frac{1}{1 + 2^{-\alpha}}. \] The threshold is \(2/3\) at \(\alpha = 1\) and \(0.58578644\) at \(\alpha = 1/2\). The ratio is \(0.73045450\) from unrounded values, so \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) at both values of \(\alpha\).
0060| Proof
0061| Dominance is Example 2.1. For the weighted values, the outcome 0 contributes \(\omega(2/5)\,v(0) = 0\). So \(V_\omega(A) = \omega(3/10)\,(50^{\alpha} + 100^{\alpha})\) and \(V_\omega(B) = \omega(3/5)\,100^{\alpha}\). Since \(50^{\alpha} = 100^{\alpha} 2^{-\alpha}\), dividing both sides by \(100^{\alpha} > 0\) gives \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) if and only if \(\omega(3/10)(1 + 2^{-\alpha}) > \omega(3/5)\). Dividing by \(\omega(3/5) > 0\) gives the stated condition. At \(\alpha = 1\) the threshold is \(1/(1 + 1/2) = 2/3\). At \(\alpha = 1/2\) it is \(1/(1 + 2^{-1/2}) = 0.58578644\). The ratio \(\omega(3/10)/\omega(3/5)\) is computed from the unrounded closed form of Example 1.4, and it is \(0.73045450\) to eight decimals. The quotient of the eight-decimal values printed above, \(0.28579088 / 0.39125077\), is \(0.73045449\), so the last digit depends on the basis.
0062| Remark 2.3 (what the reversal does and does not show)
0063| Under the identity weighting, the same pair gives \(V(A) = 0.3 \times 150 = 45\) and \(V(B) = 0.6 \times 100 = 60\) at \(\alpha = 1\), so \(B\) ranks higher, as dominance requires. Holding the outcomes and the value function fixed, the change from the identity weighting to \(\omega\) reverses the ranking, so the reversal comes from the weights. It is a property of this form of the weighted value. It is not evidence about how people choose. The lesson takes no position on that question.
0064| Lab 1 · The weighting function, and the pair that the weighted value ranks against dominance
0065| Stated before running: the closed form of Example 1.4 equals the general formula on 1001 probabilities to floating-point accuracy, and, on the 1001-point grid, \(\omega(p) > p\) below a crossing near \(0.278\) and not above it. 0241| 3Where the canon breaks
0242| Expected utility is linear in the probabilities. The weighted value of Definition 1.2 is not. Take \(\omega = \omega_{1/2}\). The value of a single probability is \(\omega(p)\), and an affine function (a function \(a + bp\) for constants \(a\) and \(b\)) with \(\omega(0) = 0\) and \(\omega(1) = 1\) must be the identity (Problem R1). The example has \(\omega(1/2) = 0.35355339 \ne 1/2\). The reversal needs this non-linearity: Remark 1.3 gives expected utility as the identity weighting, and Lesson 4 (Theorem 1.5) shows that expected utility with a nondecreasing utility, on a finite set of outcomes, cannot reverse dominance.
0243| One repair is cumulative: the weight is applied to the probability that the outcome is at least a level, and not to the probability of that outcome alone. This lesson does not derive that repair. Problem R2 shows only the step that a nondecreasing weight preserves the ordering of tail probabilities, which is what a cumulative construction needs. No cumulative value is computed here, and no magnitude of any weighting function is quoted for people. The one source the lesson cites, Kahneman and Tversky (1979), is used for its title only.
0244| The non-cumulative value is a convenient form. It is not the only way to write a weighting, and nothing in the exhibit shows that people use it.
0245| 4Misconceptions
0246| “A nondecreasing weighting function cannot rank a dominated lottery higher, because the weights respect the order of the probabilities.”
0247| The weights respect the order of single probabilities, but not the order of lotteries. Proposition 2.2 gives a pair where \(\omega\) is nondecreasing, \(B\) dominates \(A\), and \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\). The two positive outcomes of \(A\) carry the weight \(2\,\omega(3/10) = 0.57158177\) (Remark 1.6), which exceeds the weight \(\omega(3/5) = 0.39125077\) of the single positive outcome of \(B\).
0248| “The decision weights of a lottery's outcomes add up to one, so a weighted value is an average.”
0249| Under \(\omega_{1/2}\) the two positive outcomes of \(A\) carry the weight \(2\,\omega(3/10) = 0.57158177\), and the identity weighting would give \(0.6\). The weights are not probabilities: over all three outcomes of \(A\) they sum to \(0.89103669\), not one (Remark 1.6).
0250| “Overweighting small probabilities means every lottery with a small chance of a prize is valued above its expected value.”
0251| The weight of a small probability is above the probability itself: \(\omega(0.01) = 0.0834\). But the rest of the lottery is weighted too. For \(L\) paying 100 CU with probability \(0.01\) and 50 CU with probability \(0.99\), the weighted value at \(\alpha = 1\) is \(\omega(0.01)\,100 + \omega(0.99)\,50 = 8.3403 + 41.4925 = 49.8328\), with the weights taken unrounded (\(\omega(0.01) = 0.08340301\), \(\omega(0.99) = 0.82984948\)), while the expected value is \(0.01 \times 100 + 0.99 \times 50 = 50.5\). The lottery is valued below its expected value. The overweighting of \(0.01\) adds \((0.08340301 - 0.01) \times 100 = 7.34\) CU, and the underweighting of \(0.99\) (Lab 1, part A, prints \(\omega(0.99) = 0.8298 < 0.99\)) subtracts \((0.99 - 0.82984948) \times 50 = 8.01\) CU, so the net effect is \(-0.67\) CU. The counterexample is a computation from the closed form, graded T.

0258| With \(p = 1/4\), \(\sqrt{p} = 1/2\) and \(\sqrt{p(1 - p)} = \sqrt{3}/4\). So \(\omega(1/4) = (1/2) / (1 + \sqrt{3}/2) = 1/(2 + \sqrt{3})\). Multiplying the numerator and denominator by \(2 - \sqrt{3}\) gives \(2 - \sqrt{3} = 0.26794919\). Since \(0.2679 > 0.25\) and \(1/4\) lies below the crossing near \(0.278\), the weight exceeds the probability there, as Observation 1.5 reports on the grid.
0259| M2 · Weighted value against expected value
0260| A lottery pays 100 CU with probability \(1/2\) and 0 otherwise. Take \(v(x) = x\). Compute \(V_\omega\) under \(\omega_{1/2}\), and compare it with the expected value.
0261| Solution
0262| The positive outcome carries weight \(\omega(1/2) = 1/(2\sqrt{2}) = 0.35355339\), so \(V_\omega = 100 \times 0.35355339 = 35.355339\) CU. The expected value is \(50\) CU. The weighted value is below the expected value because \(\omega(1/2) < 1/2\), which is the underweighting of a middle probability (Lab 1, part A, prints \(\omega(0.5) = 0.3536 < 0.5\)).
0263| M3 · The endpoints of the closed form
0264| Show from the closed form of Example 1.4 that \(\omega_{1/2}(0) = 0\) and \(\omega_{1/2}(1) = 1\), and check the general formula at \(p = 1\).
0265| Solution
0266| At \(p = 0\) the numerator is \(\sqrt{0} = 0\) and the denominator is \(1 + 2\sqrt{0} = 1\), so \(\omega(0) = 0\). At \(p = 1\) the numerator is \(1\) and the denominator is \(1 + 2\sqrt{1 \cdot 0} = 1\), so \(\omega(1) = 1\). In the general formula the same values come from \(0^{\eta} = 0\) and \(1^{\eta} = 1\) with \(1 - p = 0\), which is \(0^{\eta} = 0\) for \(\eta > 0\).

0268| P1 · The closed form for \(\eta = 1/2\)
0269| Starting from \(\omega_\eta(p) = p^{\eta}/\bigl(p^{\eta} + (1 - p)^{\eta}\bigr)^{1/\eta}\), show that \(\omega_{1/2}(p) = \sqrt{p}/\bigl(1 + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)}\bigr)\) for \(0 < p < 1\).
0270| Solution
0271| At \(\eta = 1/2\), \(p^{\eta} = \sqrt{p}\) and \(1/\eta = 2\). So the denominator is \(\bigl(\sqrt{p} + \sqrt{1 - p}\bigr)^2 = p + (1 - p) + 2\sqrt{p}\sqrt{1 - p} = 1 + 2\sqrt{p(1 - p)}\). Dividing the numerator \(\sqrt{p}\) by this denominator gives the closed form. The identity uses only \(p + (1 - p) = 1\) and \(\sqrt{p}\sqrt{1 - p} = \sqrt{p(1 - p)}\), both valid for \(0 \le p \le 1\).
0272| P2 · The identity weighting gives the expected value
0273| Prove Remark 1.3 for a lottery with three outcomes, from Definition 1.2 and no other fact.
0274| Solution
0275| Let \(L = \{(x_1, p_1), (x_2, p_2), (x_3, p_3)\}\). With \(\omega(p) = p\), Definition 1.2 gives \(V_\omega(L) = p_1 v(x_1) + p_2 v(x_2) + p_3 v(x_3)\). The expected value of \(v(X)\), with \(\Prob(X = x_i) = p_i\), is the same sum by the definition of expectation for a finite distribution. The two expressions are identical term by term, so the claim holds.
0276| P3 · The threshold for the reversal
0277| Show that \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) if and only if \(\omega(3/10)/\omega(3/5) > 1/(1 + 2^{-\alpha})\), for the pair of Example 2.1 and \(\alpha > 0\).
0278| Solution
0279| The outcome 0 has value \(v(0) = 0\), so it contributes nothing. Then \(V_\omega(A) = \omega(3/10)(50^{\alpha} + 100^{\alpha})\) and \(V_\omega(B) = \omega(3/5)\,100^{\alpha}\). Write \(50^{\alpha} = 2^{-\alpha}\,100^{\alpha}\). Then \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) is \(\omega(3/10)(1 + 2^{-\alpha}) 100^{\alpha} > \omega(3/5)\,100^{\alpha}\). Dividing by \(100^{\alpha} > 0\) and then by \(\omega(3/5) > 0\) gives the stated condition. The equivalence holds in both directions because each step divides by a positive number.

0294| R1 · Why the example is not linear in the probability
0295| Show that an affine function \(\omega(p) = a + bp\) with \(\omega(0) = 0\) and \(\omega(1) = 1\) is the identity, and conclude that \(\omega_{1/2}\) is not affine.
0296| Solution
0297| \(\omega(0) = a = 0\) and \(\omega(1) = a + b = 1\), so \(a = 0\) and \(b = 1\), and \(\omega(p) = p\). The example has \(\omega_{1/2}(1/2) = 0.35355339\), which differs from \(1/2\). So \(\omega_{1/2}\) is not affine, and \(V_\omega\) is not a linear function of the probability vector. This is the non-linearity that expected utility lacks, and the reversal in Proposition 2.2 needs it.
0298| R2 · Tail probabilities keep their order
0299| For the pair of Example 2.1, let \(\mathrm{Tail}_L(x) = \Prob(X \ge x)\) be the tail probability. Show that \(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x) \ge \mathrm{Tail}_A(x)\) at \(x = 50\) and \(x = 100\), and that any nondecreasing \(\omega\) gives \(\omega(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x)) \ge \omega(\mathrm{Tail}_A(x))\).
0300| Solution
0301| At \(x = 50\), \(\mathrm{Tail}_A = 1 - F_A(49) = 1 - 2/5 = 3/5\), and \(\mathrm{Tail}_B = 1 - F_B(49) = 3/5\), so they are equal. At \(x = 100\), \(\mathrm{Tail}_A = 3/10\) and \(\mathrm{Tail}_B = 3/5\), so \(\mathrm{Tail}_B > \mathrm{Tail}_A\). Hence \(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x) \ge \mathrm{Tail}_A(x)\) at both outcomes. For a nondecreasing \(\omega\), \(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x) \ge \mathrm{Tail}_A(x)\) implies \(\omega(\mathrm{Tail}_B(x)) \ge \omega(\mathrm{Tail}_A(x))\), which is the step a cumulative construction needs. A full cumulative value is not defined here, and this solution does not show that it respects dominance in general.
0302| R3 · A choice that separates the two forms
0303| Design a choice between \(A\) and \(B\) of Example 2.1 whose outcome distinguishes the non-cumulative value of Definition 1.2 from a form that respects dominance. State the prediction of each form. No data are reported in this lesson.
0304| Solution
0305| Ask a decision maker to choose between \(A\) and \(B\) as stated, with the outcomes paid by a stated mechanism. Under the non-cumulative form at \(\alpha = 1\) and \(\omega_{1/2}\), the prediction is a choice of \(A\), because \(V_\omega(A) > V_\omega(B)\) (Proposition 2.2). Under any form that respects dominance, including expected utility with a nondecreasing utility, the prediction is a choice of \(B\) or indifference, by Lesson 4. A majority choice of \(A\) would be evidence for the non-cumulative form over the dominance-respecting forms, and a majority choice of \(B\) would count against it. The design says nothing about the size of any effect, and a real study would need its own sample and controls.
