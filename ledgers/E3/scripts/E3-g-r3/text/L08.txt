0014| Expected utility needs one probability for each outcome. When the probability of an outcome is itself unknown, a model can rank bets by a set of probabilities instead. This lesson defines maxmin expected utility over a prior set, defines ambiguity aversion as a certainty equivalent below the average-prior one, and compares four models on one gamble: expected utility, prospect value, two-date Epstein–Zin preferences and maxmin. The comparison shows where the models differ and where they agree. The Epstein–Zin row equals the expected-utility row, for a reason the lesson proves. The parameters are stated, and no estimate is quoted.
0015| 1Prior sets and the maxmin rule
0016| A gamble \(f\) pays a finite list of outcomes in cents, and each outcome has a probability. When the probability of an outcome is unknown, the decision maker holds a set of candidate probabilities. The candidates are not weighted here, so the set is the whole model of the uncertainty.
0017| Definition 1.1 (prior set)
0018| A prior set \(\mathcal{P}\) is a finite nonempty set of probability distributions over the outcomes of the gamble. For the two-outcome gambles of this lesson, a prior is a number \(p\) in \([0, 1]\), the probability of the first outcome.
0019| Definition 1.2 (maxmin and alpha-MEU)
0020| Let \(u\) be a utility function on final wealth, \(\mathcal{P}\) a prior set, and \(\tau \in [0, 1]\). The weight is written \(\tau\), not \(\alpha\), because \(\alpha\) is the gains exponent of Lesson 6. The alpha-MEU value of \(f\) is \[ V_\tau(f) = \tau \min_{P \in \mathcal{P}} \E_P\, u(f) + (1 - \tau) \max_{P \in \mathcal{P}} \E_P\, u(f). \] The maxmin expected utility value is \(V_1(f) = \min_{P \in \mathcal{P}} \E_P\, u(f)\). The certainty equivalent of \(f\) is \(\mathrm{CE}(f) = u^{-1}(V_\tau(f))\), the sure wealth that has the same utility. The name maxmin expected utility appears in the title of the 1989 Gilboa and Schmeidler paper; this lesson uses the rule as defined here and does not reproduce the paper's axioms or results.
0021| Proposition 1.3 (a one-element prior set gives expected utility)
0022| Assume:
0023| A1. \(\mathcal{P} = \{P\}\) is a single prior;
0024| A2. \(\tau \in [0, 1]\).
0025| Then \(V_\tau(f) = \E_P\, u(f)\), and the certainty equivalent is the expected-utility certainty equivalent under \(P\).
0026| Proof
0027| With one prior the minimum and the maximum over \(\mathcal{P}\) are both \(\E_P u(f)\), so \(V_\tau(f) = \tau \E_P u(f) + (1 - \tau)\E_P u(f) = \E_P u(f)\). Applying \(u^{-1}\) gives the certainty equivalent.
0028| Example 1.4 (the ambiguous urn and its average prior)
0029| An urn holds 90 balls, each red or black. The number \(k\) of red balls is unknown, and the prior set is \(\mathcal{P} = \{P_k : k = 30, 31, \ldots, 60\}\), with \(P_k(\text{red}) = k/90\). The uniform average of the set is \(\bar P\), with \(\bar P(\text{red}) = \frac{1}{31}\sum_{k=30}^{60} k/90 = \frac{45}{90} = \frac{1}{2}\). Each of the three models of Section 4 that takes one probability uses \(\bar P(\text{red}) = 1/2\).
0030| 2Ambiguity aversion
0031| The gamble of Section 4 is evaluated under the average prior and under the worst prior of Example 1.4. Ambiguity aversion compares these two evaluations.
0032| Definition 2.1 (ambiguity aversion)
0033| Let \(\bar P\) be the uniform average of a finite prior set \(\mathcal{P}\), and let \(\mathrm{CE}_{\bar P}(f) = u^{-1}(\E_{\bar P} u(f))\) be the certainty equivalent under \(\bar P\). A decision rule is ambiguity averse at \(f\) if \(\mathrm{CE}(f) \le \mathrm{CE}_{\bar P}(f)\). It is ambiguity neutral at \(f\) if equality holds.
0034| Theorem 2.2 (maxmin is ambiguity averse)
0035| Assume:
0036| A1. \(\mathcal{P}\) is finite and nonempty, and \(\bar P\) is its uniform average;
0037| A2. \(u\) is strictly increasing on the range of final wealth, so \(u^{-1}\) is defined and strictly increasing on the range of values;
0038| A3. \(f\) has finitely many outcomes.
0039| Then \(\mathrm{CE}_{\text{maxmin}}(f) \le \mathrm{CE}_{\bar P}(f)\) for every such \(f\).
0040| Proof
0041| Let \(P_1, \ldots, P_n\) be the priors and \(m = \min_i \E_{P_i} u(f)\). The average prior gives \(\E_{\bar P} u(f) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \E_{P_i} u(f)\), which is an average of \(n\) numbers each at least \(m\). So \(\E_{\bar P} u(f) \ge m\). Since \(u^{-1}\) is strictly increasing (A2), \(u^{-1}(m) \le u^{-1}(\E_{\bar P} u(f))\). The left side is \(\mathrm{CE}_{\text{maxmin}}(f)\) and the right side is \(\mathrm{CE}_{\bar P}(f)\). Strict case: if the numbers \(\E_{P_i} u(f)\) are not all equal, at least one of them exceeds \(m\), so their average \(\E_{\bar P} u(f)\) is strictly greater than \(m\), and the inequality is strict, because \(u^{-1}\) is strictly increasing.
0042| Remark 2.3 (the inequality needs a prior set with spread)
0043| By Proposition 1.3, a one-element prior set gives equality in Theorem 2.2, so it is ambiguity neutral. The inequality is strict when the priors give different expected utilities at \(f\), by the strict case of the proof of Theorem 2.2. In the gamble of Section 4, the priors \(1/3\) and \(2/3\) give different expected utilities, and the maxmin certainty equivalent \(99523.8095\) cents is below the average-prior certainty equivalent \(101951.2195\) cents.
0044| Proposition 2.4 (the worst prior depends on the bet)
0045| Let an urn hold 90 balls, each red or black, with \(k\) red balls unknown. Assume:
0046| A1. the prior set is \(\{P_k : k = 0, 1, \ldots, 90\}\), where \(P_k(\text{red}) = k/90\);
0047| A2. \(u\) is strictly increasing on the range of final wealth;
0048| A3. a bet on red pays a prize: final wealth is \(W_{\text{red}}\) if the ball is red and \(W_{\text{no}}\) otherwise, with \(W_{\text{red}} > W_{\text{no}}\). A bet on black pays the same prize if the ball is black, so final wealth is \(W_{\text{red}}\) if the ball is black and \(W_{\text{no}}\) otherwise.
0049| Then the worst prior for the bet on red is \(P_0\), with no red balls. For the bet on black it is \(P_{90}\), with no black balls. So one prior is not the worst for both bets.
0050| Proof
0051| For the bet on red, under a prior with \(P(\text{red}) = p\), the expected utility is \(\E_p u(f) = u(W_{\text{no}}) + p\,\bigl(u(W_{\text{red}}) - u(W_{\text{no}})\bigr)\). The bracket is positive by A2 and A3, so this is increasing in \(p\). Its minimum over the prior set is therefore at the smallest \(p\), which is \(p = 0\) under A1 (that is \(P_0\)). For the bet on black the probability of black is \(q = 1 - p\); the same argument applies to \(q\), and the minimum is at \(q = 0\), that is \(p = 1\), which is \(P_{90}\).
0052| Example 2.5 (a known urn against the ambiguous urn)
0053| A known urn holds 30 red and 60 black balls, so its red probability is \(1/3\) and its black probability is \(2/3\). The bet pays 10000 cents (100 CU) on the stated colour and 0 otherwise, so final wealth is 110000 cents or 100000 cents. Take \(u(W) = -1/W\) (CRRA with \(\gamma = 2\), as in Section 4). With it, the known urn's CE for red is \(103125\) cents, and its CE for black is \(106451.6129\) cents. Under maxmin over the full set \(\{0, \ldots, 90\}\) of red counts, the ambiguous urn's CE is \(100000\) cents for red and \(100000\) cents for black, because the worst prior gives no red and no black ball respectively (Proposition 2.4). The numbers depend on this choice of \(u\), but the preference for the known urn does not. For any strictly increasing \(u\), the worst prior for red, \(P_0\), and the worst prior for black, \(P_{90}\), each give final wealth 100000 cents surely, so the ambiguous CE is 100000 cents for both colours. The known urn's expected utility is \(\tfrac13 u(110000) + \tfrac23 u(100000)\) for red and \(\tfrac23 u(110000) + \tfrac13 u(100000)\) for black, and each exceeds \(u(100000)\), so the known urn's CE exceeds 100000 cents for both colours. So the model prefers the known urn for both colours.
0054| The registry title of the 1961 paper by Ellsberg contains the word ambiguity. The lesson did not read the 1961 paper, so its design and results are not attributed here. The numbers above are the lesson's own, and they describe the model only.
0055| 3Epstein–Zin preferences with a stated two-date timing
0056| Epstein–Zin preferences separate the elasticity of intertemporal substitution \(\psi\) (Lesson 5) from the risk-aversion coefficient \(\gamma\). A gamble has to be placed in time before its value can be compared. This lesson fixes one convention, stated here, and uses it for the one-shot gamble of Section 4.
0057| Definition 3.1 (the two-date value and the timing convention)
0058| Let \(a = 1 - 1/\psi\), with \(\psi > 0\) and \(\psi \ne 1\), and let \(\gamma \ne 1\) and \(\beta \in (0, 1)\). Date 0 consumes a sure amount \(c_0 > 0\). Date 1 consumes \(c_1 > 0\), which may be random. The two-date Epstein–Zin value is \[ V_0 = \Bigl[(1 - \beta)\, c_0^{a} + \beta\,\bigl(\E\, c_1^{1 - \gamma}\bigr)^{a/(1 - \gamma)}\Bigr]^{1/a}. \] Timing convention: the gamble pays at date 1 only. The certainty equivalent of a gamble is the sure date-1 amount \(c\) for which the sure plan \((c_0, c)\) has the same \(V_0\) as the gamble.
0059| Proposition 3.2 (a gamble at the last date gives the CRRA certainty equivalent)
0060| Under Definition 3.1, assume:
0061| A1. \(\psi > 0\) and \(\psi \ne 1\), so that \(a \ne 0\);
0062| A2. \(\gamma \ne 1\);
0063| A3. \(\beta \in (0, 1)\);
0064| A4. \(c_0 > 0\);
0065| A5. the date-1 gamble has finite support, and every outcome \(c_1\) is positive.
0066| Then the Epstein–Zin certainty equivalent equals \((\E\, c_1^{1-\gamma})^{1/(1-\gamma)}\), the CRRA certainty equivalent with the same \(\gamma\). The formula contains neither \(\psi\), nor \(\beta\), nor \(c_0\).
0067| Proof
0068| Let \(c\) be the sure date-1 amount. The sure plan has \(V_0 = [(1 - \beta)c_0^{a} + \beta c^{a}]^{1/a}\), because \(\E\, c^{1-\gamma} = c^{1-\gamma}\) and \((c^{1-\gamma})^{a/(1-\gamma)} = c^{a}\). The gamble has \(V_0 = [(1 - \beta)c_0^{a} + \beta K]^{1/a}\) with \(K = (\E\, c_1^{1-\gamma})^{a/(1-\gamma)}\). Since \(a \ne 0\) and both bases are positive, the map \(x \mapsto x^{1/a}\) is injective on positive numbers. Equal values therefore require \(\beta c^{a} = \beta K\), and \(\beta > 0\) gives \(c^{a} = K\). Taking the \(a\)-th root, which is injective on positive numbers because \(a \ne 0\), gives \(c = (\E\, c_1^{1-\gamma})^{1/(1-\gamma)}\). This is the CRRA certainty equivalent, and it does not involve \(\psi\), \(\beta\) or \(c_0\).
0069| Remark 3.3 (what the convention leaves out)
0070| Proposition 3.2 covers a gamble that resolves at the last date. A gamble that resolves earlier, and changes a later consumption, has a random continuation value. Its certainty equivalent can then depend on \(\psi\) and \(\beta\), as a hypothesis the lesson does not test; Problem R1 names the step that fails, and the lesson does not compute the number. The convention is a choice, and the four-model comparison below uses it for the Epstein–Zin row.

0374| For a two-outcome gamble, \(\E_P[1/W] = p/W_1 + (1-p)/W_L\) is affine in \(p\). Let \(p_{\min}\) and \(p_{\max}\) be the smallest and largest priors. 0376| 5Where the canon breaks
0377| The prior set is a modelling choice. Maxmin depends on the endpoints of the set. With the full set of red counts from 0 to 90, the worst prior for this gamble gives \(P(\text{red}) = 0\), and the certainty equivalent is \(W_L = 95000\) cents. With the set from 30 to 60 it is \(99523.8095\) cents. The numbers of Section 4 are for the set of Example 1.4.
0378| Epstein–Zin adds nothing for a gamble at the last date. Proposition 3.2 makes the Epstein–Zin row equal to the CRRA row for every \(\psi\) and \(\beta\). The certainty equivalent that Proposition 3.2 gives contains neither \(\psi\) nor \(\beta\). Remark 3.3 names the case, a gamble resolved before a later consumption date, where they can enter; this lesson does not compute it.
0379| The magnitudes are parameters, not estimates. The prospect model uses \(\lambda = 2\) and exponents \(1/2\), and the CRRA model uses \(\gamma = 2\). Each is stated for the example. The lesson quotes no published estimate of loss aversion or of any risk-aversion coefficient. Its two cited sources are used for their titles only, and the lesson did not read them.
0380| The alpha-MEU row is one point of a family. The lesson computes \(\tau = 1\) and \(\tau = 1/2\). Its coincidence with expected utility at \(\tau = 1/2\) holds because \(g\) is affine and the midpoint of the extreme priors equals the average prior, both \(1/2\) here (Remark 4.2).
0381| 6Misconceptions
0382| “A maxmin agent applies one pessimistic prior to every gamble.”
0383| The worst prior depends on the gamble. Over the full set of red counts 0 to 90, the worst prior for a bet on red is the prior with no red balls, and for a bet on black it is the prior with no black balls (Proposition 2.4). The same decision maker evaluates two bets with two different worst priors, so no single prior summarises the rule.
0384| “A prior set with one element is ambiguity averse, because maxmin is a worst-case rule.”
0385| With one prior the worst case is the only case. Proposition 1.3 gives expected utility, and Remark 2.3 notes that the comparison is an equality, so the model is ambiguity neutral.
0386| “Epstein–Zin preferences give a different certainty equivalent from CRRA for every gamble, because they separate risk aversion from the elasticity of substitution.”
0387| For a gamble at the last date the two certainty equivalents are equal for every \(\psi\) and \(\beta\) (Proposition 3.2). Lab 1, part B, prints gaps from \(0.00\) to \(-2.91 \times 10^{-11}\) cents over the six pairs, all below \(10^{-6}\) cents. The gaps are of the size of floating-point rounding in the powers and in the bisection, which runs to floating-point resolution. The lesson's convention fixes the dates, and Remark 3.3 names the case where the convention leaves the answer open.

0391| M1 · Expected utility at one quarter
0392| For the gamble of Section 4, with final wealth \(110000\) cents if red and \(95000\) cents otherwise, compute the expected-utility certainty equivalent under \(P(\text{red}) = 1/4\), for \(\gamma = 2\).

0396| For the prior set \(\{1/4, 3/4\}\) and the same gamble, find the maxmin certainty equivalent in cents.
0397| Solution

0399| M3 · Optimistic weighting over the same two priors

0403| Proof
0404| P1 · The one-element prior set
0405| Prove Proposition 1.3 for every \(\tau \in [0, 1]\), using only Definition 1.2.
0406| Solution
0407| With \(\mathcal{P} = \{P\}\), the minimum and the maximum of \(\E_P u(f)\) over \(\mathcal{P}\) are both \(\E_P u(f)\). Substituting into Definition 1.2 gives \(V_\tau(f) = \tau \E_P u(f) + (1 - \tau)\E_P u(f) = \E_P u(f)\), because the two weights sum to one. The certainty equivalent is then \(u^{-1}(\E_P u(f))\), the expected-utility value.
0408| P2 · Averages of priors do not raise the maxmin value
0409| Prove Theorem 2.2 for the case of two priors \(P_1, P_2\) and a mixture \(\theta P_1 + (1 - \theta) P_2\), with \(\theta \in [0, 1]\), in place of the uniform average.
0410| Solution
0411| The mixture gives \(\E_{\theta P_1 + (1 - \theta)P_2} u(f) = \theta \E_{P_1} u(f) + (1 - \theta)\E_{P_2} u(f)\), a weighted average of two numbers with nonnegative weights summing to one. A weighted average of two numbers with nonnegative weights summing to one is at least their minimum \(m\). So the mixture's expected utility is at least \(m\), and applying the strictly increasing \(u^{-1}\) gives \(\mathrm{CE}_{\text{maxmin}}(f) \le u^{-1}(\text{mixture value})\), as in the proof of Theorem 2.2.
0412| P3 · Alpha-MEU at the midpoint
0413| Let \(\mathcal{P} = \{p_{\min}, p_{\max}\}\) for a two-outcome gamble. Show that the alpha-MEU certainty equivalent at \(\tau = 1/2\) equals the expected-utility certainty equivalent at \((p_{\min} + p_{\max})/2\), for \(u(W) = -1/W\).
0414| Solution
0415| Write \(g(p) = \E_p[1/W] = p/W_1 + (1-p)/W_L\), which is affine in \(p\). With \(u(W) = -1/W\), the alpha-MEU certainty equivalent is \(1/(\tau \max_P g(P) + (1-\tau)\min_P g(P))\). At \(\tau = 1/2\) the denominator is \(\tfrac{1}{2}g(p_{\min}) + \tfrac{1}{2}g(p_{\max})\) (since \(W_1 > W_L\) makes \(g\) decreasing, \(\max_P g = g(p_{\min})\) and \(\min_P g = g(p_{\max})\)). Affinity gives \(\tfrac{1}{2}g(p_{\min}) + \tfrac{1}{2}g(p_{\max}) = g\bigl((p_{\min} + p_{\max})/2\bigr)\). So the CE equals \(1/g\) at the midpoint, which is the expected-utility CE there.

0430| R1 · The step that fails for an earlier-resolving gamble
0431| Name the step in the proof of Proposition 3.2 that fails when the gamble resolves at date 1 and changes date-2 consumption, and say on what the certainty equivalent then depends.
0432| Solution
0433| The proof uses that the date-1 value is the consumption \(c_1\) itself, so the expectation is over \(c_1^{1-\gamma}\). That step fails when a later consumption \(c_2\) enters: the date-1 value becomes \(Z_1 = [(1-\beta)c_1^{a} + \beta c_2^{a}]^{1/a}\), which is random when \(c_1\) is random, so the expectation must be taken over \(Z_1^{1-\gamma}\). Whether the certainty equivalent then depends on \(\psi\) and \(\beta\) is the untested hypothesis of Remark 3.3; this solution does not compute it.
