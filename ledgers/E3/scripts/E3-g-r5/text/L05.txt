Under CRRA expected utility, one curvature parameter sets both how much a person dislikes risk and how readily that person trades consumption today for consumption later. Remark 3.4 derives the second of these for CRRA expected utility, where it equals 1/γ for γ ≠ 0. Recursive preferences, defined in Section 1, give the second trade-off its own parameter. This lesson defines the two-period form, shows that the date-1 lottery enters the value only through its certainty equivalent, computes the elasticity of intertemporal substitution from the value function, and identifies the parameter restriction ψ = 1/γ under which the form is a power transform of CRRA expected utility and ranks plans the same way (Corollary 3.3).

1A two-period plan and its Epstein–Zin value

A two-period plan gives consumption \(c_0 > 0\) at date 0. At date 1 it gives a finite lottery: states \(s = 1, \dots, J\) occur with probabilities \(p_s > 0\), \(\sum_s p_s = 1\), and consumption \(c_{1,s} > 0\) in state \(s\). We write \(c_1\) for the date-1 lottery and \(\E\,g(c_1) = \sum_s p_s\, g(c_{1,s})\) for the expectation of a function of it. Four standing assumptions hold throughout Sections 1 to 3:

A1: \(0 < \beta < 1\), where \(\beta\) is the discount factor, the number that the M1 concept discount factor names.

A2: \(\psi > 0\) and \(\psi \ne 1\). Definition 2.1 in Section 2 defines the elasticity of intertemporal substitution (EIS), and Proposition 2.2 shows that it equals \(\psi\).

A3: \(\gamma \ne 1\). The parameter \(\gamma\) is the risk-aversion parameter of the CRRA power form of Lesson L03.

A4: every consumption level in the plan is strictly positive, so every power below is of a positive number.

Definition 1.1 (Epstein–Zin value of a two-period plan)
Let \(a := 1 - 1/\psi\), which is nonzero by A2. The Epstein–Zin recursive value of the plan is

\[V_0 = \Bigl[(1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,a} + \beta\,\bigl(\E\, V_1^{\,1-\gamma}\bigr)^{a/(1-\gamma)}\Bigr]^{1/a}, \qquad V_1 := c_1.\]

The date-1 value is the consumption itself, a terminal convention. The recursion has one step, so it is a two-period recursion. Lesson L08 states the timing convention that a one-shot gamble needs, and Section 4 names the limits of this form.

Remark 1.2 (the period-1 certainty equivalent)
Let \(\mathrm{CE}_1 := \bigl(\E\, c_1^{\,1-\gamma}\bigr)^{1/(1-\gamma)}\). This is the certainty equivalent (Lesson L02) of the date-1 lottery under the CRRA utility of Lesson L03: the sure amount \(c\) with \(c^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma) = \E\, c_1^{1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)\). By A4 it is strictly positive. Its defining identity, which the proofs use, is \(\mathrm{CE}_1^{\,1-\gamma} = \E\, c_1^{\,1-\gamma}\).

Theorem 1.3 (the date-1 lottery enters through its certainty equivalent)
Assume A1 to A4. Then

(a) \(V_0 = \bigl[(1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,a} + \beta\, \mathrm{CE}_1^{\,a}\bigr]^{1/a}\), and

(b) for fixed \(c_0\), \(\beta\), \(\gamma\) and \(\psi\), \(V_0\) is strictly increasing in \(\mathrm{CE}_1\).

Proof
(a) By Remark 1.2, \(\mathrm{CE}_1^{\,1-\gamma} = \E\, c_1^{\,1-\gamma}\). Raising both sides to the power \(a/(1-\gamma)\), which is defined because A3 gives \(1-\gamma \ne 0\), gives \(\bigl(\E\, c_1^{\,1-\gamma}\bigr)^{a/(1-\gamma)} = \mathrm{CE}_1^{\,a}\). Substituting \(V_1 = c_1\) into Definition 1.1 gives the formula in (a).

(b) Let \(H(x) := (1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,a} + \beta\, x^{a}\) for \(x > 0\). Then \(H(x) > 0\) by A1 and A4. If \(a > 0\), then \(x \mapsto x^{a}\) is strictly increasing, so \(H\) is strictly increasing, and \(t \mapsto t^{1/a}\) is strictly increasing on \((0, \infty)\). If \(a < 0\), then \(x \mapsto x^{a}\) is strictly decreasing, so \(H\) is strictly decreasing, and \(t \mapsto t^{1/a}\) is strictly decreasing on \((0, \infty)\), because \(1/a < 0\). A composition of two decreasing functions is increasing. In both cases \(V_0 = H(\mathrm{CE}_1)^{1/a}\) is strictly increasing in \(\mathrm{CE}_1\). \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 1.4 (the risky date-1 plan is worth its certainty-equivalent plan)
Assume A1 to A4. Let \(c_0\) be given. The Epstein–Zin value of the plan with the risky date-1 lottery \(c_1\) equals the value of the sure plan whose date-1 consumption is the number \(\mathrm{CE}_1\).

Proof
For a sure date-1 consumption \(k > 0\), the lottery is a single state with probability 1, so \(\mathrm{CE}_1 = (k^{1-\gamma})^{1/(1-\gamma)} = k\). Take \(k = \mathrm{CE}_1\) of the risky plan. Both plans have the same \(c_0\) and the same \(\mathrm{CE}_1\), so Theorem 1.3(a) gives the same value for both. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 1.5 (what the period-1 risk does)
By Theorem 1.3(b), a riskier date-1 lottery with a lower certainty equivalent has a lower value at fixed \(c_0\). By Theorem 1.3(a), the period-1 risk therefore acts only through \(\mathrm{CE}_1\), and the lottery enters only through \(\mathrm{CE}_1\), which depends on the whole distribution of \(c_1\), not only on its mean; so two lotteries with the same mean can have different values. For example, the lotteries that give \(c_1 = 1\) or \(4\) with probability \(1/2\) each, and \(c_1 = 2\) or \(3\) with probability \(1/2\) each, both have mean \(5/2\), but at \(\gamma = 2\) their certainty equivalents are \(8/5\) and \(12/5\) (the first from \(\mathrm{CE}_1^{-1} = \E\, c_1^{-1} = 5/8\), the second from \(5/12\)). Lab 1 shows one case: the mean of the lottery is \(5/2\), its certainty equivalent is \(8/5\), and the sure plan at \(8/5\) has the same value as the risky plan.

2The elasticity of intertemporal substitution

Definition 2.1 (marginal rate of substitution and the elasticity of intertemporal substitution)
Under A1 to A4, consider deterministic plans, so the date-1 consumption \(c_1 > 0\) is a number. By Theorem 1.3 with \(\mathrm{CE}_1 = c_1\), the value \(V_0\) is a differentiable function of \((c_0, c_1)\) on the positive quadrant. Its marginal rate of substitution is \(\mathrm{MRS}(c_0, c_1) := (\partial V_0/\partial c_0)/(\partial V_0/\partial c_1)\). The elasticity of intertemporal substitution (EIS) is \(\varepsilon := d\ln(c_1/c_0)\big/ d\ln \mathrm{MRS}\), the elasticity of the consumption ratio with respect to the marginal rate of substitution.

Proposition 2.2 (the EIS of a two-period plan is ψ)
Assume A1 to A4 for deterministic plans. Then \(\mathrm{MRS}(c_0, c_1) = \dfrac{1-\beta}{\beta}\Bigl(\dfrac{c_1}{c_0}\Bigr)^{1/\psi}\), and the EIS equals \(\psi\).

Proof
Write \(K := (1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,a} + \beta\, c_1^{\,a} > 0\), so that \(V_0 = K^{1/a}\) by Theorem 1.3(a). By the chain rule,

\[\frac{\partial V_0}{\partial c_0} = \frac{1}{a} K^{1/a - 1}\,(1-\beta)\,a\, c_0^{\,a-1} = (1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,a-1} K^{1/a - 1}, \qquad \frac{\partial V_0}{\partial c_1} = \beta\, c_1^{\,a-1} K^{1/a - 1}.\]

The factor \(K^{1/a-1}\) cancels in the ratio, so \(\mathrm{MRS} = \dfrac{1-\beta}{\beta}\,(c_0/c_1)^{a-1} = \dfrac{1-\beta}{\beta}\,(c_1/c_0)^{1-a}\). Since \(1 - a = 1/\psi\), the first claim follows. Taking logarithms, \(\ln\mathrm{MRS} = \ln\dfrac{1-\beta}{\beta} + \dfrac{1}{\psi}\ln\dfrac{c_1}{c_0}\), so \(d\ln\mathrm{MRS}/d\ln(c_1/c_0) = 1/\psi\), and the elasticity in Definition 2.1 is \(\varepsilon = \psi\). \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 2.3 (the EIS does not involve γ)
For a deterministic plan, \(\mathrm{CE}_1 = c_1\) for every \(\gamma \ne 1\), as in the proof of Corollary 1.4. So \(V_0\) depends on \(c_0\), \(c_1\), \(\beta\) and \(\psi\) only, and the EIS of Proposition 2.2 is \(\psi\) at every \(\gamma \ne 1\), the values A3 allows. The risk-aversion parameter has no effect on deterministic choices in this model. Lab 1 checks that a deterministic value is the same for three values of \(\gamma\), and it estimates the EIS at one deterministic plan for three values of \(\psi\), at \(\gamma = 2\) only.

3The restriction ψ = 1/γ and CRRA expected utility

Remark 3.1 (the exponent ratio)
Let \(\theta := (1-\gamma)/(1-1/\psi) = (1-\gamma)/a\). Then \(\theta = 1\) exactly when \(a = 1-\gamma\), that is, exactly when \(1/\psi = \gamma\). Under A1 to A4, therefore, \(\theta = 1\) holds exactly when \(\psi = 1/\gamma\).

Theorem 3.2 (the value is a power transform of CRRA expected utility when ψ = 1/γ)
Assume A1 to A4, and \(\psi = 1/\gamma\). The restriction needs \(\gamma \ne 0\), and A2 then forces \(\gamma > 0\). Let \(u(c) := c^{\,1-\gamma}/(1-\gamma)\) and \(U := (1-\beta)\, u(c_0) + \beta\, \E\, u(c_1)\), the CRRA expected utility of the plan. Then

\[(1-\gamma)\, U = V_0^{\,1-\gamma}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad V_0 = \bigl((1-\gamma)\, U\bigr)^{1/(1-\gamma)}.\]

Proof
Here \(a = 1 - 1/\psi = 1 - \gamma\). Theorem 1.3(a) gives \(V_0^{\,a} = (1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,a} + \beta\,\mathrm{CE}_1^{\,a}\), and \(\mathrm{CE}_1^{\,a} = \mathrm{CE}_1^{\,1-\gamma} = \E\, c_1^{\,1-\gamma}\) by Remark 1.2. Hence \(V_0^{\,1-\gamma} = (1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,1-\gamma} + \beta\,\E\, c_1^{\,1-\gamma}\). Since \(c^{\,1-\gamma} = (1-\gamma)\, u(c)\) for every \(c > 0\), the right side equals \((1-\gamma)\big[(1-\beta)\, u(c_0) + \beta\,\E\, u(c_1)\big] = (1-\gamma)\, U\). \(\blacksquare\)

Corollary 3.3 (the same ranking as CRRA expected utility when ψ = 1/γ)
Under the assumptions of Theorem 3.2, for any two plans \(P\) and \(Q\) with positive consumption, \(V_0(P) \ge V_0(Q)\) if and only if \(U(P) \ge U(Q)\).

Proof
By Theorem 3.2, \(V_0 = h(U)\) with \(h(u) := ((1-\gamma)\, u)^{1/(1-\gamma)}\). The derivative is \(h'(u) = ((1-\gamma)\, u)^{\gamma/(1-\gamma)} > 0\) on the set where \((1-\gamma)\, u > 0\). That set contains every value of \(U\), because \((1-\gamma)\, U = V_0^{\,1-\gamma} > 0\) by A4. So \(h\) is strictly increasing on the values of \(U\), and the two orderings agree. \(\blacksquare\)

Remark 3.4 (off the restriction the criteria can disagree)
For a deterministic plan, the CRRA expected utility \(U = (1-\beta)\, u(c_0) + \beta\, u(c_1)\) has marginal rate of substitution \(\mathrm{MRS}_U = \dfrac{1-\beta}{\beta}\,(c_1/c_0)^{\gamma}\), because \(u'(c) = c^{-\gamma}\). Taking logarithms as in Proposition 2.2, \(\ln\mathrm{MRS}_U = \ln\dfrac{1-\beta}{\beta} + \gamma\ln\dfrac{c_1}{c_0}\), so the elasticity of the consumption ratio with respect to \(\mathrm{MRS}_U\), defined as in Definition 2.1 with \(U\) in place of \(V_0\), is \(1/\gamma\) for \(\gamma \ne 0\). At \(\gamma = 0\) the marginal rate of substitution does not depend on \(c_1/c_0\), so the elasticity is undefined there. Proposition 2.2 gives \(\mathrm{MRS} = \dfrac{1-\beta}{\beta}\,(c_1/c_0)^{1/\psi}\) for the Epstein–Zin value. The two expressions agree at every deterministic plan exactly when \(1/\psi = \gamma\), which is the case of Theorem 3.2. The "only if" part follows from one plan with \(c_1 \ne c_0\): writing \(r = c_1/c_0 \ne 1\), agreement gives \(r^{\gamma} = r^{1/\psi}\), so \(\gamma \ln r = (1/\psi)\ln r\), and \(\ln r \ne 0\) forces \(\gamma = 1/\psi\). Off that case the two criteria assign different marginal rates of substitution at every plan with \(c_1 \ne c_0\), because \(r^{\gamma} \ne r^{1/\psi}\) for \(r \ne 1\) when \(\gamma \ne 1/\psi\). On the diagonal \(c_1 = c_0\) both have marginal rate of substitution \((1-\beta)/\beta\), so the two indifference curves there have the same slope \(-(1-\beta)/\beta\). Lab 1 gives an explicit pair of plans that the two criteria rank in opposite orders.

"""E3 lesson L05 lab: two-period Epstein-Zin values, checked against CRRA expected utility.

Standard library only (fractions, math). No randomness and no network: every case is fixed, so
the output is the same on every run. Consumption is a number of units of one good, and every value
is a dimensionless number. Exact cases use fractions.Fraction. Other cases use floats, and the
output prints them to at most four decimals or as True or False against a stated tolerance.

Formulas (Definition 1.1 and Theorem 1.3 of the lesson):
a = 1 - 1/psi (psi > 0, psi != 1)
CE1 = (sum_s p_s c1_s^(1-gamma))^(1/(1-gamma)) (gamma != 1)
V0 = ((1-beta) c0^a + beta CE1^a)^(1/a)
CRRA expected utility of the same plan, with u(c) = c^(1-gamma)/(1-gamma):
U = (1-beta) u(c0) + beta sum_s p_s u(c1_s)
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import itertools
import math
from collections.abc import Sequence
from fractions import Fraction

Num = Fraction | float
Plan = Sequence[tuple[Num, Num]] # (probability, consumption at date 1) for each state

def power(base: Num, exponent: Num) -> Num:
"""base ** exponent: exact when base is a Fraction and the exponent is a whole number."""
if isinstance(exponent, int) or (isinstance(exponent, Fraction) and exponent.denominator == 1):
return base ** int(exponent)
return math.pow(float(base), float(exponent))

def crra_ce(plan: Plan, gamma: Num) -> Num:
"""CRRA certainty equivalent CE1 of the date-1 lottery plan, for positive consumption and gamma != 1."""
if gamma == 1:
raise ValueError("gamma = 1 is outside the power form")
mean_power: Num = sum((p * power(c, 1 - gamma) for p, c in plan), Fraction(0))
return power(mean_power, 1 / (1 - gamma))

def crra_utility(c: Num, gamma: Num) -> Num:
"""CRRA utility u(c) = c^(1-gamma) / (1-gamma), for c > 0 and gamma != 1."""
return power(c, 1 - gamma) / (1 - gamma)

def ez_value(c0: Num, plan: Plan, beta: Num, gamma: Num, psi: Num) -> Num:
"""Two-period Epstein-Zin value V0 of a plan: sure consumption c0 and a date-1 lottery."""
if psi == 1:
raise ValueError("psi = 1 is outside this formula: the exponent a = 1 - 1/psi is zero")
a = 1 - 1 / psi
bracket = (1 - beta) * power(c0, a) + beta * power(crra_ce(plan, gamma), a)
return power(bracket, 1 / a)

def crra_eu(c0: Num, plan: Plan, beta: Num, gamma: Num) -> Num:
"""CRRA expected utility of the same plan: discounted sum of u(c0) and the expected u(c1)."""
expected_u1: Num = sum((p * crra_utility(c, gamma) for p, c in plan), Fraction(0))
return (1 - beta) * crra_utility(c0, gamma) + beta * expected_u1

def theta(gamma: Num, psi: Num) -> Num:
"""The exponent ratio theta = (1-gamma)/(1-1/psi); theta equals 1 exactly when psi = 1/gamma."""
return (1 - gamma) / (1 - 1 / psi)

def mrs_numeric(c0: float, c1: float, beta: float, psi: float, h: float = 1e-6) -> float:
"""MRS = (dV0/dc0)/(dV0/dc1) for a deterministic plan, by central differences of ez_value."""

def value(x: float, y: float) -> float:
result = ez_value(x, [(1.0, y)], beta, 2.0, psi)
return float(result)

dv_dc0 = (value(c0 * (1 + h), c1) - value(c0 * (1 - h), c1)) / (2 * c0 * h)
dv_dc1 = (value(c0, c1 * (1 + h)) - value(c0, c1 * (1 - h))) / (2 * c1 * h)
return dv_dc0 / dv_dc1

def eis_numeric(c0: float, c1: float, beta: float, psi: float) -> float:
"""EIS = d ln(c1/c0) / d ln MRS, estimated over c1 in [0.999, 1.001] times c1."""
lo, hi = 0.999 * c1, 1.001 * c1
numerator = math.log(hi / c0) - math.log(lo / c0)
denominator = math.log(mrs_numeric(c0, hi, beta, psi)) - math.log(mrs_numeric(c0, lo, beta, psi))
return numerator / denominator

The discordant pair is one pair of deterministic plans: it shows that the two criteria can rank plans differently when ψ ≠ 1/γ, and it does not show how often they disagree. The fall of CE1 with γ is checked on three values of γ only. C Every result of this lab holds on the listed cases; the general statements are the theorems above, and the lab does not replace their proofs.

4Where the canon breaks

The two-period form is the simplest recursion that separates the two parameters. Four limits stay with the form.

The separation is a parameter restriction, not a general result. Theorem 3.2 shows that at ψ = 1/γ the Epstein–Zin value is a transform of CRRA expected utility, so the two criteria rank plans alike (Corollary 3.3). Off that restriction, Remark 3.4 shows that the two criteria assign different marginal rates of substitution. Whether observed choices lie on the restriction is an empirical question, and this lesson states no answer to it.

Two dates only. The recursion in Definition 1.1 has one step and the terminal value \(V_1 = c_1\). A longer plan needs a continuation value (the value of the rest of the plan) at each intermediate date, and an infinite-horizon version needs an argument that a recursive definition has a solution. This lesson gives neither, and it claims no result for longer horizons.

Timing of the risk. Theorem 1.3 takes the date-1 lottery as given, with the date-0 consumption sure. It does not say what the value of a gamble resolved before date 1 should be. Lesson L08 states the timing convention for a one-shot gamble, where the four preference models are compared.

The EIS is defined on deterministic plans. Definition 2.1 uses deterministic plans only. Its meaning for risky plans is not given here.

5Misconceptions

“Epstein–Zin preferences with ψ = 1/γ are a different model from CRRA expected utility, and they rank plans differently.”

Corollary 3.3 proves that at ψ = 1/γ the two criteria give the same ranking of all plans with positive consumption. Lab 1 checks the ranking of four exact plans under both criteria and prints True. The disagreement in Lab 1 occurs at ψ = 2, which is outside the restriction.

“The elasticity of intertemporal substitution is the same thing as risk aversion, so γ sets how much consumption moves across dates.”

Proposition 2.2 gives an EIS equal to ψ. Remark 2.3 shows that γ does not enter the value of a deterministic plan, so it cannot set the EIS. Lab 1 prints a deterministic value that is the same for γ = 0.5, 2 and 3 at ψ = 1/2, and prints EIS estimates equal to ψ for ψ = 0.5, 2 and 0.25.

“A risky date-1 consumption is worth its mean under Epstein–Zin preferences.”

Corollary 1.4 says the risky plan is worth the sure plan at its certainty equivalent. For the exact tree the mean is \(5/2\) and the certainty equivalent at γ = 2 is \(8/5\). Lab 1 prints that the sure plan at \(8/5\) has the value \(80/49\), the same as the risky plan.

6Problem set

Attempt each problem before opening its solution. Problems marked E use the output of Lab 1.

Mechanics

M1 · The certainty equivalent of a two-state lottery
Let γ = 2 and let \(c_1 = 1\) with probability \(1/4\) and \(c_1 = 3\) with probability \(3/4\). Find \(\mathrm{CE}_1\) and the mean of \(c_1\).

Solution

With γ = 2, \(\mathrm{CE}_1 = (\E\, c_1^{-1})^{-1}\). Here \(\E\, c_1^{-1} = \tfrac14\cdot 1 + \tfrac34\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12\), so \(\mathrm{CE}_1 = 2\). The mean is \(\tfrac14\cdot 1 + \tfrac34\cdot 3 = \tfrac{10}{4} = \tfrac52\), so the certainty equivalent 2 lies below the mean 5/2.

M2 · The value of a deterministic plan
Let β = 1/2, ψ = 1/2, c0 = 1 and c1 = 3. Find \(V_0\). Take γ = 2 for the computation.

Solution

Here \(a = 1 - 1/\psi = -1\). Remark 2.3 says the deterministic value does not depend on γ, so γ = 2 is a valid choice. By Theorem 1.3(a) with \(\mathrm{CE}_1 = c_1 = 3\), \(V_0 = \bigl[\tfrac12\cdot 1^{-1} + \tfrac12\cdot 3^{-1}\bigr]^{-1} = \bigl[\tfrac12 + \tfrac16\bigr]^{-1} = \bigl[\tfrac23\bigr]^{-1} = \tfrac32\).

M3 · The exponent ratio θ
Find θ for γ = 2 and ψ = 2. Does the identity of Theorem 3.2 apply?

Solution

Here \(a = 1 - 1/2 = 1/2\), so \(\theta = (1-2)/(1/2) = -2\). Since \(\theta \ne 1\), the identity of Theorem 3.2 does not apply: Theorem 3.2 requires \(\psi = 1/\gamma\), and here \(\psi = 2\) while \(1/\gamma = 1/2\).

Proof

P1 · Corollary 1.4 from Theorem 1.3
Using Theorem 1.3(a) and the fact that a sure consumption is its own certainty equivalent, show that a risky plan and the sure plan at its certainty equivalent have the same Epstein–Zin value.

Solution

Theorem 1.3(a) expresses \(V_0\) as a function of \(c_0\), β, ψ and \(\mathrm{CE}_1\) alone. The sure plan with date-1 consumption \(\mathrm{CE}_1\) has, by the calculation in the proof of Corollary 1.4, the certainty equivalent \(\mathrm{CE}_1\). So both plans enter the same formula with the same \(c_0\), β, ψ and certainty equivalent, and their values are equal.

P2 · The EZ value of a sure plan when ψ = 1/γ
Let ψ = 1/γ and let \(c_1 > 0\) be a sure number. Show that \(V_0^{\,1-\gamma} = (1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,1-\gamma} + \beta\, c_1^{\,1-\gamma}\).

Solution

For a sure \(c_1\), \(\mathrm{CE}_1 = c_1\). The identity of Theorem 3.2 with \(\E\, c_1^{\,1-\gamma} = c_1^{\,1-\gamma}\) gives \(V_0^{\,1-\gamma} = (1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,1-\gamma} + \beta\, c_1^{\,1-\gamma}\) directly. Proposition 2.2 then gives the EIS of this family as \(\psi = 1/\gamma\).

P3 · The value does not depend on γ for deterministic plans
Show that for a sure \(c_1 > 0\), the value \(V_0\) depends on γ in no way once ψ is fixed.

Solution

For a sure \(c_1\), \(\mathrm{CE}_1 = (c_1^{\,1-\gamma})^{1/(1-\gamma)} = c_1\) for every \(\gamma \ne 1\). Theorem 1.3(a) then gives \(V_0 = \bigl[(1-\beta)\, c_0^{\,a} + \beta\, c_1^{\,a}\bigr]^{1/a}\), which contains β, ψ (through \(a = 1 - 1/\psi\)), \(c_0\) and \(c_1\), and no γ.

Empirical

E1 · Reproduce the exact value of Lab 1
For the tree of Lab 1 (γ = 2, ψ = 1/2, β = 9/10, c0 = 2, c1 = 1 or 4 with probability 1/2 each), compute \(V_0\) and U by hand and check them against the first lines of the output.

Solution

Here \(a = -1\), so \(V_0^{-1} = (1-\beta)/c_0 + \beta\,\E\, c_1^{-1}\) and \(\E\, c_1^{-1} = \tfrac12\cdot 1 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac58\). Then \(V_0^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{20} + \tfrac{9}{10}\cdot\tfrac58 = \tfrac{1}{20} + \tfrac{9}{16} = \tfrac{49}{80}\), so \(V_0 = \tfrac{80}{49}\), which the output prints. The CRRA utility is \(u(c) = -1/c\), so \(U = -\tfrac{1}{20} - \tfrac{9}{16} = -\tfrac{49}{80}\), which the output prints as −49/80.

E2 · Change the probabilities of the tree
Keep γ = 2, ψ = 1/2, β = 9/10 and c0 = 2, and keep c1 = 1 or 4, but give \(c_1 = 1\) probability 1/4 and \(c_1 = 4\) probability 3/4. Find \(\mathrm{CE}_1\) and \(V_0\) without running the lab.

Solution

\(\E\, c_1^{-1} = \tfrac14\cdot 1 + \tfrac34\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac{7}{16}\), so \(\mathrm{CE}_1 = 16/7\). Then \(V_0^{-1} = \tfrac{1}{20} + \tfrac{9}{10}\cdot\tfrac{7}{16} = \tfrac{8}{160} + \tfrac{63}{160} = \tfrac{71}{160}\), so \(V_0 = 160/71\). The value rises from \(80/49\) to \(160/71\), because the certainty equivalent rises from \(8/5\) to \(16/7\) (the state \(c_1 = 4\) now has probability \(3/4\)), and Theorem 1.3(b) makes \(V_0\) strictly increasing in \(\mathrm{CE}_1\).

E3 · Read the EIS lines of the output
Lab 1 prints the EIS for ψ = 0.5, 2.0 and 0.25 to four decimal places. Explain how each number is computed, the step size used, and what the printed digits represent.

Solution

Each estimate is \(\ln(c_1/c_0)\) differenced across \(c_1\) in \([0.999, 1.001]\) times 4, divided by the difference of \(\ln\mathrm{MRS}\) over the same interval. The MRS is itself a central difference of the value function with relative step \(10^{-6}\), taken in \(c_0\) and in \(c_1\). The printed digits are the estimate, printed to four decimal places. The estimate follows Definition 2.1 numerically, and Proposition 2.2 says that the exact EIS is ψ, which is the printed value in each case.

Research

R1 · Why a longer plan needs more than Theorem 1.3
Explain in words why the terminal convention \(V_1 = c_1\) cannot be applied to the intermediate dates of a longer plan, and what object a longer recursion must carry.

Solution

At an intermediate date the value of the rest of the plan is a random quantity over the later states, not a consumption number, so the recursion must carry a continuation value at each date. An infinite-horizon version defines that value implicitly, as the solution of a self-referential condition, and showing that the condition has a solution is a separate argument. This lesson does not give that argument, and no result for longer horizons is claimed here.

R2 · Which choices identify ψ
Argue which kind of choice between plans identifies ψ alone, and what a choice between risky plans adds to the identification of γ. Use Remark 2.3.

Solution

Choices among deterministic plans depend on \(c_0\), \(c_1\), β and ψ only, by Remark 2.3, so, given β, they can identify ψ without reference to γ. A choice between risky date-1 plans depends on \(\mathrm{CE}_1\), which depends on γ, so such choices can depend on γ once ψ is known. This is an argument about identification in the model. It does not estimate either parameter from data.

R3 · Where the two marginal rates of substitution differ
Show that the two marginal rates of substitution of Remark 3.4 agree on the diagonal \(c_1 = c_0\), and state the exponent on \(c_1/c_0\) that each one carries. Then explain why the pair of plans used in Lab 1, \((4, 4)\) and \((1, 10)\) at ψ = 2 and γ = 2, lies where the two criteria can disagree.

Solution

At \(c_1 = c_0\) both expressions equal \((1-\beta)/\beta\), since \((c_1/c_0)^{\gamma}\) and \((c_1/c_0)^{1/\psi}\) both equal 1. Off the diagonal the exponents are \(\gamma = 2\) for the CRRA criterion and \(1/\psi = 1/2\) for Epstein–Zin, so the two slopes differ whenever \(c_1 \ne c_0\), because for \(r = c_1/c_0 \ne 1\) the powers \(r^{2}\) and \(r^{1/2}\) are equal only when \(r^{3/2} = 1\), that is, \(r = 1\). The plan \((4, 4)\) sits on the diagonal, while \((1, 10)\) is far from it. The disagreement follows from the exponents, by Remark 3.4; Lab 1 reports only this pair.
