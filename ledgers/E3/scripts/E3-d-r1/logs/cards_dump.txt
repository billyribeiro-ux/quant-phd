E3-c001 L01 | What is a finite lottery? || A finite list of pairs (x_i, p_i) with distinct real outcomes, non-negative probabilities that sum to 1. Equivalently, a random variable with finitely many values (Definition 1.1).
E3-c002 L01 | What is the expected value of a finite lottery? || E X is the sum of p_i times x_i over its outcomes (Definition 1.2).
E3-c003 L01 | What is expected utility? || For a Bernoulli utility u defined on the outcomes, E u(X) is the sum of p_i times u(x_i). It is a number that depends on u as well as on the lottery (Definition 1.3).
E3-c004 L01 | What is assumption A1 of Lesson 1? || The lottery has finitely many outcomes, and u is real-valued on them. Every sum in the lesson uses A1, and no result there covers a lottery with infinitely many outcomes (Section 4).
E3-c005 L01 | What is the mixture alpha L + (1 - alpha) M of two finite lotteries? || The lottery on the union of their outcomes that pays x with probability alpha p_L(x) + (1 - alpha) p_M(x), where p_L(x) = 0 when L does not pay x (Definition 2.1).
E3-c006 L01 | What is a risk-neutral utility? || A utility u(x) = b x with b > 0. Its expected utility is b times the expected value, so two lotteries tie exactly when their expected values are equal (Definition 3.2).
E3-c007 L01 | Is a mixture of two finite lotteries again a finite lottery? || Yes. Under A1, for every alpha in [0, 1] the mixture has finitely many outcomes, non-negative weights, and weights that sum to one (Proposition 2.2).
E3-c008 L01 | What does Proposition 2.3 say about expected utility of a mixture, and under which assumptions? || Under A1, with u real-valued on the outcomes of L and M and alpha in [0, 1]: E u(alpha L + (1 - alpha) M) = alpha E u(L) + (1 - alpha) E u(M). Expected utility is linear in the probabilities (Proposition 2.3).
E3-c009 L01 | Does the linearity identity show that preferences over lotteries have the expected-utility form? || No. Proposition 2.3 is an identity about the formula in Definition 1.3. The representation of preferences is an axiomatic question that the lesson neither answers nor sources (Remark 2.4).
E3-c010 L01 | What is E u(X) when u(x) = a + b x for real constants a and b? || Under A1, E u(X) = a + b E X. With a = 0 and b = 1 the expected utility is the expected value (Proposition 3.1).
E3-c011 L01 | Does a positive affine transformation v = a + b u keep the ranking of lotteries by expected utility? || Yes, for b > 0. Then E v(L) = a + b E u(L), so E v(L) >= E v(M) exactly when E u(L) >= E u(M) (Proposition 3.3).
E3-c012 L01 | What does a negative scale factor do to that ranking? || It reverses it. With b < 0, E v(L) >= E v(M) holds exactly when E u(M) >= E u(L) (Problem P2).
E3-c013 L01 | Among all finite lotteries on a fixed finite set of outcomes, which one maximizes expected utility? || The lottery that pays the best outcome for certain, with value m = max u(x_i). Every lottery has E u(X) <= m, with equality only when all its probability sits on outcomes that attain m (Problem R1).
E3-c014 L01 | Is the expected utility of a lottery the utility of its expected outcome? || Only when u is affine (Proposition 3.1). For lottery A of Example 1.4 under u(x) = x^2 the expected outcome is 150 CU, so u(150) = 22500 CU squared, while the expected utility is 45000 CU squared (Misconception 1).
E3-c015 L01 | Does a risk-neutral decision maker ignore the probabilities? || No. Under a risk-neutral utility the expected utility is b times the expected value, a probability-weighted average of the outcomes (Definition 3.2).
E3-c016 L01 | What does Part A of Lab 1 print for the expected values and quadratic expected utilities of A, B and C? || Expected values of 15000, 12500 and 15000 cents, and quadratic expected utilities of 45000, 21250 and 37500 (exact, Fractions).
E3-c017 L01 | Does adding 3 to every utility, or doubling it, change which lottery is preferred? || Not when the factor is positive. A positive affine transformation preserves every comparison of expected utilities (Proposition 3.3). Lab 1, part C, prints six agreeing comparisons.
E3-c018 L02 | What is assumption A2 of Lesson 2? || u is twice differentiable on an open interval I, with u'(y) > 0 and u''(y) <= 0 for every y in I. The lesson defines concavity by the sign of u'' (A2).
E3-c019 L02 | Under A1 to A3, does a finite lottery have exactly one certainty equivalent? || Yes. E u(X) lies in the image of I under u, because it is a weighted average of the values u(x_i). Since u is strictly increasing on I, exactly one c in I has u(c) = E u(X) (Proposition 1.1).
E3-c020 L02 | What is the certainty equivalent of a lottery X under u? || The unique c with u(c) = E u(X), written CE(X; u). It is the sure amount with the same expected utility as the gamble (Definition 1.2).
E3-c021 L02 | What is the certainty-equivalent risk premium? || E X minus CE(X; u). The lesson uses the phrase risk premium only in this sense (Definition 1.2).
E3-c022 L02 | How does the certainty equivalent of a concave utility compare with the expected value? || Under A1 to A3, CE(X; u) <= E X (Proposition 1.3(a)). The proof uses the fact that the tangent line at E X lies above u, and averages it over the lottery.
E3-c023 L02 | When is the inequality of Proposition 1.3 strict? || When u'' < 0 on I, the condition stated for Proposition 1.3(b), and some outcome with positive probability differs from E X. Then CE(X; u) < E X (Proposition 1.3(b)).
E3-c024 L02 | What are the Arrow-Pratt absolute and relative risk aversion? || r_A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w), in units of 1/CU, and r_R(w) = w r_A(w), which is dimensionless (Definition 2.1).
E3-c025 L02 | Is r_A unchanged by a positive affine transformation of u? || Yes. For v = a + b u with b > 0, v' = b u' and v'' = b u'', so the factor b cancels and r_A is the same for v and u (Proposition 2.2).
E3-c026 L02 | What are r_A and r_R for u(w) = sqrt(w)? || r_A(w) = 1/(2w) and r_R = 1/2 at every w > 0. At w = 100 CU, r_A = 0.005 per CU and r_R = 0.5 (Example 2.3).
E3-c027 L02 | What is the second-order limit of the risk premium of a small gamble? || Under A1 and A5, the premium pi(eps) of the wealth lottery w + eps X satisfies pi(eps)/eps^2 tending to (1/2) r_A(w) s^2 as eps falls to 0, where s^2 = Var(X) for X with E X = 0 (Proposition 3.1).
E3-c028 L02 | Does the approximation (1/2) r_A Var(eps X) give a size for the error at a fixed eps? || No. The statement is asymptotic: the error divided by eps^2 tends to 0 as eps falls. It gives no bound at a fixed eps (Remark 3.2).
E3-c029 L02 | Is the risk premium half the variance of the gamble? || No. For the lab gamble (variance 1800 CU squared), the half-variance rule gives 900 CU, while the Pratt approximation is 4.5 CU. The premium uses r_A, not the variance alone (Misconception 1).
E3-c030 L02 | Do absolute and relative risk aversion measure the same thing? || No. They differ by the factor w. For u = sqrt(w) at w = 100 CU, r_A = 0.005 per CU and r_R = 0.5, in different units (Definition 2.1; Example 2.3).
E3-c031 L02 | Does a linear utility give a zero premium only for a degenerate gamble? || No. For u(w) = w, CE(X; u) = E X for every finite lottery, so the premium is zero whatever the gamble (Misconception 3).
E3-c032 L02 | What does Part A of Lab 1 print for the square-root utility at eps = 1? || ce_cents 9493 and premium_cents 507, with r_A 0.005000 and r_R 0.500000. The exact premium is 5.065835 CU, the approximation 4.500000 CU, and the error 0.565835 CU.

E3-c036 L03 | What is the power w^a for w > 0? || e^(a ln w), where ln is the natural logarithm (Definition 1.1).
E3-c037 L03 | What are the powers-of-powers and product rules for positive numbers? || For w > 0 and real a, b: (w^a)^b = w^(ab). For positive a, b and real c: (ab)^c = a^c b^c (Proposition 1.2).
E3-c038 L03 | What is the CRRA utility with coefficient gamma? || u_gamma(w) = (w^(1 - gamma) - 1)/(1 - gamma) for gamma not equal to 1, and u_1(w) = ln w, for w > 0 (Definition 1.3).
E3-c039 L03 | Why does the normalized CRRA form subtract 1? || It makes u_gamma(1) = 0 for every gamma, and it gives a finite limit at gamma = 1. The subtracted constant leaves the derivatives, and so the Arrow-Pratt measures, unchanged, and changes no certainty equivalent (Proposition 2.7).
E3-c040 L03 | What is the limit of u_gamma(w) as gamma tends to 1? || ln w, for every w > 0 (Proposition 1.4). With s = 1 - gamma and h = s ln w, u_gamma(w) equals ln w times (e^h - 1)/h, which tends to ln w.
E3-c041 L03 | What are the Arrow-Pratt measures of the CRRA family? || u_gamma'(w) = w^(-gamma) and u_gamma''(w) = -gamma w^(-gamma - 1). So r_A(w) = gamma/w and r_R(w) = gamma for every w > 0 (Proposition 1.7).
E3-c042 L03 | What are the Arrow-Pratt measures as recalled in Lesson 3? || For a twice differentiable utility u on (0, infinity) with u'(w) > 0: r_A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w) and r_R(w) = w r_A(w). Lesson 2 defines both (Definition 1.6).
E3-c043 L03 | What does constant relative risk aversion imply about u? || Under A3 (u twice differentiable on (0, infinity) with u' > 0) and A4 (r_R(w) = gamma for one real gamma), u = K u_gamma + C with K > 0 and a constant C (Theorem 2.1).
E3-c044 L03 | Does Theorem 2.1 show that a particular investor has constant relative risk aversion? || No. It runs from the utility's properties to its form. Whether a real decision maker has constant relative risk aversion is an empirical question, and no source is cited for it (Remark 2.2).
E3-c045 L03 | What is the certainty equivalent of a lottery with positive outcomes under u? || The amount c with u(c) = E u(X), as in Lesson 2. Every outcome is positive, so each outcome lies in the domain of A1 (Definition 2.3).
E3-c046 L03 | What is the closed form of the CRRA certainty equivalent? || For positive outcomes and gamma not equal to 1, with s = 1 - gamma and M = sum of p_i x_i^s: CE = M^(1/s). For gamma = 1, CE = exp(sum of p_i ln x_i) (Proposition 2.4).
E3-c047 L03 | What are the two exact certainty equivalents of the 1000 and 4000 cent lottery? || 1600 cents at gamma = 2 and 2000 cents at gamma = 1, for outcomes 1000 and 4000 with probability one half each (Example 2.5).
E3-c048 L03 | Does doubling every outcome double the CRRA certainty equivalent? || Yes, for every real gamma and every k > 0: CE_gamma(kX) = k CE_gamma(X) (Proposition 2.6). The identity uses (k x)^s = k^s x^s.
E3-c049 L03 | Does a positive affine change of utility change a certainty equivalent? || No. For a > 0 and real b, the certainty equivalent under a u + b equals the one under u (Proposition 2.7).
E3-c050 L03 | What rounding rule does the unit use for a money amount, and what is the bound on rounded multiples? || Compute the value once as a float, then round to the nearest whole cent with halves going up. For a whole number k >= 1, the rounded value of k v differs from k times the rounded value of v by at most (k + 1)/2 cents (Remark 2.8).
E3-c051 L03 | Does constant relative risk aversion mean constant utility? || No. The utility changes with w. What is constant is r_R = gamma (Proposition 1.7). The absolute measure r_A = gamma/w falls as wealth rises when gamma > 0 (Misconception 1).
E3-c052 L03 | Is the logarithm a separate case outside the CRRA family? || No. It is the member at gamma = 1, and the family converges to it as gamma tends to 1 (Proposition 1.4).
E3-c053 L03 | Is the certainty equivalent scale invariant only for the logarithm? || No. Proposition 2.6 gives CE_gamma(kX) = k CE_gamma(X) for every real gamma. Lab 1 checks k = 2, 3 and 10 on its grid (Misconception 3).

E3-c056 L04 | What are the distribution function and the integrated distribution function of X? || F_X(t) = P(X <= t), and I_X(t) is the integral from minus infinity to t of F_X(s) ds (Definition 1.1).
E3-c057 L04 | What is I_X(t) under A1? || E[(t - X)^+], the expected shortfall below t, where z^+ = max(z, 0) (Lemma 1.2).
E3-c058 L04 | When does X dominate Y in the first order? || When F_X(t) <= F_Y(t) for every real t. Then X is at least as likely as Y to exceed every level t (Definition 1.3).
E3-c059 L04 | When is a function u on S nondecreasing, and when is it concave on S? || Nondecreasing: u(s_j) <= u(s_{j+1}) for each j. Concave on S: the chord slopes (u(s_{j+1}) - u(s_j))/(s_{j+1} - s_j) never increase along the support (Definition 1.4).
E3-c060 L04 | What does first-order dominance on a finite support characterize? || Under A1, F_X(t) <= F_Y(t) for every real t if and only if E u(X) >= E u(Y) for every nondecreasing u on S (Theorem 1.5).
E3-c061 L04 | Do the first-order inequalities need to hold at every real t? || No. Under A1 it is enough that F_X(s) <= F_Y(s) at each support point s in S (Lemma 1.6).
E3-c062 L04 | Does first-order dominance imply the integrated order, and is equal means needed? || Yes. If F_X <= F_Y everywhere, then I_X(t) <= I_Y(t) for every real t. The equal-means assumption A2 is not needed (Proposition 1.8).
E3-c063 L04 | What is second-order dominance? || Under A1 and A2 (equal means), X dominates Y in the second order when I_X(t) <= I_Y(t) for every real t (Definition 2.1).
E3-c064 L04 | Does the integrated test need every real t? || No. Under A1 and A2, I_X <= I_Y at every real t if and only if it holds at every support point (Lemma 2.2).
E3-c065 L04 | What does second-order dominance characterize? || Under A1 and A2, I_X(t) <= I_Y(t) for every real t if and only if E u(X) >= E u(Y) for every nondecreasing concave u on S (Theorem 2.3).
E3-c066 L04 | Does the proof of the only-if direction of Theorem 2.3 use monotonicity? || No. It uses concavity through nonnegative chord-slope differences and A2 through the vanishing affine term, so the conclusion holds for every concave u on S (Remark 2.4).
E3-c067 L04 | What are the integrated values of the spread pair of Example 2.5? || X (probability 1/2 on 2000 and on 4000) has I_X(3000), I_X(4000) and I_X(5000) equal to 500, 1000 and 2000. Y (1/4 on 1000, 1/2 on 3000, 1/4 on 5000) has I_Y(2000), I_Y(3000), I_Y(4000) and I_Y(5000) equal to 250, 500, 1250 and 2000. The means are both 3000 cents.
E3-c068 L04 | Does the spread pair of Example 2.5 have a first-order ranking? || No. F_X(2000) = 1/2 > 1/4 = F_Y(2000), while F_Y(3000) = 3/4 > 1/2 = F_X(3000). X dominates Y in the second order (Example 2.5).
E3-c069 L04 | Do equal means give a ranking in Example 2.6? || Not by the first-order test. X is 1/2 on 1000 and 1/2 on 5000, and Y is 3000 for certain. The two means are 3000 cents, the distribution functions cross, and Y dominates X in the second order (Example 2.6).
E3-c070 L04 | Which lottery of Example 1.7 dominates in the first order? || Y, because F_Y(t) <= F_X(t) at every support point. On 2000, 3000 and 4000 cents, F_X = (1/2, 1/2, 1) and F_Y = (0, 1/2, 1) (Example 1.7).
E3-c071 L04 | With unequal means, does second-order dominance still give E u(X) >= E u(Y) for nondecreasing concave u? || Yes, when I_X(t) <= I_Y(t) for every t. At t = s_m the integrated value is s_m minus the mean, so the hypothesis gives E X >= E Y, and the slope on the last interval is nonnegative because u is nondecreasing (Problem R3).
E3-c072 L04 | Do equal means make one lottery dominate the other? || No. Equal means are a hypothesis of the second-order theorem, not a ranking. Example 2.6 has equal means and no first-order ranking (Misconception 1).
E3-c073 L04 | Is second-order dominance a weaker test that ranks every pair? || No. It applies only to equal-mean pairs, and it ranks a pair only when the integrated functions are ordered at every support point. In Lab 1, 2 of the 8 equal-mean seeded pairs (pairs 16 and 19) have no second-order ranking.
E3-c074 L04 | Is the second-order test the first-order test under another name? || No. Example 2.5 has a second-order ranking and no first-order ranking. Proposition 1.8 shows that a first-order ranking implies the integrated ordering.

E3-c077 L04 | Does Lab 1 prove Theorems 1.5 and 2.3? || No. The expected-utility side checks a finite test set (the witnesses and three CRRA utilities) on seeded pairs. The theorems quantify over every nondecreasing or concave utility on S, which the lab does not check (Section 3).
E3-c078 L05 | What is the two-period Epstein-Zin value of a plan? || V0 = [(1 - beta) c0^a + beta (E V1^(1 - gamma))^(a/(1 - gamma))]^(1/a), with a = 1 - 1/psi and terminal value V1 = c1 (Definition 1.1). Assumptions: 0 < beta < 1, psi > 0 and psi not 1, gamma not 1, and positive consumption.
E3-c079 L05 | What is the period-1 certainty equivalent CE1? || CE1 = (E c1^(1 - gamma))^(1/(1 - gamma)), the CRRA certainty equivalent of the date-1 lottery. It satisfies CE1^(1 - gamma) = E c1^(1 - gamma) (Remark 1.2).
E3-c080 L05 | How does the date-1 lottery enter the Epstein-Zin value? || Only through CE1: V0 = [(1 - beta) c0^a + beta CE1^a]^(1/a). For fixed c0, beta, gamma and psi, V0 is strictly increasing in CE1 (Theorem 1.3, under A1 to A4).
E3-c081 L05 | What is the value of a risky date-1 plan? || It equals the value of the sure plan whose date-1 consumption is CE1 (Corollary 1.4).
E3-c082 L05 | Does the mean of the date-1 lottery enter the Epstein-Zin value? || No. The period-1 risk acts only through CE1 (Remark 1.5). In Lab 1 the mean of c1 is 5/2 and CE1 is 8/5.
E3-c083 L05 | What is the elasticity of intertemporal substitution? || For deterministic plans, eps = d ln(c1/c0) / d ln MRS, where MRS = (dV0/dc0)/(dV0/dc1) is the marginal rate of substitution (Definition 2.1).
E3-c084 L05 | What is the EIS of the two-period Epstein-Zin form? || psi. Under A1, A2 and A4 for deterministic plans, MRS = ((1 - beta)/beta)(c1/c0)^(1/psi), so the elasticity equals psi (Proposition 2.2).
E3-c085 L05 | Does risk aversion gamma change the EIS or the value of a deterministic plan? || No. For a deterministic plan CE1 = c1, so V0 depends on c0, c1, beta and psi only. The EIS is psi for every gamma (Remark 2.3).
E3-c086 L05 | When is theta = (1 - gamma)/(1 - 1/psi) equal to 1? || Exactly when psi = 1/gamma (Remark 3.1).
E3-c087 L05 | When is the Epstein-Zin value a power transform of CRRA expected utility? || When psi = 1/gamma, under A1, A3 and A4: (1 - gamma) U = V0^(1 - gamma), where U = (1 - beta) u(c0) + beta E u(c1) and u(c) = c^(1 - gamma)/(1 - gamma) (Theorem 3.2).
E3-c088 L05 | Under psi = 1/gamma, do the two criteria rank plans the same way? || Yes. V0 is a strictly increasing function of U, so V0(P) >= V0(Q) exactly when U(P) >= U(Q) (Corollary 3.3).
E3-c089 L05 | What are the MRS exponents of the two criteria off the restriction? || CRRA expected utility has MRS = ((1 - beta)/beta)(c1/c0)^gamma. Epstein-Zin has ((1 - beta)/beta)(c1/c0)^(1/psi). They agree on every deterministic plan exactly when 1/psi = gamma (Remark 3.4).

E3-c118 L07 | What is a weighting function? || A map omega from [0, 1] to [0, 1] that is nondecreasing, with omega(0) = 0 and omega(1) = 1. omega(p) is the decision weight of an outcome with probability p (Definition 1.1).
E3-c119 L07 | What is the non-cumulative weighted value of a lottery on gains? || V_omega(L) = sum of omega(p_i) v(x_i). Each outcome's own probability is weighted, not the probability of reaching it. With omega(p) = p and v = u it is expected utility (Definition 1.2).
E3-c120 L07 | What does the identity weighting give? || V_omega(L) = sum of p_i v(x_i), which is the expected value of v(X) (Remark 1.3).
E3-c121 L07 | What is the weighting function with eta = 1/2 in closed form, and what are its values at 3/10, 3/5 and 1/2? || omega(p) = sqrt(p)/(1 + 2 sqrt(p(1 - p))), with omega(0) = 0 and omega(1) = 1. E3-c136 L08 | What is a prior set? || A finite nonempty set of probability distributions over the outcomes of a gamble. For a two-outcome gamble a prior is a number p in [0, 1], the probability of the first outcome (Definition 1.1).
E3-c137 L08 | What are the alpha-MEU value and the maxmin value? || V_alpha(f) = alpha min over P of E_P u(f) + (1 - alpha) max over P of E_P u(f). Maxmin expected utility is V_1(f) = min over P of E_P u(f). The certainty equivalent is u^(-1)(V_alpha(f)) (Definition 1.2).
E3-c138 L08 | What does a one-element prior set give for every alpha? || Expected utility under that prior, because the minimum and the maximum coincide (Proposition 1.3).
E3-c139 L08 | What is the average prior of the 90-ball urn in Example 1.4? || P(red) = 1/2. The prior set has P_k(red) = k/90 for k = 30 to 60, and the uniform average of those priors is 45/90 = 1/2 (Example 1.4).
E3-c140 L08 | When is a decision rule ambiguity averse at f? || When CE(f) <= CE_Pbar(f), where Pbar is the uniform average of the prior set and CE_Pbar(f) = u^(-1)(E_Pbar u(f)) (Definition 2.1).
E3-c141 L08 | What does Theorem 2.2 say about maxmin? || Under A1 (a finite nonempty prior set with uniform average), A2 (u strictly increasing) and A3 (finitely many outcomes), the maxmin certainty equivalent is at most the certainty equivalent at the average prior (Theorem 2.2).
E3-c142 L08 | When is the ambiguity-aversion inequality strict? || Never for a one-element prior set, which gives equality. It is strict when the priors give different expected utilities at f, as in the gamble of Section 4 (Remark 2.3).
E3-c143 L08 | Does one prior serve as the worst prior for every bet? || No. For a bet on red the worst prior in the full set has no red balls, and for a bet on black it has no black balls (Proposition 2.4).
E3-c144 L08 | What are the two-urn certainty equivalents of Example 2.5? || The known urn (30 red of 90) has red CE 103125 cents and black CE 106451.6129 cents.
