E3-c001 L01 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is a finite lottery?
  B: A finite list of pairs (x_i, p_i) with distinct real outcomes, non-negative probabilities that sum to 1. Equivalently, a random variable with finitely many values (Definition 1.1).
E3-c002 L01 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is the expected value of a finite lottery?
  B: E X is the sum of p_i times x_i over its outcomes (Definition 1.2).
E3-c003 L01 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is expected utility?
  B: For a Bernoulli utility u defined on the outcomes, E u(X) is the sum of p_i times u(x_i). It is a number that depends on u as well as on the lottery (Definition 1.3).
E3-c004 L01 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is assumption A1 of Lesson 1?
  B: The lottery has finitely many outcomes, and u is real-valued on them. Every sum is finite under A1, and no result there covers a lottery with infinitely many outcomes (Section 4).
E3-c005 L01 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is the mixture alpha L + (1 - alpha) M of two finite lotteries?
  B: The lottery on the union of their outcomes that pays x with probability alpha p_L(x) + (1 - alpha) p_M(x), where p_L(x) = 0 when L does not pay x (Definition 2.1).
E3-c006 L01 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is a risk-neutral utility?
  B: A utility u(x) = b x with b > 0. Its expected utility is b times the expected value (Definition 3.2).
E3-c007 L01 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Is a mixture of two finite lotteries again a finite lottery?
  B: Yes. Under A1, for every alpha in [0, 1] the mixture has finitely many outcomes, non-negative weights, and weights that sum to one (Proposition 2.2).
E3-c008 L01 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What does Proposition 2.3 say about expected utility of a mixture, and under which assumptions?
  B: Under A1, with u real-valued on the outcomes of L and M and alpha in [0, 1]: E u(alpha L + (1 - alpha) M) = alpha E u(L) + (1 - alpha) E u(M). Expected utility is linear in the probabilities (Proposition 2.3).
E3-c009 L01 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does the linearity identity show that preferences over lotteries have the expected-utility form?
  B: No. Proposition 2.3 is an identity about the formula in Definition 1.3. It makes no claim about which preference orderings have that form (Remark 2.4).
E3-c010 L01 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What is E u(X) when u(x) = a + b x for real constants a and b?
  B: Under A1, E u(X) = a + b E X. With a = 0 and b = 1 the expected utility is the expected value (Proposition 3.1).
E3-c011 L01 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does a positive affine transformation v = a + b u keep the ranking of lotteries by expected utility?
  B: Under A1 and for b > 0, E v(L) = a + b E u(L), so E v(L) >= E v(M) exactly when E u(L) >= E u(M) (Proposition 3.3).
E3-c012 L01 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What does a negative scale factor do to that ranking?
  B: It reverses it. Under A1 and with b < 0, E v(L) >= E v(M) holds exactly when E u(M) >= E u(L) (Problem P2).
E3-c013 L01 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Among all finite lotteries on a fixed finite set of outcomes, which one maximizes expected utility?
  B: The lottery that pays, for certain, an outcome whose utility equals m, with m = max u(x_i). Every lottery has E u(X) <= m, with equality only when all its probability sits on outcomes that attain m (Problem R1).
E3-c014 L01 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Is the expected utility of a lottery the utility of its expected outcome?
  B: Not in general. Under an affine u it is (Proposition 3.1). For lottery A of Example 1.4 under u(x) = x^2, u(E X) = 150^2 = 22500 CU squared, while E u(X) is 45000 CU squared (section 5, first misconception).
E3-c015 L01 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does a risk-neutral decision maker ignore the probabilities?
  B: No. Under a risk-neutral utility the expected utility is b times the expected value, a probability-weighted average of the outcomes (Definitions 1.2 and 3.2).

E3-c018 L02 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is assumption A2 of Lesson 2?
  B: u is twice differentiable on an open interval I, with u'(y) > 0 and u''(y) <= 0 for every y in I. Concavity is defined here by the sign of u'' (A2).
E3-c019 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Under A1 to A3, with F1 and F5, does a finite lottery have exactly one certainty equivalent?
  B: Yes. E u(X) is a weighted average of the values u(x_i), so it lies in the image u(I), which is an interval by F1. Since u is strictly increasing on I by F5, exactly one c in I has u(c) = E u(X) (Proposition 1.1).
E3-c020 L02 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is the certainty equivalent of a lottery X under u?
  B: The unique c with u(c) = E u(X), written CE(X; u). It is the sure amount with the same expected utility as the gamble (Definition 1.2).
E3-c021 L02 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is the certainty-equivalent risk premium?
  B: E X minus CE(X; u). The phrase risk premium is used only in this sense (Definition 1.2) on this page.
E3-c022 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: How does the certainty equivalent of a concave utility compare with the expected value?
  B: Assuming F1, F2 and F5, under A1 to A3, CE(X; u) <= E X (Proposition 1.3(a)). The proof uses the fact that the tangent line at E X lies above u, and averages it over the lottery.
E3-c023 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: When is the inequality of Proposition 1.3 strict?
  B: Assuming F1, F2 and F5, under A1 to A3, and A4 (u'' < 0 on I): CE(X; u) < E X when some outcome with positive probability differs from E X (Proposition 1.3(b)).
E3-c024 L02 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What are the Arrow-Pratt absolute and relative risk aversion?
  B: r_A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w), in units of 1/CU, at each w where u'(w) is nonzero, and r_R(w) = w r_A(w), which is dimensionless (Definition 2.1).
E3-c025 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Is r_A unchanged by a positive affine transformation of u?
  B: Yes. For v = a + b u with b > 0, v' = b u' and v'' = b u'', so at every w where u'(w) is nonzero the factor b cancels and r_A is the same for v and u (Proposition 2.2).
E3-c026 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Assuming F6, what are r_A and r_R for u(w) = sqrt(w)?
  B: r_A(w) = 1/(2w) and r_R = 1/2 at every w > 0. At w = 100 CU, r_A = 0.005 per CU and r_R = 0.5 (Example 2.3).
E3-c027 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What is the second-order limit of the risk premium of a small gamble?
  B: Assuming F1, F3, F4 and F5, under A1 and A5, for a gamble X with E X = 0, the premium pi(eps) of the wealth lottery w + eps X satisfies pi(eps)/eps^2 tending to (1/2) r_A(w) s^2 as eps falls to 0, where s^2 = Var(X) (Proposition 3.1).
E3-c028 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does the approximation (1/2) r_A Var(eps X) give a size for the error at a fixed eps?
  B: No. The statement is asymptotic: the error divided by eps^2 tends to 0 as eps falls. It gives no bound at a fixed eps (Remark 3.2).
E3-c029 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Is the risk premium half the variance of the gamble?
  B: No. For the lab gamble (variance 1800 CU squared), the half-variance rule gives 900 CU squared, while the Pratt approximation is 4.5 CU. The premium uses r_A, not the variance alone (section 5, first misconception).
E3-c030 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Do absolute and relative risk aversion measure the same thing?
  B: No. They differ by the factor w. Assuming F6, for u = sqrt(w) at w = 100 CU, r_A = 0.005 per CU and r_R = 0.5, in different units (Definition 2.1; Example 2.3).
E3-c031 L02 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does a linear utility give a zero premium only for a degenerate gamble?
  B: No. For u(w) = w, CE(X; u) = E X for every finite lottery with outcomes in I (A1 and A3), so the premium is zero whatever the gamble (section 5, third misconception).

E3-c036 L03 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is the power w^a for w > 0?
  B: e^(a ln w), where ln is the natural logarithm (Definition 1.1).
E3-c037 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What are the powers-of-powers and product rules for positive numbers?
  B: For w > 0 and real a, b: (w^a)^b = w^(ab). For positive x and y and real c: (xy)^c = x^c y^c (Proposition 1.2).
E3-c038 L03 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is the CRRA utility with coefficient gamma?
  B: u_gamma(w) = (w^(1 - gamma) - 1)/(1 - gamma) for gamma not equal to 1, and u_1(w) = ln w, for w > 0 (Definition 1.3).
E3-c039 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Why does the normalized CRRA form subtract 1?
  B: It makes u_gamma(1) = 0 for every gamma (Definition 1.3).
E3-c040 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Assuming A2, what is the limit of u_gamma(w) as gamma tends to 1?
  B: ln w, for every w > 0 (Proposition 1.4). With s = 1 - gamma and h = s ln w, u_gamma(w) equals ln w times (e^h - 1)/h, which tends to ln w.
E3-c041 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What are the Arrow-Pratt measures of the CRRA family?
  B: Assuming F2, u_gamma'(w) = w^(-gamma) and u_gamma''(w) = -gamma w^(-gamma - 1). So r_A(w) = gamma/w and r_R(w) = gamma for every w > 0 (Proposition 1.7).
E3-c043 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What does constant relative risk aversion imply about u?
  B: Assuming F1 and F2, under A3 (u twice differentiable on (0, infinity) with u' > 0) and A4 (r_R(w) = gamma for one real gamma), u = K u_gamma + C with K > 0 and a constant C (Theorem 2.1).
E3-c044 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does Theorem 2.1 show that a particular investor has constant relative risk aversion?
  B: No. It runs from the utility's properties to its form. Whether a real decision maker has constant relative risk aversion is an empirical question, and no source is cited for it (Remark 2.2).
E3-c045 L03 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: Why must every outcome be positive for the CRRA certainty equivalent?
  B: So that each outcome lies in the domain of A1 (wealth above zero), where the power and the logarithm are defined (Definition 2.3).
E3-c046 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What is the closed form of the CRRA certainty equivalent?
  B: For positive outcomes and gamma not equal to 1, with s = 1 - gamma and M = sum of p_i x_i^s: CE = M^(1/s). For gamma = 1, CE = exp(sum of p_i ln x_i) (Proposition 2.4).
E3-c047 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What are the two exact certainty equivalents of the 1000 and 4000 cent lottery?
  B: 1600 cents at gamma = 2 and 2000 cents at gamma = 1, for outcomes 1000 and 4000 with probability one half each (Example 2.5).
E3-c048 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does doubling every outcome double the CRRA certainty equivalent?
  B: For a lottery with positive outcomes and k > 0, CE_gamma(kX) = k CE_gamma(X) for every real gamma (Proposition 2.6). The identity uses (k x)^s = k^s x^s.
E3-c049 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does a positive affine change of utility change a certainty equivalent?
  B: No. For a lottery whose certainty equivalent under u exists and is unique, and for a > 0 and real b, the certainty equivalent under a u + b equals the one under u (Proposition 2.7).
E3-c050 L03 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What rounding rule does the unit use for a money amount?
  B: Compute the value once as a float, then round it to the nearest whole cent with halves going up, which is floor(v + 1/2) for v >= 0. The rule rounds the unrounded value, not an intermediate one (Remark 2.8).
E3-c051 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does constant relative risk aversion mean constant utility?
  B: No. The utility changes with w. Assuming F2, what is constant is r_R = gamma (Proposition 1.7), and the absolute measure r_A = gamma/w falls as wealth rises when gamma > 0 (section 4, first misconception).
E3-c052 L03 grade=T basis=None cite=None

E3-c056 L04 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What are the distribution function and the integrated distribution function of X?
  B: F_X(t) = P(X <= t), and I_X(t) is the integral from minus infinity to t of F_X(s) ds (Definition 1.1).
E3-c057 L04 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What is I_X(t) under A1?
  B: E[(t - X)^+], the expected shortfall below t, where z^+ = max(z, 0) (Lemma 1.2).
E3-c058 L04 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: When does X dominate Y in the first order?
  B: When F_X(t) <= F_Y(t) for every real t. Then X is at least as likely as Y to exceed every level t (Definition 1.3).
E3-c059 L04 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: When is a function u on S nondecreasing?
  B: u(s_j) <= u(s_{j+1}) for each j (Definition 1.4).
E3-c060 L04 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What does first-order dominance on a finite support characterize?
  B: Under A1, F_X(t) <= F_Y(t) for every real t if and only if E u(X) >= E u(Y) for every nondecreasing u on S (Theorem 1.5).
E3-c061 L04 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Do the first-order inequalities need to hold at every real t?
  B: No. Under A1 it is enough that F_X(s) <= F_Y(s) at each support point s in S (Lemma 1.6).
E3-c062 L04 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does first-order dominance imply the integrated order, and is equal means needed?
  B: Under A1, if F_X <= F_Y everywhere, then I_X(t) <= I_Y(t) for every real t. The equal-means assumption A2 is not needed (Proposition 1.8).
E3-c063 L04 grade=None basis=None cite=None
  F: What is second-order dominance?
  B: Under A1 and A2 (equal means), X dominates Y in the second order when I_X(t) <= I_Y(t) for every real t (Definition 2.1).
E3-c064 L04 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: Does the integrated test need every real t?
  B: No. Under A1 and A2, I_X <= I_Y at every real t if and only if it holds at every support point (Lemma 2.2).
E3-c065 L04 grade=T basis=None cite=None
  F: What does second-order dominance characterize?
  B: Under A1 and A2, I_X(t) <= I_Y(t) for every real t if and only if E u(X) >= E u(Y) for every nondecreasing concave u on S (Theorem 2.3).
E3-c066 L04 grade=T basis=None cite=None
