Decision Theory Under Risk & Ambiguity

Some economic choices compare gambles: a sure payment against a lottery, a consumption plan with a risky second period, or a bet on an urn whose composition is not known. This module gives the models that economists use for those comparisons, and the assumptions each one makes. It covers expected utility and the certainty equivalent, constant relative risk aversion, stochastic dominance, Epstein–Zin recursive preferences, prospect theory with loss aversion and probability weighting, and maxmin ambiguity aversion. It ends in a preference-elicitation project, in which you estimate a participant's risk-aversion and loss-aversion parameters from a questionnaire by maximum likelihood and report their uncertainty. You leave able to compute the certainty equivalent of a gamble under each model, to test two gambles for stochastic dominance, and to state what each model assumes.

Prerequisites: E1 Microeconomics and M4 Probability. The unit uses the concepts linked in the prerequisites section.

Unlocks: E4 General Equilibrium & Information in Markets, AP2 Mean-Variance, CAPM, ICAPM & APT, and AP7 Behavioral Finance.

Project: Preference elicitation, graded by an autograder and a rubric

Data: none. The labs compute from formulas and from lotteries written into their code, and the labs of Lessons 1 to 3 draw no random numbers. The project's autograder responses are labelled SIMULATED on the project page.

56 h lessons · 14 h project (20%)

Objectives

By the end of E3 you can:

Compute the expected utility of a finite lottery, and prove that it is linear in the probabilities (Lesson 1).

Derive the certainty equivalent and the Arrow–Pratt measures of a concave utility, and prove that the certainty equivalent does not exceed the expected value (Lesson 2).

Compute the certainty equivalent under constant relative risk aversion in whole cents, and check its homogeneity (Lesson 3).

Test two finite lotteries for first-order and second-order stochastic dominance, and check each verdict against expected-utility rankings (Lesson 4).

Compute a two-period Epstein–Zin value, and state when it is a power transform of the CRRA expected-utility value and ranks plans the same way (Lesson 5).

Evaluate a reference-dependent value with loss aversion, and weigh the probabilities of a gamble with a probability-weighting function (Lessons 6 and 7).

Compare four preference models on one gamble, including a maxmin model of ambiguity aversion (Lesson 8).

Estimate risk-aversion and loss-aversion parameters from choices by maximum likelihood, and report their uncertainty (project).

Placement diagnostic

Eight questions: six on the prerequisites, and two skill checks, marked as such, on what rungs 1 and 2 of the project teach. The mastery bar is 80 percent. For eight items that means seven correct: six of eight is 75 percent, and seven of eight is 87.5 percent. To skip rungs 1 and 2, you must reach the bar and answer both skill checks. A result below the bar is not a judgement of the rest of your work; it means that rungs 1 and 2 are worth reading.

A fair coin pays 100 CU on heads and 0 CU on tails. What is its expected value, in CU?

Answer
\(100 \cdot \tfrac12 + 0 \cdot \tfrac12 = 50\) CU. This is the expectation of M4 (Definition 1.2 of L01 uses it).

A random variable takes the value 0 or 2 with probability 1/2 each. What is its variance?

Answer
The mean is 1 and \(\E X^{2} = (0 + 4)/2 = 2\), so the variance is \(2 - 1^{2} = 1\). The variance is the M4 concept used in L02.

What is the derivative of \(\ln x\) at \(x = 2\)?

Answer
The derivative of \(\ln x\) is \(1/x\), which is \(1/2 = 0.5\) at \(x = 2\). Problem M2 of L02 uses this derivative to compute \(r_A\) for the logarithm.

What is the present value, in CU to three decimal places, of 100 CU paid in one year at an annual rate of 10 percent with annual compounding?

Answer
The discount factor is \(1/1.1\), so the present value is \(100/1.1 = 90.909\) CU to three decimal places. This is the discount factor of M1.

A utility function represents a preference for more money over less when it is:

Options
Strictly increasing in the amount
Strictly decreasing in the amount
Constant in the amount

A higher amount gives a higher utility, so the function is strictly increasing. This is the consumer-theory idea that E1 introduces as consumer utility maximization.

Which object assigns probabilities to the possible values of a random variable?

Options
Its distribution
Its derivative
Its logarithm

The distribution of a random variable gives the probability of each value or range of values (M4).

Skill check (rung 1): a lottery pays 0 CU or 100 CU with probability 1/2 each. Under \(u(x) = x^{2}\), what is its expected utility, in CU squared?

Answer
\(\tfrac12 \cdot 0^{2} + \tfrac12 \cdot 100^{2} = 5000\) CU squared. This is the expected-utility sum of Definition 1.3 in L01.

Skill check (rung 2): the logit choice rule gives the probability of choosing option A over option B as \(P(A) = 1/(1 + e^{-z})\), where \(\kappa > 0\) is the choice precision and \(\mathrm{CE}_A\) and \(\mathrm{CE}_B\) are the certainty equivalents of the two options (Lesson L02, Definition 1.2), measured in cents. Which expression is the logit term \(z\)?

Options
\(\kappa(\mathrm{CE}_A - \mathrm{CE}_B)/10\,000\)
\(\kappa(\mathrm{CE}_A - \mathrm{CE}_B)\)
\(\kappa(\mathrm{CE}_A - \mathrm{CE}_B)/100\)

\(z = \kappa(\mathrm{CE}_A - \mathrm{CE}_B)/10\,000\). The certainty equivalents are in cents and 10\,000 cents is 100 CU, so the difference is measured in units of 100 CU, which is the scale the project page uses for its choice probability. Rung 2 asks you to fill in the log-likelihood term built from this \(z\).

Lessons

Eight lessons of seven study hours each, which is 56 hours in total. The lessons are sequenced, and later lessons use earlier ones. Lessons 1 and 2 give the expected-utility model and the risk measures that later lessons use. Lessons 3 and 4 give a parametric utility and a dominance test. Lessons 5 to 7 cover recursive preferences, reference dependence and probability weighting. Lesson 8 compares four models on one gamble. Each lesson page follows the same structure: graded claims, a runnable lab, a problem set with worked solutions, and a retrieval quiz that feeds your review deck.

L01 · 7 hExpected utilityFinite lotteries, the expected value and the expected utility of a finite lottery under a Bernoulli utility, the linearity of expected utility in the probabilities, and the invariance of the ranking under positive affine transformations.
L02 · 7 hRisk aversion and the certainty equivalentThe certainty equivalent of a concave utility, the proof that it does not exceed the expected value, the Arrow–Pratt measures, and the second-order approximation of the risk premium for small gambles.
L03 · 7 hConstant relative risk aversionThe CRRA utility family, its logarithmic limit, the certainty equivalent in whole cents, and the homogeneity of the certainty equivalent.
L04 · 7 hStochastic dominanceFirst-order and second-order stochastic dominance for finite lotteries, the proofs of the finite-support results, and a check of each verdict against expected-utility rankings.
L05 · 7 hRecursive Preferences and the Elasticity of Intertemporal SubstitutionA two-period Epstein–Zin value with a risky second period, its elasticity of intertemporal substitution, and its relation to the CRRA expected-utility value, which is a power transform with the same ranking when the elasticity is the reciprocal of the risk-aversion coefficient.
L06 · 7 hProspect Theory and Loss AversionA reference-dependent value function over gains and losses, the sign change of a gamble's value at the loss-aversion threshold, and what is not settled about the size of loss aversion.
L07 · 7 hProbability WeightingA probability-weighting function, its endpoints and its overweighting of small probabilities, and an exhibited pair in which the weighted value ranks a dominated lottery higher.
L08 · 7 hAmbiguity Aversion and the Four-Model ComparisonMaxmin expected utility over a stated set of priors, the two-urn comparison, and the certainty equivalents of one gamble under four preference models.

The project ladder

The module ends in the preference-elicitation project. Rungs 1 and 2 scaffold the skills that the placement check tests. To skip them, you must reach the placement bar and answer both skill checks.

Worked exampleA fully solved and annotated estimation step for one generated participant, with the certainty equivalents under the two models and the choice-likelihood term for one pair, and a reason at every step.

Completion problemThe log-likelihood contribution of one choice, with its sign and its logit term removed for you to fill in. Graded instantly in your browser.

Independent projectWrite elicit.py to the interface in the project spec, design an instrument of at least 12 pairs with at least one mixed-sign lottery, and write a report on the estimates and their uncertainty. Graded by the autograder and the rubric. Project page.

StretchAn open-ended extension with no ceiling, for example a second model of the choice noise with a stated comparison, or a probability-weighted value added to the engine, suitable for your portfolio.

Data status

No real data is used in E3. No dataset is named for this unit. The labs of Lessons 1 to 3 compute from fixed lotteries written in their code, with exact fractions in Lesson 1 and in the gamble's mean and variance in Lesson 2, and with floating-point arithmetic for the certainty equivalents in Lessons 2 and 3. They draw no random numbers. The project's autograder responses are SIMULATED, and the project page labels them so. A participant's own questionnaire answers may be personal data, and the project page states that nothing on it sends them anywhere.
