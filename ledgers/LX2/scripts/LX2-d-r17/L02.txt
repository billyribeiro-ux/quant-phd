 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks gpo1997united loc=Dirks p. 654; O'Hagan p. 652}} Trading on information that other market participants lack is not, under the two opinions read here, a violation in itself. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks gpo1997united loc=Dirks pp. 654, 660; O'Hagan p. 652}} Liability follows from a duty, and the three tests in this lesson locate the duty in one of three sources: a fiduciary relationship with the corporation and its shareholders, a breach by the insider who gave the information, or a deception practised on the source of the information. This lesson states each test with its printed page, applies the three tests to eight authored patterns, and shows which element decides each pattern. The lab does not say how a court would rule.
This lesson is an educational reading of the cited opinions. It is not legal advice. A pattern labelled HYPOTHETICAL is a fact pattern written for the lab, not a case record. It relies on two admitted opinions and on the statute text that one of them quotes. Each quoted passage, and each holding stated in a claim span, carries its registry key and the printed page of the opinion it comes from.
1The provision and the question it asks
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks gpo1997united loc=O'Hagan pp. 651-652; Dirks p. 651 n. 6}} Section 10(b) of the [href:L01.html#def-exchange-act-1934]Securities Exchange Act of 1934 is the provision that the two opinions apply, together with Rule 10b–5, which this lesson uses by name and does not read. Dirks v. SEC quotes its operative text in a footnote:
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 651 n. 6}} “To use or employ, in connection with the purchase or sale of any security registered on a national securities exchange or any security not so registered, any manipulative or deceptive device or contrivance in contravention of such rules and regulations as the Commission may prescribe as necessary or appropriate in the public interest or for the protection of investors.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 651}} The later opinion in United States v. O'Hagan states the limit that matters here: liability under Rule 10b–5 “does not extend beyond conduct encompassed by § 10(b)'s prohibition.” The question for each doctrine below is therefore whether a trade involves a deceptive device, and the answer depends on whose duty the trader breached.
The sources use the phrase material nonpublic information without defining “material” in the passages read for this lesson. This lesson does not define it. Every pattern in the lab states materiality as a fact. The lesson also uses scienter, which {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 663 n. 23}} footnote 23 of Dirks describes as “a mental state embracing intent to deceive, manipulate, or defraud”. The lab does not check scienter.
2The classical theory and the insider
 Definition 2.1 (insider-trading doctrine, classical theory)
The insider-trading doctrine {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks gpo1997united loc=pp. 651-652, 654}} in its classical form is the rule that a person with a fiduciary relationship to the corporation violates § 10(b) and Rule 10b–5 by trading in the corporation's securities on information that is material and nonpublic, without disclosing it first. O'Hagan states the classical theory as follows:
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=pp. 651-652}} “Under the ‘traditional’ or ‘classical theory’ of insider trading liability, § 10(b) and Rule 10b–5 are violated when a corporate insider trades in the securities of his corporation on the basis of material, nonpublic information.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 652}} “The classical theory applies not only to officers, directors, and other permanent insiders of a corporation, but also to attorneys, accountants, consultants, and others who temporarily become fiduciaries of a corporation.” The lab reads the role issuer_insider to include a temporary fiduciary (Assumption A3, before Lab 1), and the pattern must state that the person is one.
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 654}} “[A]n insider will be liable under Rule 10b-5 for inside trading only where he fails to disclose material nonpublic information before trading on it and thus makes ‘secret profits.’” The same page states the source of the duty. The opinion says that {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 654}} “a duty to disclose under § 10(b) does not arise from the mere possession of nonpublic market information,” and that “[s]uch a duty arises rather from the existence of a fiduciary relationship.” Possession of the information is therefore not the source of the duty; the fiduciary relationship is. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 672}} The dissent places the same duty in “the relationship of trust and confidence between the insider and the shareholder” (Blackmun, J., dissenting, from p. 667).
 Example 2.2 (pattern P1, HYPOTHETICAL)
A director of the issuer buys shares after learning of an unannounced acquisition agreement. The pattern's flags state that the information is material and nonpublic, and that nothing was public before the trade. The pattern does not say that the shares are the issuer's, and the classifier does not test that element. Lab 1, below, applies one of three branches to each pattern, and a branch fires when every one of its elements is stated. Each of the four checks passes, so Lab 1 fires the classical branch on P1, with the key gpo1997united at pp. 651-652.
3Tipping and the tippee's duty
Dirks states the difference between an insider and a tippee, the person who receives the information from an insider (the insider who gives it is the tipper): {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 655}} “Unlike insiders who have independent fiduciary duties to both the corporation and its shareholders, the typical tippee has no such relationships.”
 Definition 3.1 (tipper–tippee liability)
Tipper–tippee liability is the liability of a tippee for trading on material nonpublic information received from an insider. In Dirks it is derivative: {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=pp. 659-660}} the tippee's duty comes from the insider's breach, and it arises only when the insider has breached by disclosing the information and the tippee knows or should know of the breach (p. 660). The opinion states the test in one sentence:
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 660}} “Thus, a tippee assumes a fiduciary duty to the shareholders of a corporation not to trade on material nonpublic information only when the insider has breached his fiduciary duty to the shareholders by disclosing the information to the tippee and the tippee knows or should know that there has been a breach.”
 Assumption A1 (not read from the opinion)
A value of none for the tipper's benefit means that the insider received no direct or indirect personal benefit from the disclosure. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 662}} The opinion states the benefit test as “the test is whether the insider personally will benefit, directly or indirectly, from his disclosure.” It does not state this reading of a pattern's flag. The lesson takes it as its own reading of that test, and the proof in Pr1 (§7) uses it only under that name.
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 659}} “Thus, the tippee's duty to disclose or abstain is derivative from that of the insider's duty.” {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 657}} The opinion says that the SEC's theory of tippee liability “appears rooted in the idea that the antifraud provisions require equal information among all traders,” and that this idea conflicts with the principle that only some persons, under some circumstances, are barred from trading while in possession of material nonpublic information. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 659}} “[T]he conclusion that recipients of inside information do not invariably acquire a duty to disclose or abstain does not mean that such tippees always are free to trade on the information.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 662}} The breach itself depends in large part on purpose. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 662}} “Whether disclosure is a breach of duty therefore depends in large part on the purpose of the disclosure.” The test is stated next: “Thus, the test is whether the insider personally will benefit, directly or indirectly, from his disclosure. Absent some personal gain, there has been no breach of duty to stockholders. And absent a breach by the insider, there is no derivative breach.” The personal benefit that counts may be financial or not: {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 663}} “a direct or indirect personal benefit from the disclosure, such as a pecuniary gain or a reputational benefit that will translate into future earnings.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 664}} “[T]here may be a relationship between the insider and the recipient that suggests a quid pro quo from the latter, or an intention to benefit the particular recipient. The elements of fiduciary duty and exploitation of nonpublic information also exist when an insider makes a gift of confidential information to a trading relative or friend.” The same page adds that the benefit question is not always easy for courts: {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 664}} “Determining whether an insider personally benefits from a particular disclosure, a question of fact, will not always be easy for courts.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 668}} Counter-evidence: Justice Blackmun, with whom Justices Brennan and Marshall join, dissented (from p. 667). The dissent says that the personal-benefit requirement “engrafts a special motivational requirement on the fiduciary duty doctrine” and “excuses a knowing and intentional violation of an insider's duty to shareholders if the insider does not act from a motive of personal gain.” {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 674}} It states that “[p]ersonal gain is not an element of the breach of this duty.” {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 676 n. 13}} Its footnote 13 says that “[u]nder the Court's somewhat cynical view, gifts involve personal gain,” and asks why the benefit an insider obtains from exposing a fraud differs from the benefit of a gift to a friend or relative. The benefit test in this section is the majority's; the dissent is the counter-evidence that it is contested.
 Example 3.2 (patterns P2 to P5, HYPOTHETICAL)
P2 is a tip to a friend with no payment and no gift. The tipper's benefit is none, so Lab 1 fails the benefit element at p. 662 and does not fire the tippee branch. P3 is a tip for a cash payment, and P4 is a gift to a brother-in-law who trades, with no payment. Both state a personal benefit, and Lab 1 fires the tippee branch on both, with the key gpo1983dirks at p. 660. P5 is a tip for a cash payment to a manager with no reason to know of the payment. The benefit element passes, and the knowledge element fails at p. 660, so the tippee branch does not fire.
 Remark 3.3
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 662}} The opinion states the benefit test for whether a tip is a breach. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 660}} The tippee's knowledge is a separate condition, which the opinion states at p. 660, and the lab checks it as its own element. A pattern can state a benefit and still fail on knowledge, as P5 does.
4The misappropriation theory
 Definition 4.1 (misappropriation theory)
The misappropriation theory {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 652}} holds that a person commits fraud in connection with a securities transaction when the person misappropriates confidential information for trading and breaches a duty owed to the source of that information. O'Hagan states it as follows:
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 652}} “The ‘misappropriation theory’ holds that a person commits fraud ‘in connection with’ a securities transaction, and thereby violates § 10(b) and Rule 10b–5, when he misappropriates confidential information for securities trading purposes, in breach of a duty owed to the source of the information.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 652}} “In lieu of premising liability on a fiduciary relationship between company insider and purchaser or seller of the company's stock, the misappropriation theory premises liability on a fiduciary-turned-trader's deception of those who entrusted him with access to confidential information.” {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=pp. 652, 653 n. 5}} The trader need not be an insider of the company whose stock he trades. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 653 n. 5}} The opinion's footnote 5 says that the trader was not an insider of that company, and that “[u]nder the misappropriation theory, [his] securities trading does not escape Exchange Act sanction.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 654}} The opinion treats deception through nondisclosure as the core of the theory. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 654}} “Deception through nondisclosure is central to the theory of liability for which the Government seeks recognition.” The opinion then states the limit that the lab's last check uses: {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 655}} “if the fiduciary discloses to the source that he plans to trade on the nonpublic information, there is no ‘deceptive device’ and thus no § 10(b) violation—although the fiduciary-turned-trader may remain liable under state law for breach of a duty of loyalty.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 656}} The fraud is consummated when, without disclosure to his principal, the fiduciary “uses the information to purchase or sell securities.” {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 656}} The opinion finds the “in connection with” element satisfied because “[t]he securities transaction and the breach of duty thus coincide.” The opinion also limits the theory: {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 656}} “The theory does not catch all conceivable forms of fraud involving confidential information; rather, it catches fraudulent means of capitalizing on such information through securities transactions.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 680}} Counter-evidence: Justice Thomas, with whom The Chief Justice joins, concurred in the judgment in part and dissented in part. He states that “the Commission’s misappropriation theory fails to provide a coherent and consistent interpretation” of the requirement that a deceptive device be used “in connection with the purchase or sale of any security.” {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 679}} Justice Scalia, concurring in part and dissenting in part, does not agree with Part II of the opinion, and says that the statutory language “must be construed to require the manipulation or deception of a party to a securities transaction.” The misappropriation test in this section is the majority's, and both separate opinions dispute its reach.
 Example 4.2 (patterns P6 and P7, HYPOTHETICAL)
P6 is an associate at an accounting firm who reads a client's confidential file on a planned acquisition, buys shares of the target, and does not tell the client. Lab 1 fires the misappropriation branch on P6, with the key gpo1997united at p. 652. P7 is the same associate, who tells the client in writing that he plans to trade, and then trades. The disclosure element fails at p. 655, so the branch does not fire on P7.
 Remark 4.3
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 655 n. 7}} The opinion's footnote 7 covers a trader who owes duties to two entities and discloses to only one: “the trader may still be liable under the misappropriation theory.” Lab 1 does not model that case. Its misappropriation branch has one source of duty.
 Assumptions of Lab 1 (numbered; A1 is stated in §3)
A2 (not read from the opinions). The facts that each pattern states are true. The lab reads materiality, confidentiality, benefit, knowledge and disclosure as stated, and does not assess them.
A3 (not read from the opinions). A temporary fiduciary is stated in the pattern with the role issuer_insider, and the classifier does not test whether the person is one. The classical theory applies to temporary fiduciaries as quoted at p. 652 (§2).
Lab 1 · Insider-trading fact-pattern classifier (HYPOTHETICAL patterns)
Stated before running. Under the rules as coded, four of the eight HYPOTHETICAL patterns state every element of a branch: P1, P3, P4 and P6. The other four fail one element: P2 at the benefit element, P5 at the knowledge element, P7 at the disclosure-to-source element, and P8 at the duty element. Each failing row prints the element, with the key and printed page it rests on. The benefit element reads Assumption A1 (§3), and the facts are read as stated under Assumptions A2 and A3 (before the lab).
"""LX2 lab L02: a rule-based classifier for eight insider-trading fact patterns, with the citation for each decision.
HYPOTHETICAL. The eight patterns are authored for this lab. None describes a real person, firm, security, filing or
court case, and nothing on the page says that a pattern happened.
Standard library only. No randomness, no network and no data. The classifier applies, in order, the elements that the
lesson states from two admitted opinions: Dirks v. SEC (key gpo1983dirks) and United States v. O'Hagan (key
gpo1997united). For each pattern it stops at the first element the pattern does not state, and reports that element
with the registry key and the printed page it rests on. A pattern that states every element of a branch fires it.
The three branches and the elements each one checks, in order:
 classical (role issuer_insider): material information; nonpublic information; a trade (the classifier does not test
 that the security is the issuer's); no public disclosure before the trade.
 tippee (role tippee): material and nonpublic information from an insider; a personal benefit to the tipper; the
 tippee traded; the tippee knew or should have known of the breach.
 misappropriation (role outsider): confidential information; a duty owed to its source; the information was used to
 trade; the source was not told of the trade before it was made.
A pattern with role outsider and no duty owed to its source fails the duty check, and no branch fires.
Materiality, confidentiality, benefit, knowledge and disclosure are facts that each pattern states. The classifier
reads them and does not assess them. It does not assess scienter, which the lesson treats as a separate element. A
fired branch means that the stated facts contain every element of the branch under the rules coded here. It does not
say how a court would rule.
"""

def branch_checks(p: Pattern) -> tuple[str, tuple[Check, ...]]:
 """The branch a pattern is tested against, and its elements in the order the classifier checks them."""
 if p.role == "issuer_insider":
 return "classical", (
 Check("material information", p.material, KEY_O_HAGAN, "pp. 651-652"),
 Check("nonpublic information", p.nonpublic, KEY_O_HAGAN, "pp. 651-652"),
 Check("a trade", p.traded, KEY_O_HAGAN, "pp. 651-652"),
 Check("no public disclosure before the trade", not p.public_before_trade, KEY_DIRKS, "p. 654"),
 )
 if p.role == "tippee":
 benefit_ok = p.tipper_benefit in PERSONAL_BENEFITS
 benefit_locator = PERSONAL_BENEFITS[p.tipper_benefit] if benefit_ok else NO_BENEFIT_LOCATOR
 return "tippee", (
 Check("material nonpublic information from an insider", p.material and p.nonpublic, KEY_DIRKS, "p. 654"),
 Check(f"tipper's personal benefit ({p.tipper_benefit})", benefit_ok, KEY_DIRKS, benefit_locator),
 Check("tippee traded", p.traded, KEY_DIRKS, "p. 660"),
 Check("tippee knew or should have known of the breach", p.knew_or_should_have_known, KEY_DIRKS, "p. 660"),
 )
 if p.role == "outsider":
 return "misappropriation", (
 Check("confidential information", p.confidential, KEY_O_HAGAN, "p. 652"),
 Check("duty owed to the source of the information", p.duty_to_source, KEY_O_HAGAN, "p. 652"),
 Check("information used to trade", p.traded, KEY_O_HAGAN, "p. 656"),
 Check("source not told of the trade before it was made", not p.source_told_of_trade, KEY_O_HAGAN, "p. 655"),
 )
 raise ValueError(f"{p.label}: unknown role {p.role!r}")
def classify(p: Pattern) -> Decision:
 """The first element the pattern does not state decides the outcome; if none fails, the branch fires."""
 branch, checks = branch_checks(p)
 for check in checks:
 if not check.passed:
 return Decision(p.label, branch, False, f"fails: {check.element}", check.key, check.locator)
 key, locator = BRANCH_RULE[branch]
 return Decision(p.label, branch, True, "all elements stated", key, locator)

 """Print each pattern's facts, then the decision for each one, then the two lists of labels."""

 print("Read as stated, not assessed: materiality, confidentiality, benefit, knowledge and disclosure.")
 print("Scienter is not checked.")
main()

The table shows which element decided each pattern under the rules as coded, and nothing more. The patterns are HYPOTHETICAL: the lab authors each fact, and no pattern describes a real person or case. The lab reads materiality, confidentiality, benefit, knowledge and disclosure as stated. It does not assess them, and it does not check scienter, {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 663 n. 23}} a “mental state embracing intent to deceive, manipulate, or defraud”. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks gpo1997united loc=Lab 1 output, rows P1 to P8}} The printed result is four fired branches (P1, P3, P4, P6) and four unfired (P2, P5, P7, P8), and each unfired pattern names the element it lacks. A fired branch means the stated facts contain every element of that branch under these rules. It does not say that a court would impose liability. P2 is not fired because, under Assumption A1 (§3), the tipper's benefit is none, and {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 664}} whether a benefit exists is, under Dirks, a question of fact that the lab cannot decide.
5Where the canon breaks
The three tests are the lesson's reading of the two opinions, and the reading has five limits.
Scienter is outside the lab. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 663 n. 23}} Scienter, “a mental state embracing intent to deceive, manipulate, or defraud,” is “an independent element of a Rule 10b-5 violation.” Without the lab's check, a pattern that states every element of a branch can still fail on intent, so a fired branch is not a finding of liability.
Materiality is a stated fact. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 662}} “[I]t may not be clear—either to the corporate insider or to the recipient analyst—whether the information will be viewed as material nonpublic information.” The lab accepts the pattern's statement. Each pattern's materiality flag is either true or false, so the lab cannot represent an uncertain materiality.
Benefit is one label. The lab records one value for the tipper's benefit. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 664}} Dirks says that “objective facts and circumstances that often justify such an inference” of benefit, and names a relationship suggesting a quid pro quo and an intention to benefit the recipient (p. 664). The lab has no field for either fact, so a pattern that differs from P3 only in the relationship or the intention would print the same row.
The misappropriation check reads one source of duty. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 655 n. 7}} Footnote 7 of O'Hagan (p. 655 n. 7) covers a trader with duties to two entities, which Lab 1 does not model. Remark 4.3 gives the limit.
One trade per pattern. Each pattern has one trade and one trader. The lab does not model a sequence of trades, a market, or price effects, so none of its results speak to those.
6Misconceptions
“Holding material nonpublic information is itself the offence.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 654}} Under the test at p. 654, a duty to disclose or abstain does not arise from mere possession of nonpublic information. It arises from a fiduciary relationship. Pattern P8 in Lab 1 overhears confidential information, has no duty to its source, and is not fired.
“A tippee always inherits the insider's duty.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 660}} The tippee assumes the duty only when the insider breached it and the tippee knows or should know of the breach (p. 660). Pattern P5 states a personal benefit and fails the knowledge element, so Lab 1 does not fire the tippee branch on it.
“Any tip to a friend is a breach.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 662}} The test is personal benefit: “Absent some personal gain, there has been no breach of duty to stockholders” (p. 662). Pattern P2 is a tip to a friend with no benefit, and it is not fired. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 664}} A gift of confidential information to a trading relative or friend is a different case. P4 is fired on it: the gift benefit element cites p. 664, and the decision row prints p. 660.
“Misappropriation requires a duty to the issuer of the stock traded.”
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 652}} The misappropriation theory requires a duty owed to the source of the information (p. 652). {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 653 n. 5}} O'Hagan was an outsider to the company whose stock he traded (p. 653 n. 5). Pattern P6 is an outsider case, and Lab 1 fires it.

Proof
 Pr1 · A tippee whose tipper received no benefit has no tippee duty
Assumption A1 of §3 is a reading, not a sentence of the opinion: the classifier's value none for the tipper's benefit means that the insider received no direct or indirect personal benefit. Assuming A1, and using only the quoted text of Dirks, prove that a tippee whose tipper received no personal benefit has no duty to disclose or abstain under the tippee test of Definition 3.1. State every hypothesis you use.
 Solution
Hypothesis H1 (p. 660): a tippee assumes the duty only when the insider has breached the duty by disclosing the information, and the tippee knows or should know of the breach. Hypothesis H2 (p. 662): absent some personal gain there has been no breach of duty to stockholders. Hypothesis H3 (p. 662): absent a breach by the insider there is no derivative breach. Assumption A1 of §3 applies. Proof: suppose the tipper received no personal benefit. By A1 and H2, the tipper did not breach the duty to stockholders by disclosing. By H3, there is no derivative breach. H1 says the tippee assumes the duty only when the insider has breached, and that condition fails, so the tippee does not assume the duty. Only the necessity direction of H1 is used. The converse, that a benefit creates the duty, is not claimed. Grade C, with the keys and pages above.
 Pr2 · The classifier fires a branch if and only if every check passes
Prove that, in the code of Lab 1, a branch fires for a pattern if and only if every check of that branch passes for it. Say which lines of the function classify the proof uses.
 Solution
The loop in classify returns a not-fired decision at the first check that does not pass. If the loop finishes without returning, every check has passed, and the function returns the fired decision. So a not-fired result implies some check failed, and a fired result implies every check passed. Conversely, if every check passes, the loop never returns early, and the fired decision is returned. The proof uses the loop's early return and the final return after the loop. It concerns the code only. It shows nothing about whether a court applies the same test. Grade C, with the basis named as the classify function in Lab 1's code.
 Pr3 · No trade, no tippee branch
Show that the tippee branch cannot fire for a pattern in which the tippee did not trade, whatever the other facts. Name the check that does the work and its locator.
 Solution
By problem Pr2, the tippee branch fires only if its check “tippee traded” passes. If the pattern states that the tippee did not trade, that check fails, whatever the other checks show. The check cites gpo1983dirks at p. 660, the passage in which the tippee's duty is a duty not to trade on material nonpublic information. The tippee branch therefore cannot fire for a non-trading tippee.

Research
 R1 · Facts behind the benefit label
The lab records one label for the tipper's benefit. List the objective facts that Dirks gives for inferring a personal benefit, with a page for each, and say which of them the lab cannot record.
 Solution
At p. 663, the opinion gives a direct or indirect personal benefit, such as a pecuniary gain or a reputational benefit that will translate into future earnings. At p. 664, it gives a relationship between the insider and the recipient that suggests a quid pro quo, an intention to benefit the particular recipient, and a gift of confidential information to a trading relative or friend. The lab records the benefit as one label. It records a gift as a value of the label, but it has no field for the relationship or for the intention, so it cannot test either fact.
 R2 · Elements a court must decide
The lab reads materiality, benefit and knowledge as stated, and it does not check scienter. For materiality, benefit and scienter, name the page on which Dirks addresses the question, and quote the words that do so.
 Solution
Materiality: at p. 662 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 662}} the opinion says it “may not be clear” whether information “will be viewed as material nonpublic information.” Benefit: at p. 664 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 664}} it calls the question “a question of fact.” Scienter: at p. 663 n. 23 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 663 n. 23}} it calls scienter “an independent element of a Rule 10b-5 violation.” The lab records no evidence on any of the three, so it cannot decide them.
 R3 · Confidential information used outside a trade
Write a pattern in which a consultant uses a client's confidential information in a memo sold to subscribers, and no trade occurs. Say which check fails and what O'Hagan says about this kind of case.

The pattern states confidential information, a duty to the client, and no securities trade. 9Retrieval quiz
Under the classical theory as O'Hagan states it, which trader violates § 10(b) and Rule 10b–5 by trading on material nonpublic information?
Options
 A corporate insider, or a temporary fiduciary of the corporation, who trades in its securities on that information
 Anyone who holds material nonpublic information when the trade is made
 Any tippee who receives a tip, whatever the tipper gained
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=pp. 651-652; p. 652}} O'Hagan, pp. 651-652, states the classical theory for a corporate insider, and p. 652 extends it to temporary fiduciaries. {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 654}} Dirks, p. 654, says that mere possession does not create the duty.
Under Dirks at p. 660, when does a tippee assume the insider's duty?
Options
 As soon as the tippee receives material nonpublic information from an insider
 Only when the insider breached by disclosing the information, and the tippee knows or should know of the breach
 Only when the tippee pays the insider for the information
The sentence at p. 660 uses “only when” and states both conditions. Payment is one form of personal benefit, and a reputational benefit also counts (p. 663), so payment is not a condition of the tippee's duty.
A tipper gives a friend a tip and receives nothing. What does Dirks at p. 662 say about the tippee's duty?
Options
 There is no breach of duty to stockholders, so no derivative breach arises
 There is a breach, because the tip was made to a friend
 There is a breach only if the friend trades in the same quarter
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 662}} Dirks, p. 662: “Absent some personal gain, there has been no breach of duty to stockholders. And absent a breach by the insider, there is no derivative breach.” The friendship is not the test. The benefit is.
Under O'Hagan at p. 655, what follows if the fiduciary discloses to the source that he plans to trade?
Options
 The trade is still a violation, because the trader owes a duty to the market
 There is no deceptive device and so no § 10(b) violation, though state-law duties may remain
 The disclosure cures nothing, because the duty runs to the buyer
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1997united loc=p. 655}} O'Hagan, p. 655: “if the fiduciary discloses to the source that he plans to trade on the nonpublic information, there is no ‘deceptive device’ and thus no § 10(b) violation—although the fiduciary-turned-trader may remain liable under state law for breach of a duty of loyalty.”
What does Dirks, p. 663 n. 23, say about scienter in a Rule 10b-5 case?
Options
 Scienter is presumed once a trade is shown
 Scienter is an independent element of a Rule 10b-5 violation
 Scienter is not relevant to tipper liability
 {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 663 n. 23}} Footnote 23 calls scienter “an independent element of a Rule 10b-5 violation.” {{CLAIM grade=C cite=gpo1983dirks loc=p. 663}} The text at p. 663, to which footnote 23 is attached, says scienter is relevant in some cases to whether the tipper has violated his duty, so the third option is wrong.
