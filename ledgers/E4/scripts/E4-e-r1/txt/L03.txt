00020 An equilibrium is a claim about prices, and it is also a claim about outcomes. The efficiency result (Proposition 3.4) says that, in the economy of Lesson L02, no feasible reallocation (Definition 3.1) can make some agent better off without making anyone worse off, at a competitive equilibrium. It states its assumptions and proves both parts. Part (a) is that statement, in the terms of Definition 3.2. Lemma 2.4 makes every equilibrium bundle
00021 interior
00022 , with every entry strictly positive. Part (b) says that, at an equilibrium, the rate at which an agent trades one state's good for another's at the same utility (Definition 3.3) equals the price ratio for both agents. A grid search is a check, not a proof.
00023 1Feasibility, Pareto improvements and marginal rates
00024 The economy is the one of Lesson L02: \(S\) states, probabilities \(\pi_s\), two agents with endowments \(e^1, e^2\), aggregate \(\bar e = e^1 + e^2\), and utilities \(U_i\). The assumptions used in this lesson are A1, A3 and A4. A1 is stated in Lesson L01, and A3 and A4 in Lesson L02; they keep their numbers here. The symbol \(e^i\) with an agent superscript is an endowment, as in Lesson L02. The security \(e_s\) of Lesson L01 (Definition 1.3) is used only in the proof of part (b) of Proposition 3.4, where it is named.
00025 Definition 3.1 (feasible allocation)
00026 A
00027 feasible allocation
00028 is a pair \((y^1, y^2)\) of bundles \(y^i \in \R^{S}_{+}\) with \(y^1 + y^2 = \bar e\). The condition \(y^1 + y^2 = \bar e\) says that the whole aggregate is distributed, with no good destroyed. Research problem R1 treats the case with free disposal.
00029 Definition 3.2 (Pareto improvement)
00030 Let \((x^1, x^2)\) be a feasible allocation. A feasible allocation \((y^1, y^2)\) is a
00031 Pareto improvement
00032 of it when \(U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^i)\) for both agents, with strict inequality for at least one. An allocation with no Pareto improvement is Pareto efficient, the notion of E1 applied to this economy.
00033 Definition 3.3 (marginal rate of substitution between states)
00034 Let \(u_i\) be differentiable as in A4, and let \(x\) be a strictly positive bundle (\(x_r > 0\) for every state \(r\)). A bundle with a zero entry has \(U_i = -\infty\) under A4, so the partial derivatives below are defined only at strictly positive bundles. The derivative \(u_i'\) is positive on \((0, \infty)\), as shown in the proof of Proposition 3.4 (b), so the ratio is well defined. The
00035 marginal rate of substitution
00036 of agent \(i\) between states \(s\) and \(t\) at \(x\) is
00037 \[
00038 \mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x) = \frac{\partial U_i / \partial x_s}{\partial U_i / \partial x_t} = \frac{\pi_s\, u_i'(x_s)}{\pi_t\, u_i'(x_t)} .
00039 \]
00040 It is the number of units of the good in state \(t\) that agent \(i\) would give up for one more unit in state \(s\), at the same utility.
00041 2The first welfare theorem
00042 The statement below is the
00043 first welfare theorem
00044 for the economy of Definition 2.3. Its name is conventional. The result is a proposition that this lesson proves.
00045 Proposition 3.4 (first welfare theorem)
00046 Assume A1, A3 and A4, and let \((q^*, (x^{1*}, x^{2*}))\) be an Arrow–Debreu equilibrium in the sense of Definition 2.3, with prices \(q^* \in (0, \infty)^{S}\). Then:
00047 (a) no feasible allocation is a Pareto improvement of \((x^{1*}, x^{2*})\); and
00048 (b) for every agent \(i\) and every pair of states \(s\) and \(t\),
00049 \[
00050 \mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x^{i*}) = \frac{q^*_s}{q^*_t}.
00051 \]
00052 In particular, the two agents have the same marginal rate of substitution between any two states.
00053 Proof of (a)
00054 Write \(w_i = q^* \cdot e^i\), so that \(w_1 + w_2 = q^* \cdot \bar e\). Here \(w_i > 0\), because \(q^*\) is strictly positive and \(e^i\) is strictly positive by A3. Lemma 2.4 therefore gives that every entry of \(x^{i*}\) is strictly positive, so \(U_i(x^{i*})\) is finite, and \(q^* \cdot x^{i*} = w_i\) by Proposition 2.5. Suppose \((y^1, y^2)\) is a Pareto improvement.
00055 Step 1. If \(U_i(y^i) > U_i(x^{i*})\), then \(q^* \cdot y^i > w_i\). Otherwise \(y^i\) lies in the budget set \(B_i(q^*)\) and has a strictly higher utility than the demand \(x^{i*}\), which is impossible.
00056 Step 2. If \(U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^{i*})\), then \(q^* \cdot y^i \ge w_i\). Suppose instead \(q^* \cdot y^i < w_i\). Since \(U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^{i*}) > -\infty\), the bundle \(y^i\) has positive entries. Here \(\iota = (1, \ldots, 1)\) is the vector of ones. Choose \(\delta > 0\) with \(q^* \cdot (y^i + \delta \iota) \le w_i\). Then \(y^i + \delta \iota \in B_i(q^*)\), and \(U_i(y^i + \delta \iota) > U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^{i*})\) by A4 and A1, which contradicts the demand property of \(x^{i*}\).
00057 Step 3. Since the allocation is a Pareto improvement, some agent \(k\) satisfies \(U_k(y^k) > U_k(x^{k*})\), so \(q^* \cdot y^k > w_k\) by Step 1. The other agent \(j\) satisfies \(q^* \cdot y^j \ge w_j\) by Step 2. Adding,
00058 \[
00059 q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) > w_1 + w_2 = q^* \cdot \bar e .
00060 \]
00061 But \(y^1 + y^2 = \bar e\) by feasibility, so the left side equals \(q^* \cdot \bar e\), which is a contradiction.
00062 Proof of (b)
00063 Fix an agent \(i\), two states \(s\) and \(t\), and write \(x = x^{i*}\), which is strictly positive by Lemma 2.4 (its wealth is positive, as in the proof of (a)). Define the bundle change \(d = e_s / q^*_s - e_t / q^*_t\), where \(e_s\) is the Arrow–Debreu security of Definition 1.3. Then \(q^* \cdot d = 1 - 1 = 0\), so \(x + \varepsilon d\) costs the same as \(x\) for every \(\varepsilon\). For \(|\varepsilon|\) small enough, \(x + \varepsilon d\) is strictly positive, so it lies in the budget set. Since \(u_i\) is differentiable, \(\varepsilon \mapsto U_i(x + \varepsilon d)\) has derivative at 0 equal to
00064 \[
00065 G = \frac{\pi_s u_i'(x_s)}{q^*_s} - \frac{\pi_t u_i'(x_t)}{q^*_t} .
00066 \]
00067 If \(G > 0\), then \(U_i(x + \varepsilon d) > U_i(x)\) for small \(\varepsilon > 0\), and the bundle is affordable, which contradicts the demand property. If \(G < 0\), the same holds for small \(\varepsilon < 0\). So \(G = 0\). The derivative \(u_i'(x_t)\) is positive. Indeed, \(u_i'\) is nonnegative because \(u_i\) is strictly increasing, and nonincreasing because \(u_i\) is concave. If \(u_i'(x_t) = 0\), then \(0 \le u_i'(y) \le 0\) for every \(y > x_t\), so \(u_i\) is constant on \((x_t, \infty)\), which contradicts strict monotonicity. Hence \(\pi_t u_i'(x_t) / q^*_t > 0\), and dividing \(G = 0\) by it gives \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x) = q^*_s / q^*_t\), which is (b). The two agents share the price ratio, so their marginal rates agree.
00068 Remark 3.5 (what each part uses)
00069 Part (a) uses A4's strict monotonicity, A1 and A3 through Lemma 2.4, and the positivity of the prices. It uses no derivative. Part (b) uses the differentiability in A4 and the strict monotonicity and concavity of A4, which show that \(u_i'\) is positive (proof of (b)). Its strictly positive equilibrium bundle is the one that Lemma 2.4 supplies for part (a), so part (b) adds no positivity hypothesis. A boundary allocation with a zero entry is outside (b), and the lesson does not assert the MRS condition there.
00070 Example 3.6 (the equilibrium of Lesson L02)
00071 Take the economy of Example 2.11, with \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)\), prices \(q^* = (\tfrac18, \tfrac1{32}, \tfrac1{16})\) and bundles \(x^{1*} = (\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) and \(x^{2*} = (\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\). With \(u_i = \ln\), Definition 3.3 gives \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x) = (\pi_s / x_s) / (\pi_t / x_t)\). States are numbered 1, 2 and 3, as in Example 2.11. For agent 1, \(\mathrm{MRS}_{12} = 4\), \(\mathrm{MRS}_{13} = 2\) and \(\mathrm{MRS}_{23} = \tfrac12\). For agent 2 the same three numbers hold, and they equal \(q^*_1 / q^*_2 = 4\), \(q^*_1 / q^*_3 = 2\) and \(q^*_2 / q^*_3 = \tfrac12\). Lab 3 Part A checks these equalities in exact arithmetic.
00072 Lab 3 · Marginal rates at the equilibrium, and a finite search for Pareto improvements
00073 The lab uses no data. It takes the economy of Example 2.11 and computes its equilibrium in exact Fractions. Before running it, the hypothesis is this. Part A prints the marginal rates \(4\), \(2\) and \(\tfrac12\) for both agents, which equal the price ratios, so each line reads True (Proposition 3.4 (b)). Part B searches a grid of 125 feasible allocations, with each agent's share of each state on the grid \(\{0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, 1\}\). At the equilibrium the search should find no Pareto improvement on the grid, which is consistent with Proposition 3.4 (a) but is only a finite check. At the endowment the search should find at least one improvement on the grid. Problem R2 gives an explicit improvement by hand, and the printed count is the evidence for the grid search. Comparisons use floating-point utilities with a tolerance of \(10^{-12}\). No random draws are used.
00074 """E4 L03 lab 1: marginal rates of substitution at an Arrow-Debreu equilibrium, and a finite Pareto search.
00075 Inputs are illustrative numbers, not data. This is the economy of Lesson L02: three states with probabilities PROBS,
00076 an aggregate endowment equal to the sum of ENDOWMENTS, and two agents. Agent i's expected utility of a bundle x is the
00077 sum over states of PROBS[s] times ln(x[s]). The equilibrium prices and demands are those of Lesson L02, which are
00078 computed here in exact Fractions.
00079 Part A: the marginal rate of substitution between states, (PROBS[s] / x[s]) / (PROBS[t] / x[t]), for each agent at
00080 the equilibrium, checked exactly against the price ratio q[s] / q[t]. States are numbered 1 to 3 in the printed output,
00081 as in the lesson, and indexed 0 to 2 in the code, so printed "states 1 2" is the code pair (0, 1).
00082 Part B: a finite search. Agent 1 receives the fraction f[s] of the aggregate in state s, with f[s] in the grid
00083 GRID, and agent 2 receives the rest. The search counts the grid allocations that make one agent strictly better off
00084 and no agent worse off than the equilibrium, and the same count against the endowment allocation.
00085 The utilities in Part B are floating-point. A comparison counts as worse when the new utility is below the reference by
00086 more than TOL, and as better when it is above the reference by more than TOL, with TOL = 1e-12.
00087 No random draws are used, so every run prints the same lines.
00088 """
00089 from __future__ import annotations
00090 import math
00091 from fractions import Fraction
00092 from itertools import product
00093 Payoff = tuple[Fraction, ...]
00094 PROBS: Payoff = (Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 4))
00095 ENDOWMENTS: tuple[Payoff, ...] = (
00096 (Fraction(3), Fraction(4), Fraction(1)),
00097 (Fraction(1), Fraction(4), Fraction(3)),
00098 )
00099 GRID: tuple[Fraction, ...] = (Fraction(0), Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(3, 4), Fraction(1))
00100 TOL = 1e-12
00101 def dot(a: Payoff, b: Payoff) -> Fraction:
00102 """The inner product of two vectors with one entry per state, as an exact Fraction."""
00103 return sum((x * y for x, y in zip(a, b, strict=True)), Fraction(0))
00104 def aggregate(endowments: tuple[Payoff, ...]) -> Payoff:
00105 """The aggregate endowment in each state."""
00106 n_states = len(endowments[0])
00107 return tuple(sum((e[s] for e in endowments), Fraction(0)) for s in range(n_states))
00108 def equilibrium(probabilities: Payoff, endowments: tuple[Payoff, ...]) -> tuple[Payoff, Payoff, Payoff]:
00109 """Prices q[s] = PROBS[s] / aggregate[s], and demands x[s] = PROBS[s] * w / q[s] with wealth w = q . endowment."""
00110 total = aggregate(endowments)
00111 prices = tuple(p / t for p, t in zip(probabilities, total, strict=True))
00112 demands = []
00113 for endowment in endowments:
00114 wealth = dot(prices, endowment)
00115 demands.append(tuple(p * wealth / q for p, q in zip(probabilities, prices, strict=True)))
00116 return prices, demands[0], demands[1]
00117 def utility(probabilities: Payoff, bundle: Payoff) -> float:
00118 """The expected log utility, the sum of PROBS[s] * ln(bundle[s]) as a float; a zero entry gives minus infinity."""
00119 total = 0.0
00120 for p, x in zip(probabilities, bundle, strict=True):
00121 if x == 0:
00122 return -math.inf
00123 total += float(p) * math.log(float(x))
00124 return total
00125 def improves(candidate: tuple[float, float], reference: tuple[float, float]) -> bool:
00126 """True when no agent is worse off by more than TOL and at least one agent is better off by more than TOL."""
00127 no_one_worse = all(c >= r - TOL for c, r in zip(candidate, reference, strict=True))
00128 someone_better = any(c > r + TOL for c, r in zip(candidate, reference, strict=True))
00129 return no_one_worse and someone_better
00130 def grid_allocations(total: Payoff) -> list[tuple[Payoff, Payoff]]:
00131 """Every allocation with agent 1 receiving GRID[k] of the aggregate in each state and agent 2 the rest."""
00132 found: list[tuple[Payoff, Payoff]] = []
00133 for fractions in product(GRID, repeat=len(total)):
00134 first = tuple(f * t for f, t in zip(fractions, total, strict=True))
00135 second = tuple((1 - f) * t for f, t in zip(fractions, total, strict=True))
00136 found.append((first, second))
00137 return found
00138 def main() -> None:
00139 """Print Parts A and B. Exact Fractions in Part A and integer counts in Part B make the output reproducible."""
00140 total = aggregate(ENDOWMENTS)
00141 prices, x1, x2 = equilibrium(PROBS, ENDOWMENTS)
00142 print("Part A: marginal rates of substitution at the equilibrium")
00143 print(f"equilibrium_prices {tuple(str(q) for q in prices)}")
00144 for left, right in ((0, 1), (0, 2), (1, 2)):
00145 price_ratio = prices[left] / prices[right]
00146 mrs = []
00147 for bundle in (x1, x2):
00148 mrs.append((PROBS[left] / bundle[left]) / (PROBS[right] / bundle[right]))
00149 same = mrs[0] == mrs[1] == price_ratio
00150 print(
00151 f"states {left + 1} {right + 1} mrs_agent_1 {mrs[0]} mrs_agent_2 {mrs[1]} "
00152 f"price_ratio {price_ratio} all_equal {same}"
00153 )
00154 feasible = all(x1[s] + x2[s] == total[s] for s in range(len(total)))
00155 print(f"equilibrium_feasible {feasible}")
00156 print("Part B: finite search over the grid of allocations")
00157 reference_eq = (utility(PROBS, x1), utility(PROBS, x2))
00158 reference_endowment = (utility(PROBS, ENDOWMENTS[0]), utility(PROBS, ENDOWMENTS[1]))
00159 candidates = grid_allocations(total)
00160 print(f"grid_allocations {len(candidates)} grid_points_per_state {len(GRID)}")
00161 improving_eq = [(a, b) for a, b in candidates if improves((utility(PROBS, a), utility(PROBS, b)), reference_eq)]
00162 print(f"pareto_improvements_on_grid_at_equilibrium {len(improving_eq)}")
00163 improving_endow = [
00164 (a, b) for a, b in candidates if improves((utility(PROBS, a), utility(PROBS, b)), reference_endowment)
00165 ]
00166 print(f"pareto_improvements_on_grid_at_endowment {len(improving_endow)}")
00167 if improving_endow:
00168 first_a, first_b = improving_endow[0]
00169 print(
00170 f"first_improvement_at_endowment agent_1 {tuple(str(z) for z in first_a)} "
00171 f"agent_2 {tuple(str(z) for z in first_b)}"
00172 )
00173 main()

00186 3Where the canon breaks
00187 Efficiency is not fairness. Definition 3.2 counts an allocation as better only when no agent is worse off. It does not rank an allocation that helps one agent and hurts another. Misconception 1 gives the consequence.
00188 The equilibrium is taken as given. Proposition 3.4 assumes an equilibrium. Existence is Assumption A5 of Lesson L02, which this lesson does not prove.
00189 Complete Arrow–Debreu markets. The theorem is about the economy of Definition 2.3, in which a good is traded in every state. It is not a statement about markets in which some claims cannot be traded. Lesson L04 studies that case.
00190 Complete preferences. Proposition 3.4 compares bundles through the utilities \(U_i\) of A4, which take each agent's ranking of bundles to be complete and represented by \(U_i\). An incomplete ranking, or one that no function of the form in A4 represents, is outside the theorem.
00191 No externalities and no frictions. Each \(U_i\) depends only on agent \(i\)'s own bundle, and the budget set has no transaction costs. An externality, in the sense of E1, is outside the theorem.
00192 Boundary allocations are outside (b). Part (b) needs differentiability in A4, and its bundles are strictly positive by Lemma 2.4, as in part (a). It says nothing about a boundary allocation with zero consumption in some state.
00193 4Misconceptions
00194 “A competitive equilibrium is the best allocation for everyone.”
00195 Definition 3.2 says that a Pareto improvement needs no agent to be worse off. So Proposition 3.4 (a) rules out a Pareto improvement, which leaves every agent at least as well off and one agent strictly better off. It says nothing about an allocation that makes one agent better off and another worse off. Lab 3 Part B counts the grid allocations that satisfy Definition 3.2.
00196 “Equal marginal rates of substitution mean equal consumption.”
00197 Proposition 3.4 (b) says the rates are equal, and it says nothing about the bundles. In Example 3.6 both agents have the marginal rate 4 between states 1 and 2, but their bundles differ, \((\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) and \((\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\), as Lab 2 Part C prints. Equal rates at different bundles are consistent, because the rate depends on the bundle.
00198 “Efficiency is a property of allocations, so the theorem holds in any market.”
00199 The theorem is about the economy of Definition 2.3, with a price for each state's good and with the assumptions of Lesson L02. The proof uses the budget sets that those prices define (Step 1 and Step 2 of Proposition 3.4 (a)). Without that market structure the proof has no budget set to work with, so the theorem does not apply.

00203 M1 · A marginal rate between two states
00204 Let \(\pi = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23)\) and \(x = (1, 4)\). Compute \(\mathrm{MRS}_{12}(x)\) for an agent with logarithmic utility.
00205 Solution
00206 The marginal rate is \(\mathrm{MRS}_{12} = (\pi_1 / x_1) / (\pi_2 / x_2) = (\tfrac13 / 1) / (\tfrac23 / 4) = \tfrac13 / \tfrac16 = 2\). So the agent would give up 2 units of the good in state 2 for one more unit in state 1, at the same utility. If \(q_1 / q_2 = 2\) are the equilibrium prices and this bundle is the agent's equilibrium bundle, Proposition 3.4 (b) says that the rate equals the price ratio, as here.
00207 M2 · A demand at prices with ratio 2
00208 Let \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(q = (2, 1)\) and wealth \(w = 6\). Find the logarithmic demand, and check that its marginal rate equals the price ratio.
00209 Solution
00210 By Proposition 2.8, \(x = (\tfrac12 \cdot 6 / 2, \tfrac12 \cdot 6 / 1) = (\tfrac32, 3)\). The spending is \(2 \cdot \tfrac32 + 3 = 6 = w\). The marginal rate is \((\tfrac12 / \tfrac32) / (\tfrac12 / 3) = \tfrac13 / \tfrac16 = 2\), which equals \(q_1 / q_2 = 2\). The proof of Proposition 3.4 (b) derives this equality for a strictly positive demand at prices \(q\). It uses the demand property, the budget, and the differentiability of A4, together with \(u_i' > 0\) from strict monotonicity and concavity. Proposition 3.4 itself is stated for equilibrium bundles, and this problem is one agent's demand at given prices.
00211 M3 · Feasibility of a split
00212 Let \(\bar e = (2, 2)\). Check whether \(y^1 = (1, 1)\) and \(y^2 = (1, 1)\) form a feasible allocation in the sense of Definition 3.1.
00213 Solution
00214 Both bundles are nonnegative, and \(y^1 + y^2 = (2, 2) = \bar e\). Definition 3.1 requires exactly this, so the split is feasible. It gives each agent half of the aggregate in each state, which is an equal split that is not necessarily an equilibrium allocation.

00216 P1 · The case in which every agent gains
00217 Under the assumptions of Proposition 3.4, show directly that no feasible allocation makes every agent strictly better off than the equilibrium.
00218 Solution
00219 Let \((y^1, y^2)\) be feasible with \(U_i(y^i) > U_i(x^{i*})\) for both agents. By Step 1 of the proof of Proposition 3.4 (a), \(q^* \cdot y^i > w_i\) for each \(i\). Adding gives \(q^* \cdot \bar e = q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) > w_1 + w_2 = q^* \cdot \bar e\), which is impossible. So no such allocation is feasible.
00220 P2 · The marginal rates of the two-agent example
00221 In the economy of Proposition 2.9, show that \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x^{i*}) = q^*_s / q^*_t\) for both agents and all states \(s\), \(t\), using the formulas of Proposition 2.9 and the derivative of the logarithm, \(u_i'(x) = 1/x\).
00222 Solution
00223 Here \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\), and \(u_i'(x) = 1/x\). So \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st} = (\pi_s / (\theta_i \bar e_s)) / (\pi_t / (\theta_i \bar e_t)) = (\pi_s \bar e_t) / (\pi_t \bar e_s)\). The share \(\theta_i\) cancels. Also \(q^*_s / q^*_t = (\pi_s / \bar e_s) / (\pi_t / \bar e_t) = (\pi_s \bar e_t) / (\pi_t \bar e_s)\). The two expressions are equal for both agents, which is (b) for this economy.
00224 P3 · Which hypotheses each part uses
00225 List the hypotheses that part (a) of Proposition 3.4 uses, and the hypotheses that part (b) uses in addition. Say which part would still hold if utilities were not differentiable.
00226 Solution
00227 Part (a) uses A1 and A3 through Lemma 2.4 (which gives positive optimal bundles), the strict monotonicity in A4, the positivity of \(q^*\), and feasibility. Part (b) uses in addition the differentiability in A4 and \(u_i' > 0\), which follows from the strict monotonicity and the concavity in A4. Part (b) needs no strict positivity beyond that of part (a), because Lemma 2.4 already gives the strict positivity of \(x^{i*}\) in the proof of part (a). Part (a) would still hold without differentiability, because its proof compares utilities and budgets and takes no derivative. Part (b) would not be established by this argument.

00233 E2 · What the endowment improvements show
00234 Part B of Lab 3 prints 2 Pareto improvements of the endowment and gives the first one. What does this count show about the endowment, and what is the witness?
00235 Solution
00236 The count of 2 shows that the grid search finds allocations that make both agents at least as well off and one strictly better off, relative to the endowment. The conclusion that the endowment is not Pareto efficient rests on the hand witness of Problem R2, not on the count. The first witness gives agent 1 the bundle \((2, 4, 3)\) and agent 2 the bundle \((2, 4, 1)\), which is feasible because the two bundles add to \((4, 8, 4)\). Relative to the endowment, each agent's expected utility rises by \(\tfrac14 \ln\tfrac43 > 0\) at this witness, which is checked by hand (grade T). The count of 2 is a finite grid enumeration (grade C), and it does not show the full set of improvements.
00237 E3 · The zero count at the equilibrium
00238 Part B of Lab 3 prints 0 Pareto improvements of the equilibrium among the 125 grid allocations. Why does this count not prove efficiency, and which result does prove it?
00239 Solution
00240 The equilibrium allocation is not on the grid, and the grid has only 125 feasible points out of the continuum of feasible allocations. A zero count is therefore a finite enumeration, grade C with that basis. The efficiency claim is proved by Proposition 3.4 (a), which covers every feasible allocation.

00242 R1 · Free disposal
00243 Replace the feasibility condition of Definition 3.1 by \(y^1 + y^2 \le \bar e\). Show that Proposition 3.4 (a) still holds, and identify the step that uses the strict positivity of every price \(q^*_s\).
00244 Solution
00245 Steps 1 and 2 of the proof give \(q^* \cdot y^i \ge w_i\) for each agent, with a strict inequality for the improved agent. Adding, \(q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) > q^* \cdot \bar e\). Now \(\bar e - (y^1 + y^2) \ge 0\), and \(q^* \cdot (\bar e - (y^1 + y^2)) \ge 0\) because every price is nonnegative. This gives \(q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) \le q^* \cdot \bar e\), which contradicts the strict inequality. So the last step needs only \(q^*_s \ge 0\). The strict positivity of the prices enters earlier, through Lemma 2.4, which makes each equilibrium utility finite.
00246 R2 · A proof that the endowment is not efficient
00247 In the economy of Example 2.11, show that the endowment \((e^1, e^2)\) is not Pareto efficient, by exhibiting a feasible allocation that is a Pareto improvement for small \(\varepsilon > 0\).
00248 Solution
00249 Let agent 1 give \(\varepsilon\) units of state 1 and receive \(4\varepsilon\) units of state 2, and agent 2 do the reverse. The new bundles are \(y^1 = (3 - \varepsilon, 4 + 4\varepsilon, 1)\) and \(y^2 = (1 + \varepsilon, 4 - 4\varepsilon, 3)\), which add to \(\bar e\) and are strictly positive for small \(\varepsilon\). The derivative of \(U_1(y^1)\) at \(\varepsilon = 0\) is \(\tfrac12 \cdot (-\tfrac13) + \tfrac14 \cdot 1 = \tfrac{1}{12} > 0\). The derivative of \(U_2(y^2)\) is \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot (-1) = \tfrac14 > 0\). So both utilities rise for small \(\varepsilon > 0\), and the endowment is not efficient. This is consistent with the marginal rates of the endowment between states 1 and 2, which are \(\tfrac83\) for agent 1 and 8 for agent 2 and differ. Agent 1 would give up \(\tfrac83\) units of state 2 for one more unit of state 1, so it would accept \(\tfrac83\) units of state 2 for each unit of state 1 that it gives up. The trade gives it 4 units of state 2 per unit, so agent 1 gains. Agent 2 would give up 8 units of state 2 for one more unit of state 1, and the trade costs it only 4 units of state 2 per unit, so agent 2 gains.
00250 R3 · Which hypothesis part (b) needs
00251 Explain why the proof of part (b) of Proposition 3.4 does not extend to a boundary allocation in which some agent consumes zero in some state, under A4.
00252 Solution
00253 The argument in the proof of (b) moves the bundle in both directions, with \(\varepsilon > 0\) and \(\varepsilon < 0\). At a bundle with a zero entry, a move that lowers that entry leaves the nonnegative orthant. Under the convention of A4 the utility there is \(-\infty\), so the derivative at \(\varepsilon = 0\) does not exist for that move. Lemma 2.4 ensures that equilibrium bundles are strictly positive when wealth is positive, so the argument applies to them.
