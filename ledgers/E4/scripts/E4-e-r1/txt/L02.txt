00020 Lesson L01 priced claims once the security prices were known. This lesson asks where those prices come from. Two agents trade one good in each state. Each agent chooses the best affordable bundle at the prices, and an equilibrium is a price vector together with chosen bundles that add up to what the economy holds. The lesson proves that budgets bind, and, under A1, A3 and A4, that the value of the excess demand (Definition 2.3) is zero at any positive price vector at which each agent has a demand (Proposition 2.5). It then solves one economy in closed form, with logarithmic utility. Existence in general is not proved here, and the lesson says where it is assumed.
00021 1Economy, budgets and demand
00022 There are two agents, \(i = 1, 2\), and the states of Lesson L01, with probabilities \(\pi_s\) and A1 in force. Each agent starts with an
00023 endowment
00024 \(e^i \in \R^{S}\), the bundle it holds before any trade, and the
00025 aggregate endowment
00026 is \(\bar e = e^1 + e^2\). The letter \(e\) with an agent superscript names an endowment in this lesson; the Arrow–Debreu security \(e_s\) of Lesson L01 carries only a state subscript and is not used in this lesson. A bundle is a vector \(x \in \R^{S}_{+}\) of nonnegative amounts of the good in each state.
00027 Assumption A3 (strictly positive endowments)
00028 For each agent \(i\) and each state \(s\), \(e^i_s > 0\). Hence \(\bar e_s > 0\) for every \(s\).
00029 Assumption A4 (utility)
00030 Agent \(i\) has a function \(u_i\) on \((0, \infty)\) that is strictly increasing, strictly concave and differentiable. Its expected utility of a bundle is
00031 \[
00032 U_i(x) = \sum_{s=1}^{S} \pi_s\, u_i(x_s) \quad \text{when } x_s > 0 \text{ for all } s, \qquad U_i(x) = -\infty \text{ when some } x_s = 0 .
00033 \]
00034 The convention \(U_i = -\infty\) at a bundle with a zero entry is the one that the logarithm needs. The display in A4 sets \(U_i = -\infty\) at a zero entry, so the value of \(u_i\) at zero never enters; no result below uses it.
00035 Assumption A4 holds for \(u_i(x) = \ln x\), which is the example of §3 and §4. Its expected utility at a bundle with a zero entry is \(-\infty\), which is the convention above. The model assumes that both agents use the same probabilities \(\pi_s\). The agents may differ in their utilities and endowments.
00036 Definition 2.1 (budget set and wealth)
00037 At a price vector \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\), the
00038 wealth
00039 of agent \(i\) is \(w_i = q \cdot e^i\), and its
00040 budget set
00041 is
00042 \[
00043 B_i(q) = \{ x \in \R^{S}_{+} : q \cdot x \le w_i \}.
00044 \]
00045 The price \(q_s\) is the number of units of value an agent must give up to receive one unit of the good in state \(s\), and the wealth is the value of the endowment at those prices.
00046 Definition 2.2 (demand)
00047 A
00048 demand
00049 of agent \(i\) at prices \(q\) is a bundle \(x^i(q) \in B_i(q)\) with \(U_i(x^i(q)) \ge U_i(x)\) for every \(x \in B_i(q)\). Agents take prices as given, which is the price-taking assumption behind the competitive equilibrium of the prerequisite module E1.
00050 Definition 2.3 (Arrow–Debreu equilibrium)
00051 An
00052 Arrow–Debreu equilibrium
00053 is a pair \((q^*, (x^{1*}, x^{2*}))\) with \(q^* \in (0, \infty)^{S}\) such that each \(x^{i*}\) is a demand of agent \(i\) at \(q^*\), and markets clear: \(x^{1*} + x^{2*} = \bar e\). Here \(x^i(q)\) denotes the demand chosen for agent \(i\) at \(q\), and \(z(q) = x^1(q) + x^2(q) - \bar e\) is the excess demand computed from those choices, so markets clear at \(q^*\) exactly when \(z(q^*) = 0\).
00054 Assumption A5 (existence)
00055 The economy under study has an Arrow–Debreu equilibrium. A5 is a hypothesis, not a result. No proposition below proves A5 for a general economy. Where a result needs an equilibrium, its statement names A5 or a given equilibrium. The example of §4 exhibits an equilibrium by construction, so A5 is not assumed there.
00056 2Budgets that bind and the value of excess demand
00057 Lemma 2.4 (an optimal bundle is strictly positive)
00058 Assume A1, A3 and A4. Let \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\) and \(w_i > 0\). Every demand of agent \(i\) at \(q\) has \(x_s > 0\) for every state \(s\).
00059 Proof
00060 Let \(\iota\) be the vector of ones, \(\iota = (1, \ldots, 1)\), and let \(y = \bigl(w_i / (2\, q \cdot \iota)\bigr)\iota\). Then \(q \cdot y = w_i/2 \le w_i\), so \(y \in B_i(q)\), and \(y\) has positive entries, so \(U_i(y)\) is finite. A demand \(x\) with a zero entry has \(U_i(x) = -\infty < U_i(y)\) by A4, which contradicts the definition of a demand. So every demand is strictly positive.
00061 Proposition 2.5 (
00062 Walras' law
00063 )
00064 Assume A1, A3 and A4. Let \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\), and let \(x^1\) and \(x^2\) be demands at \(q\). Then
00065 \[
00066 q \cdot \bigl(x^1 + x^2 - \bar e\bigr) = 0, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad q \cdot z(q) = 0 .
00067 \]
00068 The budgets bind: \(q \cdot x^i = w_i\) for each \(i\).
00069 Proof
00070 Fix \(i\) and write \(x = x^i\). Here \(w_i = q \cdot e^i > 0\), because \(q\) is strictly positive and \(e^i\) is strictly positive by A3, so Lemma 2.4 applies. By Lemma 2.4, \(x\) is strictly positive, so \(U_i(x)\) is finite. Suppose \(q \cdot x < w_i\). Let \(\delta > 0\) be small enough that \(q \cdot (x + \delta \iota) \le w_i\), which is possible because \(q \cdot (x + \delta \iota)\) tends to \(q \cdot x < w_i\) as \(\delta\) tends to 0. Then \(x + \delta \iota \in B_i(q)\). Since \(x + \delta \iota\) has positive entries and exceeds \(x\) in every state, A4 and A1 give \(U_i(x + \delta \iota) > U_i(x)\). This contradicts the demand property of \(x\). Hence \(q \cdot x = w_i\). Summing over the two agents, \(q \cdot (x^1 + x^2) = w_1 + w_2 = q \cdot (e^1 + e^2) = q \cdot \bar e\), which is the displayed identity.
00071 Remark 2.6 (what Walras' law does not say)
00072 Proposition 2.5 is a statement about the value of the excess demand, not about each market. The unweighted excess demands need not add to zero. At \(q = (2, 1, 1)\) in the economy of Example 2.11 (§4), the wealths are \(w_1 = 11\) and \(w_2 = 9\), and Proposition 2.8 (§3) gives the demands \((\tfrac{11}{4}, \tfrac{11}{4}, \tfrac{11}{4})\) and \((\tfrac94, \tfrac94, \tfrac94)\). The excess demand is therefore \((1, -3, 1)\), whose unweighted sum is \(-1\), while \(q \cdot z = 0\). Misconception 2 of this lesson gives a vector with zero unweighted sum that is not an excess demand at the prices of Example 2.11.
00073 Corollary 2.7 (the last market clears)
00074 Assume A1, A3 and A4, let \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\), and let \(x^1\) and \(x^2\) be demands at \(q\), so that \(z(q)\) is defined. If \(z_t(q) = 0\) for every state \(t\) other than \(s\), then \(z_s(q) = 0\).
00075 Proof
00076 By Proposition 2.5, \(\sum_{t} q_t z_t(q) = 0\). Every term with \(t \ne s\) vanishes by hypothesis, so \(q_s z_s(q) = 0\). Since \(q_s > 0\), \(z_s(q) = 0\).
00077 3Logarithmic utility and the closed-form demand
00078 The demand under logarithmic utility has an explicit form. Take \(u_i(x) = \ln x\), which satisfies A4, so that \(U_i(x) = \sum_s \pi_s \ln x_s\) on the strictly positive bundles.
00079 Proposition 2.8 (logarithmic demand)
00080 Assume A1. Let \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\) and \(w > 0\), and let \(U(x) = \sum_s \pi_s \ln x_s\). Then the budget set \(\{x \in \R^{S}_{+} : q \cdot x \le w\}\) has exactly one maximizer of \(U\), namely
00081 \[
00082 x^*_s = \frac{\pi_s\, w}{q_s}, \qquad s = 1, \ldots, S.
00083 \]
00084 Hence the demand is unique, and it spends the share \(\pi_s\) of wealth on each state, because \(q_s x^*_s = \pi_s w\).
00085 Proof
00086 The bundle \(x^*\) is strictly positive and satisfies \(q \cdot x^* = \sum_s \pi_s w = w\), using A1 for \(\sum_s \pi_s = 1\). So \(x^*\) lies in the budget set. Let \(x\) be any bundle in the budget set with \(U(x) > -\infty\), so every \(x_s > 0\). The tangent-line inequality for the strictly concave function \(\ln\) gives
00087 \[
00088 \ln x_s \le \ln x^*_s + \frac{x_s - x^*_s}{x^*_s}, \qquad \text{with equality if and only if } x_s = x^*_s .
00089 \]
00090 Multiply each inequality by \(\pi_s\) and sum. Since \(\pi_s / x^*_s = q_s / w\) by the formula for \(x^*\),
00091 \[
00092 U(x) \le U(x^*) + \sum_s \frac{\pi_s}{x^*_s} (x_s - x^*_s) = U(x^*) + \frac{1}{w} \sum_s q_s (x_s - x^*_s) = U(x^*) + \frac{q \cdot x - w}{w} \le U(x^*).
00093 \]
00094 Hence \(U(x) \le U(x^*)\), and \(U(x) = U(x^*)\) forces equality in every tangent inequality, so \(x_s = x^*_s\) for every \(s\). A bundle with a zero entry has \(U(x) = -\infty < U(x^*)\). So \(x^*\) is the unique maximizer.
00095 The maximizer also satisfies the KKT conditions of the problem \(\max U(x)\) subject to \(q \cdot x - w \le 0\) and \(x \ge 0\). These conditions are \(\pi_s / x_s = \mu\, q_s\) for each state, \(\mu \ge 0\), and \(\mu\,(q \cdot x - w) = 0\). The bundle \(x^*\) satisfies them with the multiplier \(\mu = 1/w\). Indeed \(\pi_s / x^*_s = q_s / w\) by the formula for \(x^*\), the budget binds, so the complementary slackness condition holds, and the nonnegativity constraints are slack because every \(x^*_s > 0\). Lab 2, Part D prints the ratio \(\pi_s / (x_s q_s)\), which is \(1/w\). The proof above does not use these conditions, and the lesson does not claim here that they are necessary or sufficient.
00096 4An equilibrium in closed form
00097 Proposition 2.9 (equilibrium with logarithmic utility)
00098 Assume A1 and A3, and that both agents have the utility \(u_i(x) = \ln x\), which satisfies A4. Define, for each agent,
00099 \[
00100 \theta_i = \sum_{s=1}^{S} \pi_s \frac{e^i_s}{\bar e_s}, \qquad q^*_s = \frac{\pi_s}{\bar e_s}.
00101 \]
00102 Then \(\theta_1 + \theta_2 = 1\), \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\), and \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\) for each agent and each state is an Arrow–Debreu equilibrium with \(q^*\). Each agent's wealth at \(q^*\) is \(w_i = \theta_i\).
00103 Proof
00104 First, \(\theta_1 + \theta_2 = \sum_s \pi_s (e^1_s + e^2_s)/\bar e_s = \sum_s \pi_s = 1\), using \(e^1_s + e^2_s = \bar e_s\). Next, \(q^* \cdot \bar e = \sum_s (\pi_s / \bar e_s) \bar e_s = \sum_s \pi_s = 1\). The price vector is strictly positive by A3. The wealth of agent \(i\) at \(q^*\) is \(w_i = \sum_s q^*_s e^i_s = \sum_s \pi_s e^i_s / \bar e_s = \theta_i\), which is positive by A3. By Proposition 2.8, the unique demand of agent \(i\) is \(x^{i*}_s = \pi_s \theta_i / q^*_s = \pi_s \theta_i \bar e_s / \pi_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\). Market clearing holds because \(x^{1*}_s + x^{2*}_s = (\theta_1 + \theta_2) \bar e_s = \bar e_s\) for each state.
00105 Remark 2.10 (the scale of the price is not fixed by demand)
00106 Under logarithmic utility, demand depends on the price and wealth only through \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\). Replacing \((q, w)\) by \((\psi q, \psi w)\) for \(\psi > 0\) leaves \(x\) unchanged. The normalization \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\) is a choice of scale, and it fixes the wealth shares \(w_i = \theta_i\). Proposition 2.9 does not prove that \(q^*\) is the only equilibrium price vector.
00107 Example 2.11 (three states, two agents)
00108 Let \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)\), \(e^1 = (3, 4, 1)\) and \(e^2 = (1, 4, 3)\). The aggregate is \(\bar e = (4, 8, 4)\). Proposition 2.9 gives \(\theta_1 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac34 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac{9}{16}\) and \(\theta_2 = \tfrac{7}{16}\). The prices are \(q^* = (\tfrac18, \tfrac{1}{32}, \tfrac1{16})\), and the equilibrium bundles are \(x^{1*} = (\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) and \(x^{2*} = (\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\). Lab 2 computes these values in exact arithmetic.
00109 Lab 2 · Equilibrium of the three-state economy, exact
00110 The lab uses no data. It solves the economy of Example 2.11 in exact Fractions, with the closed forms of Propositions 2.8 and 2.9. Before running it, the hypothesis is this. The aggregate is \((4, 8, 4)\), the wealth shares are \(\tfrac9{16}\) and \(\tfrac7{16}\), which sum to 1, and the prices are \((\tfrac18, \tfrac1{32}, \tfrac1{16})\), with \(q \cdot \bar e = 1\). The demands are \((\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) for agent 1 and \((\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\) for agent 2. The budget residuals, the market-clearing residuals in each state, and the Walras residual are all zero (Proposition 2.5). The ratio \(\pi_s / (x_s q_s)\) equals \(\tfrac{16}{9}\) for agent 1 and \(\tfrac{16}{7}\) for agent 2 in every state, which is one over the agent's wealth. Scaling every price by 3 leaves both demands unchanged when wealth is re-valued at the new prices, so that wealth equals the value of the endowment (Remark 2.10). An economy with a zero endowment entry is rejected with a ValueError. No random draws are used.
00111 """E4 L02 lab 1: an Arrow-Debreu equilibrium of a two-agent exchange economy with logarithmic utility, exact.
00112 Inputs are illustrative numbers, not data. There are three states with probabilities PROBS, which sum to exactly 1.
00113 One good is delivered in each state. Agent i has the strictly positive endowment ENDOWMENTS[i] in those three states,
00114 and the expected utility of a bundle x is the sum over states of PROBS[s] times ln(x[s]). All quantities are exact
00115 Fractions, and no rounding occurs.
00116 The economy is solved in closed form. The price of the good in state s is q[s] = PROBS[s] / aggregate[s], which
00117 gives q times the aggregate endowment equal to 1 (the normalization). Each agent's wealth is w = q times its
00118 endowment, and its demand is x[s] = PROBS[s] * w / q[s], with multiplier 1 / w.
00119 Part A: the economy, the aggregate endowment and each agent's share of wealth, which sum to 1.
00120 Part B: the equilibrium price vector and the normalization.
00121 Part C: each agent's demand, with its budget residual, and the market-clearing residual in each state, all zero.
00122 Part D: the first-order ratio PROBS[s] / (x[s] q[s]) for each agent in each state, which equals the agent's multiplier.
00123 Part E: demand is unchanged when every price is multiplied by 3 and wealth is re-valued at the new prices (wealth equals
00124 the value of the endowment), so demand does not fix the scale of the prices.
00125 Part F: an economy with a non-positive (zero) endowment entry is rejected with a ValueError.
00126 No random draws are used, so every run prints the same lines.
00127 """
00128 from __future__ import annotations
00129 from fractions import Fraction
00130 Payoff = tuple[Fraction, ...]
00131 PROBS: Payoff = (Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 4))
00132 ENDOWMENTS: tuple[Payoff, ...] = (
00133 (Fraction(3), Fraction(4), Fraction(1)),
00134 (Fraction(1), Fraction(4), Fraction(3)),
00135 )
00136 def check_economy(probabilities: Payoff, endowments: tuple[Payoff, ...]) -> None:
00137 """Raise ValueError unless the probabilities are positive and sum to 1, and every endowment entry is positive."""
00138 if any(p <= 0 for p in probabilities):
00139 raise ValueError("state probabilities must be positive")
00140 if sum(probabilities, Fraction(0)) != 1:
00141 raise ValueError("state probabilities must sum to 1")
00142 for endowment in endowments:
00143 if any(e <= 0 for e in endowment):
00144 raise ValueError("endowments must be strictly positive in every state")
00145 def aggregate(endowments: tuple[Payoff, ...]) -> Payoff:
00146 """The aggregate endowment in each state, the sum of the agents' endowments."""
00147 n_states = len(endowments[0])
00148 return tuple(sum((e[s] for e in endowments), Fraction(0)) for s in range(n_states))
00149 def dot(a: Payoff, b: Payoff) -> Fraction:
00150 """The inner product of two vectors with one entry per state, as an exact Fraction."""
00151 return sum((x * y for x, y in zip(a, b, strict=True)), Fraction(0))
00152 def equilibrium_prices(probabilities: Payoff, total: Payoff) -> Payoff:
00153 """The price of the good in each state, q[s] = probabilities[s] / total[s], normalized so that q . total = 1."""
00154 return tuple(p / t for p, t in zip(probabilities, total, strict=True))
00155 def demand(probabilities: Payoff, prices: Payoff, wealth: Fraction) -> Payoff:
00156 """The logarithmic-utility demand x[s] = probabilities[s] * wealth / prices[s], for a positive wealth."""
00157 if wealth <= 0:
00158 raise ValueError("wealth must be positive")
00159 return tuple(p * wealth / q for p, q in zip(probabilities, prices, strict=True))
00160 def main() -> None:
00161 """Print Parts A to F. Every value is an exact Fraction, so the output is reproducible."""
00162 check_economy(PROBS, ENDOWMENTS)
00163 total = aggregate(ENDOWMENTS)
00164 prices = equilibrium_prices(PROBS, total)
00165 wealth = [dot(prices, e) for e in ENDOWMENTS]
00166 print("Part A: economy, aggregate endowment and wealth shares")
00167 print(f"probabilities {tuple(str(p) for p in PROBS)} aggregate {tuple(str(z) for z in total)}")
00168 for i, e in enumerate(ENDOWMENTS, start=1):
00169 print(f"agent {i} endowment {tuple(str(z) for z in e)} wealth_share {wealth[i - 1]}")
00170 print(f"shares_sum_to_one {sum(wealth, Fraction(0)) == 1}")
00171 print("Part B: equilibrium prices and the normalization")
00172 print(f"prices {tuple(str(q) for q in prices)}")
00173 print(f"prices_dot_aggregate {dot(prices, total)}")
00174 print("Part C: demands, budget residuals and market clearing")
00175 demands = [demand(PROBS, prices, w) for w in wealth]
00176 for i, (x, e) in enumerate(zip(demands, ENDOWMENTS, strict=True), start=1):
00177 print(f"agent {i} demand {tuple(str(z) for z in x)}")
00178 print(f"agent {i} budget_residual {dot(prices, x) - dot(prices, e)}")
00179 for s in range(len(PROBS)):
00180 clearing = sum((x[s] for x in demands), Fraction(0)) - total[s]
00181 print(f"state {s} market_clearing_residual {clearing}")
00182 excess = tuple(
00183 sum((x[s] - e[s] for x, e in zip(demands, ENDOWMENTS, strict=True)), Fraction(0)) for s in range(len(PROBS))
00184 )
00185 print(f"walras_law_residual {dot(prices, excess)}")
00186 print("Part D: first-order ratio PROBS[s] / (x[s] q[s]) for each agent")
00187 for i, x in enumerate(demands, start=1):
00188 ratios = tuple(PROBS[s] / (x[s] * prices[s]) for s in range(len(PROBS)))
00189 same = all(r == 1 / wealth[i - 1] for r in ratios)
00190 print(f"agent {i} ratios {tuple(str(r) for r in ratios)} equal_to_one_over_wealth {same}")
00191 print("Part E: scaling every price by 3, with wealth re-valued at the new prices, leaves demand unchanged")
00192 scaled = tuple(3 * q for q in prices)
00193 for i, e in enumerate(ENDOWMENTS, start=1):
00194 again = demand(PROBS, scaled, dot(scaled, e))
00195 print(f"agent {i} demand_unchanged {again == demands[i - 1]}")
00196 print("Part F: an economy with a non-positive endowment is rejected")
00197 bad = ((Fraction(3), Fraction(4), Fraction(1)), (Fraction(1), Fraction(0), Fraction(3)))
00198 try:
00199 check_economy(PROBS, bad)
00200 except ValueError as exc:
00201 print(f"rejected message {exc}")
00202 main()

00229 5Where the canon breaks
00230 Two agents. Propositions 2.5 and 2.9 are stated for two agents. Research problem R1 extends Proposition 2.9 to any number of agents with the same proof.
00231 Existence is assumed. A5 is a hypothesis. For a general economy with A3 and A4, this lesson does not show that an equilibrium exists, and it names A5 wherever it is needed.
00232 The logarithm is one utility. Proposition 2.8 and Proposition 2.9 use \(\ln\). For other utilities in A4 the formula \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\) need not give the demand: the utility \(-1/x\) on \((0, \infty)\) satisfies A4. Its first-order condition \(\pi_s / x_s^2 = \mu\, q_s\) differs from the one behind Proposition 2.8, so the formulas of this section are not, in general, its demand.
00233 A constant share of the aggregate is a property of the logarithmic economy of Proposition 2.9. Proposition 2.9 gives \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\), so each agent receives a fixed share \(\theta_i\) of the aggregate in every state. That follows from the proof of Proposition 2.9, which uses the logarithmic demand. The lesson does not claim it for other utilities.
00234 6Misconceptions
00235 “The price of a state is its probability.”
00236 Proposition 2.9 gives \(q^*_s = \pi_s / \bar e_s\), so the price also depends on how much of the good the economy holds in that state. In Example 2.11, state 2 has probability \(\tfrac14\), the same as state 3, but its price is \(\tfrac1{32}\) against \(\tfrac1{16}\), because \(\bar e_2 = 8\) is larger than \(\bar e_3 = 4\). Lab 2 Part B prints the three prices.
00237 “Walras' law says that the unweighted excess demands add to zero.”
00238 Proposition 2.5 gives \(q \cdot z = 0\), a weighted sum with the prices as weights. The vector \(v = (1, -1, 0)\) has unweighted sum zero, but at the prices of Example 2.11 its value is \(\tfrac18 - \tfrac1{32} = \tfrac{3}{32} \ne 0\). So no excess demand of that economy at those prices equals \(v\).
00239 “Any bundle the agent can afford is a demand.”
00240 The endowment \(e^1 = (3, 4, 1)\) costs exactly \(w_1 = \tfrac{9}{16}\) at \(q^*\), so it lies on the budget boundary. It is not the demand, which Lab 2 Part C prints as \((\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\). A demand is the best affordable bundle (Definition 2.2), and affordability is only the constraint.

00243 Mechanics
00244 M1 · A logarithmic demand at given prices
00245 Let \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(q = (\tfrac14, \tfrac18)\) and wealth \(w = \tfrac12\). Find the demand of an agent with logarithmic utility, and check that it spends its wealth.
00246 Solution
00247 By Proposition 2.8, \(x_1 = \pi_1 w / q_1 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 / \tfrac14 = 1\) and \(x_2 = \pi_2 w / q_2 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 / \tfrac18 = 2\). The spending is \(q \cdot x = \tfrac14 \cdot 1 + \tfrac18 \cdot 2 = \tfrac12 = w\), so the budget binds, as Proposition 2.5 requires. The state probabilities are the spending shares, since \(q_s x_s = \pi_s w\) in each state.
00248 M2 · Demand with three states
00249 Let \(\pi = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12)\), \(q = (1, 2, 1)\) and \(w = 3\). Compute the logarithmic demand and check the budget.
00250 Solution
00251 The formula \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\) gives \(x = (\tfrac14 \cdot 3 / 1, \tfrac14 \cdot 3 / 2, \tfrac12 \cdot 3 / 1) = (\tfrac34, \tfrac38, \tfrac32)\). The spending is \(\tfrac34 + 2 \cdot \tfrac38 + \tfrac32 = \tfrac34 + \tfrac34 + \tfrac32 = 3\), which equals \(w\). The spending in each state is \(\pi_s w\), that is \(\tfrac34\), \(\tfrac34\) and \(\tfrac32\).
00252 M3 · A two-state equilibrium by hand
00253 Let \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(e^1 = (1, 3)\) and \(e^2 = (3, 1)\). Find \(\bar e\), the equilibrium prices of Proposition 2.9, the wealth shares, and the equilibrium bundles, and check market clearing.
00254 Solution
00255 The aggregate is \(\bar e = (4, 4)\). The prices are \(q^* = (\tfrac18, \tfrac18)\), and \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\). The share of agent 1 is \(\theta_1 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac14 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac34 = \tfrac12\), and \(\theta_2 = \tfrac12\). So \(x^{1*} = \tfrac12 \bar e = (2, 2)\) and \(x^{2*} = (2, 2)\). Their sum is \((4, 4) = \bar e\), so markets clear.
00256 Proof
00257 P1 · The last market with three states
00258 Let \(S = 3\), and suppose \(z_1(q) = z_2(q) = 0\) at some \(q \in (0, \infty)^{3}\), with demands as in Proposition 2.5. Show that \(z_3(q) = 0\).
00259 Solution
00260 This is Corollary 2.7 with \(s = 3\). By Proposition 2.5, \(q_1 z_1 + q_2 z_2 + q_3 z_3 = 0\). The first two terms vanish, so \(q_3 z_3 = 0\). Since \(q_3 > 0\), \(z_3 = 0\). The argument uses the strict positivity of the price in the last state, and Proposition 2.5, which holds under A1, A3 and A4.
00261 P2 · Scaling prices and wealth together
00262 In the economy of Proposition 2.9, let \(\psi > 0\). Show that \((\psi q^*, \psi w)\) gives the same demands, so that \(\psi q^*\) with wealth \(\psi \theta_i\) is an equilibrium with the same bundles.
00263 Solution
00264 At prices \(\psi q^*\) and wealth \(\psi \theta_i\), Proposition 2.8 gives \(x_s = \pi_s \psi \theta_i / (\psi q^*_s) = \pi_s \theta_i / q^*_s\), which is the earlier demand. The bundles are unchanged, so market clearing is unchanged too. The scaled price vector is therefore also an equilibrium price vector for the same bundles, and the normalization \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\) is one choice among these.
00265 P3 · Shares of the aggregate are state-independent
00266 In the economy of Proposition 2.9, show that \(x^{i*}_s / \bar e_s = \theta_i\) for every state \(s\), and explain what this says about how the aggregate is divided among the agents in each state.
00267 Solution
00268 By Proposition 2.9, \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\), so the ratio is \(\theta_i\) in every state. Agent \(i\) therefore consumes the same fraction of the aggregate in each state. In this economy the fraction does not depend on the state, so the agent's consumption moves in proportion to the aggregate. The statement is about this logarithmic economy, and the lesson does not claim it for other utilities.
00269 Empirical
00270 E1 · The normalization in Lab 2
00271 From Part B of Lab 2, what are the three prices, and what does the line "prices_dot_aggregate 1" check?
00272 Solution
00273 The prices printed are \((\tfrac18, \tfrac1{32}, \tfrac1{16})\). The line checks that \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\). Since \(\bar e = (4, 8, 4)\), the sum is \(\tfrac48 + \tfrac{8}{32} + \tfrac4{16} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = 1\), which is the normalization of Proposition 2.9.
00274 E2 · Residuals in Part C of Lab 2
00275 Part C prints a budget residual for each agent, three market-clearing residuals and a Walras residual. Which equation does each residual test, and what are the printed values?
00276 Solution
00277 Each agent's budget residual tests \(q^* \cdot x^i - w_i = 0\). The three market-clearing residuals test \(x^{1}_s + x^{2}_s - \bar e_s = 0\) for each state. The Walras residual tests \(q^* \cdot z = 0\) (Proposition 2.5). All printed values are 0, so each equation holds exactly for these bundles.
00278 E3 · Multipliers and the scale check in Lab 2
00279 Part D prints the ratio \(\pi_s / (x_s q_s)\) for each agent, and Part E prints whether the demands are unchanged when every price is multiplied by 3 with wealth re-valued at the new prices. What values does Part D print, and what does Part E show?
00280 Solution
00281 Part D prints \(\tfrac{16}{9}\) in each state for agent 1 and \(\tfrac{16}{7}\) for agent 2. These equal \(1/w_1\) and \(1/w_2\), as the first-order condition predicts, and the flag reads True for both. Part E prints "demand_unchanged True" for both agents. The logarithmic demand formula depends on prices only through \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\), so a common rescaling of prices and wealth leaves the bundles unchanged (Remark 2.10).
00282 Research
00283 R1 · Any number of agents
00284 Let there be \(I\) agents with logarithmic utility, endowments \(e^i\) with \(e^i_s > 0\), and \(\bar e = \sum_i e^i\). Define \(\theta_i\) and \(q^*\) as in Proposition 2.9. Show that the same formulas give an equilibrium.
00285 Solution
00286 Summing \(\theta_i = \sum_s \pi_s e^i_s / \bar e_s\) over \(i\) gives \(\sum_i \theta_i = \sum_s \pi_s \sum_i e^i_s / \bar e_s = \sum_s \pi_s = 1\). Also \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\) and each wealth is \(w_i = \theta_i > 0\). Proposition 2.8 gives the demands \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\), and \(\sum_i x^{i*}_s = \bar e_s\) for every state. So the proof of Proposition 2.9 uses no property specific to two agents.
00287 R2 · Own-price effect of a logarithmic demand
00288 For fixed wealth \(w\), find \(\partial x_s / \partial q_s\) for the demand of Proposition 2.8. What happens to the demand of state 1 when \(q_1\) doubles, with wealth fixed?
00289 Solution
00290 From \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\), \(\partial x_s / \partial q_s = -\pi_s w / q_s^2 = -x_s / q_s\). So the elasticity of demand with respect to the own price is \(-1\). Doubling \(q_1\) at fixed \(w\) gives \(x_1 \mapsto \tfrac12 x_1\), while \(x_t\) for \(t \ne 1\) does not change, because \(x_t = \pi_t w / q_t\) does not depend on \(q_1\).
00291 R3 · Where A3 is used
00292 Identify the exact places in the proofs of Propositions 2.8 and 2.9 that use A3, and say which of them require every entry of \(\bar e\) to be strictly positive and which require \(w_i > 0\).
00293 Solution
00294 Proposition 2.9 divides by \(\bar e_s\) in \(q^*_s = \pi_s / \bar e_s\) and in \(\theta_i\), so it needs \(\bar e_s > 0\) for every state. It divides by \(q^*_s\) when it computes \(x^{i*}_s\), which needs \(q^*_s > 0\), and the wealth \(w_i = \theta_i\) must be positive for Proposition 2.8. Beyond A1, Proposition 2.8 needs only \(w > 0\) and \(q \in (0,\infty)^S\). An agent with a zero endowment in one state can still have positive wealth, so A3 as a componentwise condition on each agent is stronger than the proof needs.
