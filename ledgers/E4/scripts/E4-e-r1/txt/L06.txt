00020 In this model a price can aggregate the information that investors hold. It also absorbs a supply of the asset that carries no payoff information. If that supply is random, the price no longer reveals the signal exactly. This lesson works in a one-period model with some investors who observe more than the price and a random supply of that kind. It derives the linear equilibrium in closed form, shows that investors who observe only the price learn from it through a linear projection, and gives the coefficients that Lesson L07 uses to measure how informative the price is. Section 1 defines the model's terms and assumptions (Definitions 6.1 and 6.2, Assumptions A1 to A4 and G).
00021 1The model
00022 Definition 6.1 (primitives)
00023 There is one risky asset. Its payoff is a random variable \(v\) with prior variance \(\sigma_v^2\). A
00024 signal
00025 is \(s = v + \varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\) is the signal noise with variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). A
00026 noise trader
00027 is an investor whose trade is not a function of the payoff information. In this model the noise traders are represented by a random net supply \(u\) of the asset: \(u > 0\) means that noise traders sell \(u\) units to the market, and \(u < 0\) means that they buy. The variance of \(u\) is \(\sigma_u^2\). The
00028 precisions
00029 are the reciprocals \(\tau_v = 1/\sigma_v^2\) and \(\tau_\varepsilon = 1/\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). The
00030 informed fraction
00031 is \(\lambda\), and the coefficient of absolute risk aversion is \(a\) (Definition 6.2 in this lesson).
00032 Assumption A1 (normal primitives)
00033 The random variables \(v\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(u\) are jointly normal, mean zero, and mutually independent, with \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 > 0\) and \(\sigma_u^2 > 0\). Hence \((v, s, u)\) is jointly normal, by the linear-image fact G(i) stated below. The zero prior mean is a normalization used throughout this lesson, and this lesson does not treat a nonzero mean.
00034 Definition 6.2 (CARA utility)
00035 An investor has
00036 constant absolute risk aversion (CARA) utility
00037 with coefficient \(a > 0\) if the utility of terminal wealth \(w\) is \(-\exp(-a w)\). Wealth is \(w = w_0 + x(v - p)\), where \(w_0\) is a constant, \(x\) is the risky holding and \(p\) is the price. The investor has no other random wealth.
00038 Assumption A2 (information)
00039 A fraction \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) of the investors is informed. An informed investor observes \(s\) and the price \(p\). An uninformed investor observes only \(p\). The fraction \(\lambda\) is given. It is not chosen by any investor in this lesson, and Lesson L08 endogenizes it.
00040 Assumption A3 (price taking and linear rule)
00041 Every investor takes the price \(p\) as given. A price is a function \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) of the signal and the supply, for real coefficients \(\theta_s\) and \(\theta_u\).
00042 Assumption A4 (market clearing at every pair \((s, u)\))
00043 For every pair \((s, u) \in \R^2\), with \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\), the informed demand \(x_I\) and the uninformed demand \(x_U\) (the CARA demands of Remark 6.5 in §2) satisfy
00044 \[
00045 \lambda\, x_I + (1 - \lambda)\, x_U = u .
00046 \]
00047 The residual at \((s, u)\) is the left side minus \(u\). Clearing is required at each pair \((s, u)\). Step 3 of the proof of Proposition 6.7 in §3 evaluates the residual at two of these pairs, \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\). With the price rule \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) substituted (\(\theta_s\) and \(\theta_u\) fixed), the residual is a linear function of \((s, u)\) with no constant term. A linear function that is not identically zero vanishes only on a line, and a line has probability zero because \((s, u)\) has a nondegenerate normal law under A1. So clearing with probability one forces the residual to vanish at every pair, and the coefficients are those of Proposition 6.7.
00048 Assumption G (two facts about the normal law)
00049 (i) A linear image of a jointly normal random vector is jointly normal. (ii) If the components of a jointly normal random vector are pairwise uncorrelated, they are mutually independent. Both facts are used here without proof. The moment generating function of a normal variable, which Lemma 6.2 (in §2) needs, is proved in that lemma.
00050 2Beliefs and demands
00051 Given an investor's information, the conditional distribution of \(v\) is the investor's
00052 posterior
00053 . Its mean is the posterior mean, and its variance is the posterior variance used below.
00054 Lemma 6.2 (CARA demand under normal beliefs)
00055 Assume A3 (price taking) for an investor who takes \(p\) as given. Suppose that, conditional on the investor's information, \(v\) is normal with mean \(m\) and variance \(V > 0\). Then the investor's optimal holding is the unique maximizer
00056 \[
00057 x^\ast = \frac{m - p}{a V}.
00058 \]
00059 Proof
00060 Let \(Z\) be normal with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(V\), and let \(t\) be real. Then \(\E e^{tZ} = e^{t\mu + t^2 V / 2}\). To see this, write
00061 \[
00062 \E e^{tZ} = \int e^{tz} (2\pi V)^{-1/2} e^{-(z - \mu)^2 / (2V)} \, dz .
00063 \]
00064 Completing the square gives
00065 \[
00066 tz - \frac{(z - \mu)^2}{2V} = -\frac{(z - \mu - tV)^2}{2V} + t\mu + \frac{t^2 V}{2} .
00067 \]
00068 The integral of the shifted normal density is 1, which gives the formula.
00069 Apply this with \(Z = v - p\), which has mean \(m - p\) and variance \(V\) given the information, and with \(t = -a x\). Expected utility is
00070 \[
00071 \E\bigl[-e^{-a w_0 - a x (v - p)}\bigr] = -e^{-a w_0}\, e^{g(x)},
00072 \]
00073 with
00074 \[
00075 g(x) = -a x (m - p) + \tfrac{a^2 V}{2} x^2 .
00076 \]
00077 Maximizing expected utility is minimizing \(g\), because \(-e^{-a w_0} < 0\) and \(\exp\) is increasing. The function \(g\) is a quadratic with leading coefficient \(a^2 V / 2 > 0\), so it has a unique minimizer. Setting \(g'(x) = -a(m - p) + a^2 V x\) to zero gives \(x^\ast = (m - p)/(aV)\).
00078 Lemma 6.3 (informed beliefs)
00079 Assuming A1 and G. Let \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) for any real \(\theta_s\) and \(\theta_u\). Then an informed investor, who observes \((s, p)\), has
00080 \[
00081 \E[v \mid s, p] = \omega\, s, \qquad \Var(v \mid s, p) = V_I ,
00082 \]
00083 where
00084 \[
00085 \omega = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2}, \qquad V_I = \frac{\sigma_v^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2} .
00086 \]
00087 Equivalently, \(1/V_I = \tau_v + \tau_\varepsilon\) and \(\omega/V_I = \tau_\varepsilon\).
00088 Proof
00089 Let \(e = v - \omega s\). Then \(\Cov(e, s) = \sigma_v^2 - \omega(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2) = 0\), by the choice of \(\omega\). Also \(\Cov(e, u) = \Cov(v, u) - \omega \Cov(s, u) = 0\), because \(v\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(u\) are independent by A1. The vector \((e, s, u)\) is jointly normal by A1 and G(i). Its components are pairwise uncorrelated: \(e\) is uncorrelated with \(s\) and with \(u\) as shown above, and \(\Cov(s, u) = 0\) because \(s = v + \varepsilon\) is independent of \(u\) by A1. By G, \(e\) is independent of \((s, u)\). Since \(p\) is a function of \((s, u)\), \(e\) is independent of \((s, p)\). Therefore \(\E[v \mid s, p] = \omega s + \E[e] = \omega s\), because \(\E e = 0\) by A1. Also \[\Var(v \mid s, p) = \Var(e) = \sigma_v^2 - 2\omega \sigma_v^2 + \omega^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2) = \sigma_v^2(1 - \omega) = V_I\]. The two identities follow from the definitions of \(\omega\) and \(V_I\). Since \(e\) is normal and independent of \((s, p)\), the conditional law of \(v = \omega s + e\) given \((s, p)\) is normal with mean \(\omega s\) and variance \(V_I > 0\), as Lemma 6.2 requires.
00090 Lemma 6.4 (uninformed beliefs are a linear projection)
00091 Assuming A1 and G. Let \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) with \((\theta_s, \theta_u) \neq (0, 0)\), so that \(\Var(p) > 0\). Define
00092 \[
00093 b = \frac{\Cov(v, p)}{\Var(p)} , \qquad \text{so that} \quad \Cov(v, p) = \theta_s \sigma_v^2 \quad (\text{because } \Cov(v, u) = 0 \text{ by A1}) .
00094 \]
00095 Then an uninformed investor, who observes \(p\), has \(\E[v \mid p] = b\, p\) and \(\Var(v \mid p) = \sigma_v^2 - b\, \Cov(v, p) =: V_U\).
00096 Proof
00097 Let \(e = v - bp\). Then \(\Cov(e, p) = \Cov(v, p) - b \Var(p) = 0\). The vector \((e, p)\) is a linear image of \((v, s, u)\), so it is jointly normal by A1 and G(i), and G makes \(e\) independent of \(p\). Hence \(\E[v \mid p] = bp + \E e = bp\), and \[\Var(v \mid p) = \Var(e) = \sigma_v^2 - 2b\,\Cov(v, p) + b^2 \Var(p) = \sigma_v^2 - b\,\Cov(v, p)\], because \(b^2 \Var(p) = b\,\Cov(v, p)\). Given \(p\), \(v = bp + e\) with \(e\) normal and independent of \(p\), so the conditional law of \(v\) is normal with mean \(bp\) and variance \(V_U\), as Lemma 6.2 requires. Here \(V_U = \sigma_v^2(\theta_s^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \theta_u^2 \sigma_u^2)/\Var(p) > 0\), because \((\theta_s, \theta_u) \neq (0, 0)\) and \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2, \sigma_u^2 > 0\).
00098 Remark 6.5 (the uninformed investor's demand)
00099 Lemmas 6.2 and 6.4 give the uninformed demand \(x_U = (bp - p)/(a V_U)\), with \(V_U = \Var(v \mid p)\). An uninformed investor takes the price as a signal and forms a linear forecast of \(v\) from it. The informed demand is \(x_I = (\omega s - p)/(a V_I)\) by Lemmas 6.2 and 6.3. Both demands are linear in \((s, p)\). That is why the market-clearing condition of A4 becomes a linear condition on the coefficients.
00100 3The linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium
00101 Definition 6.6 (linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium)
00102 A
00103 linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium
00104 is a pair of real coefficients \((\theta_s, \theta_u)\) such that the price \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) satisfies A4, where the informed demand is \(x_I = (\omega s - p)/(a V_I)\) (Lemma 6.3) and the uninformed demand is \(x_U = (bp - p)/(a V_U)\), with \(b\) and \(V_U\) given by Lemma 6.4 for this price. For the pair \((0, 0)\) the price is identically zero, so the uninformed belief is the prior, with \(b = 0\) and \(V_U = \sigma_v^2\).
00105 Proposition 6.7 (the coefficients are unique and given in closed form)
00106 Assuming A1 to A4 and G. Define
00107 \[
00108 \kappa = \frac{a}{\lambda \tau_\varepsilon} = \frac{a \sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\lambda} ,
00109 \]
00110 \[
00111 \sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2 \sigma_u^2 , \qquad \tau_\eta = \frac{1}{\sigma_\eta^2} ,
00112 \]
00113 \[
00114 \bar\tau = \lambda \tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda) \tau_\eta ,
00115 \]
00116 \[
00117 \theta_s^\ast = \frac{\bar\tau}{\tau_v + \bar\tau} , \qquad \theta_u^\ast = \kappa\, \theta_s^\ast .
00118 \]
00119 Then (a) \((\theta_s^\ast, \theta_u^\ast)\) is a linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium, and (b) every linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium \((\theta_s, \theta_u)\) equals \((\theta_s^\ast, \theta_u^\ast)\).
00120 Proof of (b), uniqueness
00121 Step 1: \(\theta_s \neq 0\). Suppose \(\theta_s = 0\). Then \(p = -\theta_u u\), so \(\Cov(v, p) = 0\), the uninformed belief is \(\E[v \mid p] = 0\), and their demand is \(x_U = -p/(a\sigma_v^2)\), which does not depend on \(s\). The informed demand is \(x_I = (\tau_\varepsilon s - (\tau_v + \tau_\varepsilon) p)/a\) by Lemma 6.3. Fix \(u = 0\). Then \(p = 0\), so \(x_I = \tau_\varepsilon s/a\) and \(x_U = 0\), and A4 requires \(\lambda \tau_\varepsilon s / a = 0\) for every \(s\). This fails at \(s = 1\), because \(\lambda \tau_\varepsilon / a \neq 0\). So \(\theta_s \neq 0\).
00122 Step 2: the price as a signal. Let \(\kappa' = \theta_u/\theta_s\) and \(Y = p/\theta_s = s - \kappa' u\). Then \(Y - v = \varepsilon - \kappa' u =: \eta\). The noise \(\eta\) is a function of \((\varepsilon, u)\), so it is independent of \(v\) and jointly normal with it, with variance \(\sigma_\eta'^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa'^2 \sigma_u^2\). Write \(\tau_\eta' = 1/\sigma_\eta'^2\) and \(P_U' = \tau_v + \tau_\eta'\). Since \(\sigma(p) = \sigma(Y)\), the argument of Lemma 6.4 applied to the linear combination \(Y\) gives \(\E[v \mid p] = \sigma_v^2 Y/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta'^2) = (\tau_\eta'/P_U') Y\) and \(V_U = 1/P_U'\). Hence \(x_U = (\tau_\eta' Y - P_U' p)/a\).
00123 Step 3: market clearing. With \(P_I = \tau_v + \tau_\varepsilon\), A4 gives \(\lambda(\tau_\varepsilon s - P_I p) + (1 - \lambda)(\tau_\eta' Y - P_U' p) = a u\) for every \((s, u)\). Substitute \(Y = p/\theta_s\) and write \(\bar\tau' = \lambda \tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta'\). Then
00124 \[
00125 \lambda \tau_\varepsilon s - Q\, p = a u ,
00126 \]
00127 where
00128 \[
00129 Q = \tau_v + \bar\tau' - \frac{(1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta'}{\theta_s} .
00130 \]
00131 With \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\), this reads \((\lambda \tau_\varepsilon - Q\theta_s)\, s + (Q\theta_u - a)\, u = 0\) for every \((s, u) \in \R^2\). Evaluating at \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) gives \(Q\theta_s = \lambda \tau_\varepsilon\) and \(Q\theta_u = a\).
00132 Step 4: solving. Dividing gives \(\kappa' = \theta_u/\theta_s = a/(\lambda\tau_\varepsilon) = \kappa\). So \(\sigma_\eta'^2 = \sigma_\eta^2\), \(\tau_\eta' = \tau_\eta\) and \(\bar\tau' = \bar\tau\). Now \(Q\theta_s = (\tau_v + \bar\tau)\theta_s - (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon\) gives \((\tau_v + \bar\tau)\theta_s = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta = \bar\tau\). Hence \(\theta_s = \theta_s^\ast\), and \(\theta_u = \kappa \theta_s = \theta_u^\ast\).
00133 Proof of (a), existence
00134 Define \(\theta_s^\ast\) and \(\theta_u^\ast\) as in the statement. Since \(\bar\tau > 0\), \(\theta_s^\ast \neq 0\). The price \(p^\ast = \theta_s^\ast s - \theta_u^\ast u\) gives \(Y = p^\ast/\theta_s^\ast = s - \kappa u\), so the noise in \(Y\) is \(\varepsilon - \kappa u\), with variance \(\sigma_\eta^2\). Step 2 of part (b) therefore applies with \(\kappa' = \kappa\). Let \(Q = \tau_v + \bar\tau - (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta/\theta_s^\ast\). The definition of \(\theta_s^\ast\) gives \((\tau_v + \bar\tau)\theta_s^\ast = \bar\tau\), so \(Q\theta_s^\ast = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon\). Also \(Q\theta_u^\ast = Q\kappa\theta_s^\ast = \kappa\lambda\tau_\varepsilon = a\). Then the clearing expression of Step 3 becomes \[\lambda\tau_\varepsilon s - Q(\theta_s^\ast s - \theta_u^\ast u) = (\lambda\tau_\varepsilon - Q\theta_s^\ast)s + Q\theta_u^\ast u = au\] for every \((s, u)\), which is A4.
00135 Corollary 6.8 (the uninformed projection at the equilibrium)
00136 Assuming A1 to A4 and G, and let \((\theta_s^\ast, \theta_u^\ast)\) be the equilibrium of Proposition 6.7. Then
00137 \[
00138 \E[v \mid p] = \frac{\tau_\eta}{\theta_s^\ast (\tau_v + \tau_\eta)}\, p ,
00139 \]
00140 \[
00141 \Var(v \mid p) = \frac{1}{\tau_v + \tau_\eta} = \frac{\sigma_v^2 \sigma_\eta^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2} .
00142 \]
00143 The slope \(b\) of Lemma 6.4 equals \(\tau_\eta / (\theta_s^\ast (\tau_v + \tau_\eta))\).
00144 Proof
00145 Step 4 of part (b) of Proposition 6.7 gives \(\tau_\eta' = \tau_\eta\), so Step 2 of that proof gives \(\E[v \mid p] = (\tau_\eta/P_U) Y\) and \(\Var(v \mid p) = 1/P_U\), with \(P_U = \tau_v + \tau_\eta\). Substituting \(Y = p/\theta_s^\ast\) gives the first formula. The second equality is the identity of Problem P3 with \(\sigma_\eta^2\) in place of the noise variance. Lemma 6.4 gives \(\E[v \mid p] = b\,p\), and \(\Var(p) > 0\) because \(\theta_s^\ast \ne 0\), so \(p\) is not a constant. Comparing the coefficient of \(p\) with the first formula gives \(b = \tau_\eta/(\theta_s^\ast(\tau_v + \tau_\eta))\).
00146 Example 6.9 (a base instance)
00147 Take \(\sigma_v^2 = 1\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(\sigma_u^2 = \tfrac14\). Then \(\tau_v = \tau_\varepsilon = 1\), \(\kappa = 2\), \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + 4 \cdot \tfrac14 = 2\), \(\tau_\eta = \tfrac12\), \(\bar\tau = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34\), \(\theta_s^\ast = \tfrac{3/4}{1 + 3/4} = \tfrac37\) and \(\theta_u^\ast = 2 \cdot \tfrac37 = \tfrac67\). So the price is \(p = \tfrac37 s - \tfrac67 u\). The uninformed slope is \(b = \tfrac{\tau_\eta}{\theta_s^\ast(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)} = \tfrac{1/2}{(3/7)(3/2)} = \tfrac79\), and \(V_U = \tfrac23\). At \(s = 1\) and \(u = 0\), the price is \(\tfrac37\), and the demands are \(x_I = \tfrac17\) and \(x_U = -\tfrac17\), which clear the market because \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac17 + \tfrac12 \cdot (-\tfrac17) = 0\). Lab 1 prints \(\kappa\), \(\sigma_\eta^2\), \(\tau_\eta\), \(\bar\tau\), \(\theta_s\), \(\theta_u\), the slope \(7/9\), the posterior variance \(2/3\) and the informed demand \(1/7\) at this instance. It does not print \(\tau_v = \tau_\varepsilon = 1\), the price \(3/7\) at \((s, u) = (1, 0)\), or the uninformed demand \(-1/7\). The clearing check at \((1, 0)\) is one of the 16 grid residuals of Part C.
00148 Remark 6.10 (the price as a noisy signal)
00149 Proposition 6.7 gives \(Y = p/\theta_s^\ast = s - \kappa u\). Uninformed investors therefore observe \(v + \eta\), where \(\eta = \varepsilon - \kappa u\) is noise with variance \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2 \sigma_u^2\). Since \(\sigma_\eta^2 \geq \sigma_\varepsilon^2 > 0\) for every \(\sigma_u^2 \geq 0\), the price reveals the payoff only with noise, and the supply contributes the term \(\kappa^2\sigma_u^2\) of that noise. A larger noise-trader variance \(\sigma_u^2\) raises \(\sigma_\eta^2\) and so lowers \(\tau_\eta\). Lesson L07 turns this into a measure of informativeness.
00150 Lab 1 · Exact coefficients, market clearing and a seeded sample check
00151 The lab uses the base instance of Example 6.9. Parts A to D are exact, computed with Fraction values. Before running it, the hypothesis is this. The closed form gives \(\kappa = 2\), \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 2\), \(\tau_\eta = \tfrac12\), \(\bar\tau = \tfrac34\), \(\theta_s = \tfrac37\) and \(\theta_u = \tfrac67\) (Proposition 6.7). The slope of \(v\) on \(p\) is \(\tfrac79\), the posterior variance of an uninformed investor is \(\tfrac23\), and the residual of A4 is zero at all 16 points of the grid (Part C, with A4 and Proposition 6.7). An alternative price coefficient \(\theta_s = \tfrac12\), with \(\theta_u = \tfrac67\) unchanged, leaves a nonzero residual at \((s, u) = (1, 0)\). Part E is SIMULATED: one seeded draw of 20 000 observations of \((v, \varepsilon, u)\), with seed 6. Each sample slope should lie within three estimated standard errors of its population value. No data are read.
00152 """E4 L06 lab 1: the linear noisy rational-expectations price, exact population values and a seeded sample check.
00153 Inputs are illustrative numbers, plus one seeded simulation that is labelled SIMULATED. The model has one risky asset
00154 with payoff v of prior mean zero and variance SIG_V2, a signal s = v + eps with noise variance SIG_E2, and a
00155 noise-trader supply u with mean zero and variance SIG_U2. A mass LAM of CARA investors with coefficient A_CARA observes
00156 s and the price p; the rest observe only the price p. Market clearing requires total demand to equal the supply u.
00157 Part A: the closed-form coefficients of the linear price p = theta_s * s - theta_u * u, with theta_s and
00158 theta_u from closed_form(), as exact Fractions.
00159 Part B: exact population moments of (s, p), the projection coefficient of v on p, and the posterior variance of an
00160 uninformed investor, each computed from the moments and not from the closed form.
00161 Part C: market clearing checked exactly on a grid of (s, u). Demands come from the Gaussian conditional formulas,
00162 and the uninformed belief is the projection of v on p, computed from the moments of p. Every residual is zero.
00163 Part D: a slope other than the equilibrium slope leaves a nonzero residual at one point of the grid.
00164 Part E: SIMULATED. A seeded sample of N_DRAWS draws of (v, eps, u). The sample slope of p on s and of v on p each lie
00165 within three estimated standard errors of the population value.
00166 Units: the payoff, the signal and the price are in price units per unit of the asset, so their variances are in squared
00167 price-per-asset units. The supply u is in units of the asset. The coefficient theta_s is dimensionless. The coefficients
00168 kappa and theta_u have units of price per unit of the asset squared, because kappa * u and theta_u * u are prices per
00169 unit of the asset and u is in units of the asset. The sample uses floating point and prints six decimals. Every other
00170 value is an exact Fraction.
00171 """
00172 from __future__ import annotations
00173 import math
00174 import random
00175 from fractions import Fraction
00176 from typing import NamedTuple
00177 SIG_V2 = Fraction(1)
00178 SIG_E2 = Fraction(1)
00179 A_CARA = Fraction(1)
00180 LAM = Fraction(1, 2)
00181 SIG_U2 = Fraction(1, 4)
00182 ALT_SLOPE = Fraction(1, 2)
00183 SEED = 6
00184 N_DRAWS = 20000
00185 GRID_S = (Fraction(-1), Fraction(0), Fraction(1), Fraction(2))
00186 GRID_U = (Fraction(-1), Fraction(0), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(2))
00187 class Price(NamedTuple):
00188 """The closed-form linear price p = theta_s * s - theta_u * u and the intermediate quantities that define it."""
00189 kappa: Fraction
00190 sig_eta2: Fraction
00191 tau_eta: Fraction
00192 tau_bar: Fraction
00193 theta_s: Fraction
00194 theta_u: Fraction
00195 def check_inputs(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, a: Fraction, lam: Fraction, sig_u2: Fraction) -> None:
00196 """Raise ValueError unless the variances are positive, a is positive and the informed mass lies in (0, 1)."""
00197 if sig_v2 <= 0 or sig_e2 <= 0 or sig_u2 <= 0:
00198 raise ValueError("payoff, signal and supply variances must be positive")
00199 if a <= 0:
00200 raise ValueError("CARA coefficient must be positive")
00201 if not 0 < lam < 1:
00202 raise ValueError("informed fraction must lie strictly between 0 and 1")
00203 def closed_form(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, a: Fraction, lam: Fraction, sig_u2: Fraction) -> Price:
00204 """The linear noisy REE coefficients: kappa = a / (lam / sig_e2), tau_eta = 1 / (sig_e2 + kappa^2 sig_u2)."""
00205 check_inputs(sig_v2, sig_e2, a, lam, sig_u2)
00206 tau_v = 1 / sig_v2
00207 tau_e = 1 / sig_e2
00208 kappa = a / (lam * tau_e)
00209 sig_eta2 = sig_e2 + kappa * kappa * sig_u2
00210 tau_eta = 1 / sig_eta2
00211 tau_bar = lam * tau_e + (1 - lam) * tau_eta
00212 theta_s = tau_bar / (tau_v + tau_bar)
00213 theta_u = kappa * theta_s
00214 return Price(kappa, sig_eta2, tau_eta, tau_bar, theta_s, theta_u)
00215 def residual(
00216 s: Fraction,
00217 u: Fraction,
00218 theta_s: Fraction,
00219 theta_u: Fraction,
00220 sig_v2: Fraction,
00221 sig_e2: Fraction,
00222 a: Fraction,
00223 lam: Fraction,
00224 sig_u2: Fraction,
00225 ) -> Fraction:
00226 """Total demand minus supply at (s, u) when the price is theta_s * s - theta_u * u.
00227 The informed demand uses E[v | s, p] = omega s with omega = sig_v2 / (sig_v2 + sig_e2), and Var(v | s, p) = V_I
00228 (Lemma 6.3, which holds for any price coefficients). The uninformed belief is the
00229 projection of v on p, with slope Cov(v, p) / Var(p) and residual variance Var(v) - slope Cov(v, p). Both use the
00230 moments of p, so the residual does not depend on the closed form.
00231 """
00232 price = theta_s * s - theta_u * u
00233 var_p = theta_s * theta_s * (sig_v2 + sig_e2) + theta_u * theta_u * sig_u2
00234 cov_vp = theta_s * sig_v2
00235 slope = cov_vp / var_p
00236 resid_var = sig_v2 - slope * cov_vp
00237 var_inf = sig_v2 * sig_e2 / (sig_v2 + sig_e2)
00238 mean_inf = sig_v2 * s / (sig_v2 + sig_e2)
00239 demand_inf = (mean_inf - price) / (a * var_inf)
00240 demand_unf = (slope * price - price) / (a * resid_var)
00241 return lam * demand_inf + (1 - lam) * demand_unf - u
00242 def ols(x: list[float], y: list[float]) -> tuple[float, float]:
00243 """Slope and its standard error from a simple regression of y on x, with an intercept."""
00244 n = len(x)
00245 mean_x = sum(x) / n
00246 mean_y = sum(y) / n
00247 sxx = sum((xi - mean_x) ** 2 for xi in x)
00248 sxy = sum((xi - mean_x) * (yi - mean_y) for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True))
00249 slope = sxy / sxx
00250 intercept = mean_y - slope * mean_x
00251 rss = sum((yi - intercept - slope * xi) ** 2 for xi, yi in zip(x, y, strict=True))
00252 return slope, math.sqrt(rss / (n - 2) / sxx)

00360 4Where the canon breaks
00361 Normality. Lemmas 6.3 and 6.4 use G, which applies to jointly normal vectors. With a payoff or a supply that is not normal, the conditional mean of \(v\) given \(p\) need not be linear in \(p\), and the closed form of Proposition 6.7 does not follow from this proof.
00362 Utility. Lemma 6.2 depends on CARA utility. Its proof uses the moment generating function of a normal variable with a constant risk-aversion coefficient. Under other utilities, such as CRRA utility, the demand under normal beliefs need not be the expression of Lemma 6.2, and the linear equilibrium of Proposition 6.7 need not exist.
00363 Exogenous information. The informed fraction \(\lambda\) is an input (A2). Lesson L08 makes it a choice, so the coefficients here are conditional on \(\lambda\).
00364 Independent supply. A1 makes \(u\) independent of \(v\) and \(\varepsilon\). If the supply were correlated with the payoff, the zero covariance in the proof of Lemma 6.3 would fail. Also \(\Cov(v, p) = \theta_s\sigma_v^2 - \theta_u\Cov(v, u)\) would differ from the value \(\theta_s\sigma_v^2\) used in Lemma 6.4, so the uninformed inference would change.
00365 Price taking. A3 says that no investor takes account of the price's response to its own trade. A large informed trader would face a price that depends on its demand, and that case is outside this lesson.
00366 One period and one asset. The model has one risky asset and one trading date. It does not model the dynamics of learning or repeated trade.
00367 5Misconceptions
00368 “Once the price depends on the signal, the uninformed investors learn the signal exactly.”
00369 The price is \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\), and the uninformed observe \(Y = s - \kappa u = v + \eta\), where \(\eta = \varepsilon - \kappa u\). The variance of \(\eta\) is \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2\), which is at least \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) (Remark 6.10). At the base instance it is 2 (Lab 1, Part A), so the price is a noisy signal.
00370 “A noise trader must be an irrational investor who trades on mistaken beliefs.”
00371 In Definition 6.1 the noise trader is a random supply \(u\) that is independent of the payoff. A1 and A2 say nothing about its beliefs, and the model contains no beliefs for it. By Definition 6.1 and A2, the informed and uninformed investors are the optimizing agents of the model, each with the CARA utility of Definition 6.2, and the supply \(u\) is a primitive, not a choice.
00372 “The regression slope of \(v\) on \(p\) equals the slope of \(p\) on \(s\).”
00373 The slope of \(p\) on \(s\) is \(\theta_s = 3/7\), and the slope of \(v\) on \(p\) is \(b = 7/9\) at the base instance (Lab 1, Parts A and B). The first describes how the price moves with the signal, and the second describes how the best linear forecast of the payoff moves with the price. They are different objects.
00374 “The price is pure noise to the uninformed, so they learn nothing from it.”
00375 The posterior variance of \(v\) given \(p\) is \(2/3\), which is below the prior variance \(\sigma_v^2 = 1\) (Lab 1, Part B; Corollary 6.8). The price lowers the uninformed investor's uncertainty, but it does not remove all of it.

00378 Mechanics
00379 M1 · Coefficients at a new instance
00380 Take \(\sigma_v^2 = 1\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 2\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(\sigma_u^2 = \tfrac14\). Compute \(\kappa\), \(\sigma_\eta^2\), \(\tau_\eta\), \(\bar\tau\), \(\theta_s^\ast\) and \(\theta_u^\ast\) from Proposition 6.7.
00381 Solution
00382 \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda = 2 \cdot 1 / \tfrac12 = 4\). Then \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + 16 \cdot \tfrac14 = 5\), so \(\tau_\eta = \tfrac15\). Next \(\bar\tau = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac15 = \tfrac35\). Then \(\theta_s^\ast = \tfrac{3/5}{1 + 3/5} = \tfrac38\) and \(\theta_u^\ast = 4 \cdot \tfrac38 = \tfrac32\).
00383 M2 · Projection slope and posterior variance
00384 For the instance of M1, compute the slope \(b\) of \(v\) on \(p\), the posterior variance \(\Var(v \mid p)\), and the squared correlation \(\Corr(v, p)^2\). Compute \(\Var(p)\) from A1 and the price rule of A3.
00385 Solution
00386 \[\Var(p) = (\tfrac38)^2 \cdot 2 + (\tfrac32)^2 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac{9}{32} + \tfrac{9}{16} = \tfrac{27}{32}\], and \(\Cov(v, p) = \tfrac38\). So \(b = (\tfrac38)/(\tfrac{27}{32}) = \tfrac49\). The posterior variance is \(\Var(v \mid p) = 1 - \tfrac49 \cdot \tfrac38 = \tfrac56\), which equals \(1/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta) = 1/(1 + \tfrac15) = \tfrac56\). The squared correlation is \((\tfrac38)^2 / (1 \cdot \tfrac{27}{32}) = \tfrac16\), which equals \(\sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) = 1/6\).
00387 M3 · Market clearing at one point
00388 For the instance of M1, the price is \(p = \theta_s^\ast s - \theta_u^\ast u\). At \((s, u) = (2, 1)\), compute the price, both demands and the value of \(\lambda x_I + (1 - \lambda) x_U\). Check that it equals \(u\).
00389 Solution
00390 The price is \(p = \tfrac38 \cdot 2 - \tfrac32 \cdot 1 = -\tfrac34\). The informed demand is \(x_I = (\tau_\varepsilon s - (\tau_v + \tau_\varepsilon)p)/a = (2 + \tfrac32)/2 = \tfrac74\). For the uninformed investor, \(Y = p/\theta_s^\ast = -2\), so \(x_U = (\tau_\eta Y - (\tau_v + \tau_\eta)p)/a = (-\tfrac25 + \tfrac{9}{10})/2 = \tfrac14\). Then \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac74 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac14 = 1 = u\), so the market clears at this point.
00391 Proof
00392 P1 · The informed belief does not depend on the price
00393 Under A1 and G, show that \(\E[v \mid s, p] = \omega s\) for every real \(\theta_s\) and \(\theta_u\), including \(\theta_s = 0\). Identify exactly which step of Lemma 6.3 uses each of A1 and G.
00394 Solution
00395 Lemma 6.3 does not use \(\theta_s\) or \(\theta_u\), because \(e = v - \omega s\) is uncorrelated with \(s\) and with \(u\) whatever the price coefficients are. The independence of \(u\) from \((v, \varepsilon)\), which A1 gives, makes \(\Cov(e, u) = 0\). A1 and G(i) make \((e, s, u)\) jointly normal, and G(ii) turns the zero covariances into independence of \(e\) from \((s, u)\). Since \(p\) is a function of \((s, u)\), \(e\) is independent of \((s, p)\). The mean of \(e\) is zero by A1, so the conditional mean is \(\omega s\).
00396 P2 · No equilibrium has a zero signal coefficient
00397 Show that any linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium has \(\theta_s \neq 0\), following Step 1 of the proof of Proposition 6.7 in full. State which hypothesis of A4 is used and at which points of \(\R^2\).
00398 Solution
00399 If \(\theta_s = 0\), then \(p = -\theta_u u\) is uncorrelated with \(v\), so the uninformed belief is zero and their demand is \(-p/(a\sigma_v^2)\), which depends only on \(u\). The informed demand still contains \(\tau_\varepsilon s/a\). With \(u = 0\), the clearing condition of A4 at \((s, 0)\) and \((s + 1, 0)\) differ by \(\lambda\tau_\varepsilon/a \neq 0\), but the supply \(u = 0\) is the same at both points, which contradicts A4.
00400 P3 · Variance of the uninformed posterior
00401 Show that \[\sigma_v^2 - \sigma_v^4/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) = \sigma_v^2\sigma_\eta^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)\], and that this equals \(1/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\). State the hypothesis under which each equality holds.
00402 Solution
00403 The first equality is the common-denominator identity \(\sigma_v^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) - \sigma_v^4 = \sigma_v^2\sigma_\eta^2\). For the second, divide the numerator and denominator by \(\sigma_v^2\sigma_\eta^2\) to get \(1/(\sigma_\eta^{-2} + \sigma_v^{-2}) = 1/(\tau_\eta + \tau_v)\). Both steps need only \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\) and \(\sigma_\eta^2 > 0\), which hold under A1 because \(\sigma_\eta^2 \geq \sigma_\varepsilon^2 > 0\).

00413 E3 · What the seeded check cannot show
00414 Lab 1, Part E reports that both sample slopes lie within tolerance. Give one reason why this does not test the model against observed prices, and state what the check does test.
00415 Solution

00418 R1 · The price as the informed mass vanishes
00419 Hold \(\sigma_v^2\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), \(a\) and \(\sigma_u^2\) fixed, and let \(\lambda \to 0\). Show that \(\kappa \to \infty\), \(\tau_\eta \to 0\), \(\theta_s^\ast \to 0\) and \(\Corr(v, p)^2 \to 0\). State what the limit means for the price, and say which assumption of the model is outside the range of this limit.
00420 Solution
00421 Since \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\), \(\kappa \to \infty\), so \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2 \to \infty\) and \(\tau_\eta \to 0\). Then \(\bar\tau = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta \to 0\), so \(\theta_s^\ast = \bar\tau/(\tau_v + \bar\tau) \to 0\). Since \(\theta_u^\ast = \kappa\theta_s^\ast\), \(\Var(p) = (\theta_s^\ast)^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2) = (\theta_s^\ast)^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)\) and \(\Cov(v, p) = \theta_s^\ast\sigma_v^2\), so \(\Corr(v, p)^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)\), which tends to \(0\) because \(\sigma_\eta^2 \to \infty\). In the limit the correlation between the payoff and the price tends to zero. The limit is outside A2, which requires \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\), and the proof does not extend to \(\lambda = 0\).
00422 R2 · Uninformed learning and the noise volume
00423 Show that \(\Var(v \mid p)\) is strictly increasing in \(\sigma_u^2\), with the other parameters fixed. Use Corollary 6.8 and state the sign of each step.
00424 Solution
00425 By Corollary 6.8, \(\Var(v \mid p) = 1/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\). The map \(\sigma_u^2 \mapsto \sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2\) is strictly increasing, since \(\kappa > 0\) does not depend on \(\sigma_u\). So \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\) is strictly decreasing, and \(\tau_v + \tau_\eta\) is strictly decreasing. The reciprocal of a strictly decreasing positive function is strictly increasing, which gives the claim.
00426 R3 · Non-normal payoffs
00427 Suppose the payoff \(v\) is not normal, while the signal and the supply are as in A1. Identify the steps of Lemmas 6.3 and 6.4 that use G, and name the conditional facts that Proposition 6.7 needs as explicit hypotheses. Do not claim that the linear formulas hold without those hypotheses.
00428 Solution
00429 Both lemmas use G to pass from zero covariance to independence: Lemma 6.3 for \(e = v - \omega s\) against \((s, u)\), and Lemma 6.4 for \(e = v - bp\) against \(p\). Without joint normality, zero covariance need not give independence, so neither conditional mean need be linear. The proof of Proposition 6.7 needs four facts: \(\E[v \mid s, p] = \omega s\), \(\Var(v \mid s, p) = V_I\), \(\E[v \mid p] = bp\) and \(\Var(v \mid p) = V_U\). The demands also use Lemma 6.2, which needs the conditional law of \(v\) given \((s, p)\) and given \(p\) to be normal, so that is a fifth fact. These can be stated as hypotheses. Under A1 with normal \(v\), all five follow from G. The lesson makes no claim about other payoff laws.

00502 With the price rule \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) substituted, the clearing residual in A4 is a linear function of \((s, u)\). Why does requiring clearing with probability one give the same coefficients as requiring it at every pair \((s, u)\)?
00503 Options
00504 A nonzero linear function of \((s, u)\) vanishes only on a line, which has probability zero under the normal law of \((s, u)\), so clearing with probability one already forces both coefficients of the residual to be zero
00505 Because the price must be positive in every state

00507 The residual has no constant term, because the demands and the price are linear in \((s, u)\). A nonzero one vanishes only on a line, and that line has probability zero because \((s, u)\) has a nondegenerate normal law. So a residual that is zero with probability one is zero at every pair. The pointwise form is used in Step 3 of Proposition 6.7, which evaluates the residual at two points, \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\).
