00020 A price is informative when a trader who sees it knows more about the payoff. That needs a number, and the number must respond to the things that change informativeness (Definition 7.2 below). This lesson uses the squared correlation between the payoff and the price. It shows that the number is the \(R^2\) of the best linear forecast of the payoff from the price (Lemma 7.4, for variables with zero means), that its closed form follows from the equilibrium of Lesson L06, and that, with the other parameters fixed, it falls as the noise supply grows and rises as more investors are informed (Proposition 7.7). Lesson L08 makes the informed fraction a choice, using the same measure.
00021 1A measure of informativeness
00022 Definition 7.1 (covariance, correlation and noise volume)
00023 For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) with \(\Var(Y) > 0\) and \(\Var(X) > 0\), the correlation is \(\Corr(X, Y) = \Cov(X, Y)/\sqrt{\Var(X)\Var(Y)}\). In the model of Lesson L06, the
00024 noise volume
00025 is the standard deviation \(\sigma_u\) of the net supply \(u\) of noise traders, so the noise volume is measured in units of the asset.
00026 Definition 7.2 (price informativeness)
00027 The
00028 price informativeness
00029 is the population squared correlation between the payoff and the price,
00030 \[
00031 \rho^2 = \Corr(v, p)^2 = \frac{\Cov(v, p)^2}{\Var(v)\, \Var(p)} .
00032 \]
00033 It is a number in \([0, 1]\) by Lemma 7.3, and it does not depend on the units of \(p\), by Remark 7.5.
00034 Lemma 7.3 (bounds on correlation)
00035 For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) with \(\Var(X) > 0\) and \(\Var(Y) > 0\), \(\Cov(X, Y)^2 \leq \Var(X)\Var(Y)\). Hence \(0 \leq \Corr(X, Y)^2 \leq 1\).
00036 Proof
00037 For every real \(t\), the variance \(q(t) = \Var(X + tY) = \Var(X) + 2t\,\Cov(X, Y) + t^2 \Var(Y)\) is nonnegative. This is a quadratic in \(t\) with leading coefficient \(\Var(Y) > 0\). A quadratic that is nonnegative for every real \(t\) has a discriminant that is not positive. The discriminant is \(4\Cov(X, Y)^2 - 4 \Var(X)\Var(Y)\), so \(\Cov(X, Y)^2 \leq \Var(X)\Var(Y)\). Dividing by \(\Var(X)\Var(Y) > 0\) gives the bounds.
00038 Lemma 7.4 (squared correlation is the \(R^2\) of the best linear forecast)
00039 Let \(X\) and \(Y\) have zero means, with \(\Var(X) > 0\) and \(\Var(Y) > 0\). The linear forecast \(bY\) with the smallest mean squared error \(\E(X - bY)^2\) has \(b = \Cov(X, Y)/\Var(Y)\). Its residual variance is \(\Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\), so the proportion of the variance of \(X\) that the forecast explains is \(1 - \Var(X - bY)/\Var(X) = \Corr(X, Y)^2\).
00040 Proof
00041 Since the means are zero, \(\E(X - bY)^2 = \Var(X) - 2b\,\Cov(X, Y) + b^2 \Var(Y)\). This quadratic in \(b\) has leading coefficient \(\Var(Y) > 0\), so it has a unique minimizer, at which the derivative \(-2\Cov(X, Y) + 2b\Var(Y)\) is zero. That gives \(b = \Cov(X, Y)/\Var(Y)\). At this \(b\), the value is \(\Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\). Dividing by \(\Var(X)\) and subtracting from 1 gives \(\Cov(X, Y)^2/(\Var(X)\Var(Y)) = \Corr(X, Y)^2\).
00042 Remark 7.5 (scaling)
00043 For any constant \(\alpha \neq 0\), \(\Corr(X, \alpha Y)^2 = \Corr(X, Y)^2\). The covariance is multiplied by \(\alpha\), and \(\Var(\alpha Y)\) is multiplied by \(\alpha^2\). The factors \(\alpha^2\) cancel in the ratio. So the informativeness of \(p\) and of \(p/\theta_s\) is the same, for any \(\theta_s \neq 0\).
00044 2Informativeness in the model
00045 Proposition 7.6 (closed form)
00046 Assume A1 to A4 and G of Lesson L06, with \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\) and the equilibrium price \(p = \theta_s^\ast s - \theta_u^\ast u\) of Proposition 6.7 of that lesson. Then
00047 \[
00048 \rho^2 = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2} = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \dfrac{a^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^4}{\lambda^2}\,\sigma_u^2} .
00049 \]
00050 Equivalently, \(\rho^2 = \tau_\eta/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\), where \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\) as defined in Proposition 6.7 of Lesson L06.
00051 Proof
00052 Let \(Y = p/\theta_s^\ast\). Since \(\theta_s^\ast = \bar\tau/(\tau_v + \bar\tau) > 0\), with \(\bar\tau = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta\) as defined in Proposition 6.7 of Lesson L06, the division is valid, and Step 2 of the proof of Proposition 6.7 gives \(Y = v + \eta\), where \(\eta = \varepsilon - \kappa u\) is independent of \(v\) and has variance \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2\). By Remark 7.5, \(\Corr(v, p)^2 = \Corr(v, Y)^2\). Since \(\Cov(v, Y) = \Cov(v, v) + \Cov(v, \eta) = \sigma_v^2\) and \(\Var(Y) = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2\), the squared correlation is \(\sigma_v^4/(\sigma_v^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)) = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)\). Substituting \(\kappa^2 = a^2\sigma_\varepsilon^4/\lambda^2\) gives the explicit form. The equivalent form follows because \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\) and \(\sigma_v^2 = 1/\tau_v\).
00053 Proposition 7.7 (the signs of the responses)
00054 Assume the hypotheses of Proposition 7.6, with \(\sigma_v > 0\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon > 0\), \(a > 0\), \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) and \(\sigma_u > 0\). Hold the other parameters fixed. Then \(\rho^2\) is strictly decreasing in \(\sigma_u\), strictly decreasing in \(a\), strictly decreasing in \(\sigma_\varepsilon\), and strictly increasing in \(\lambda\).
00055 Proof
00056 Write \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/D\) with \(D = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\,\sigma_u^2\) and \(\chi = a^2\sigma_\varepsilon^4/\lambda^2\). Since \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\), \(\rho^2\) is strictly decreasing in \(D\) and strictly increasing in \(1/D\). Each claim follows from the sign of the change in \(D\), because \(D > 0\) throughout. Increasing \(\sigma_u\) raises \(\chi\sigma_u^2\) if \(\chi > 0\), which is true here, so \(D\) rises. Increasing \(a\) raises \(\chi\), since \(\chi\) is proportional to \(a^2\) with \(a > 0\), so \(D\) rises. Increasing \(\lambda\) lowers \(\chi\), since \(\chi\) is proportional to \(\lambda^{-2}\), so \(D\) falls. Increasing \(\sigma_\varepsilon > 0\) raises \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) and \(\sigma_\varepsilon^4\), and both enter \(D\) with nonnegative coefficients, so \(D\) rises. Each conclusion is strict, because the changed term is strictly monotone in its argument.
00057 Remark 7.8 (the full-revelation benchmark)
00058 Let \(\rho_0^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\), which is \(\Corr(v, s)^2\) because \(\Cov(v, s) = \sigma_v^2\) and \(\Var(s) = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Proposition 7.6 gives \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\sigma_u^2)\) with \(\chi > 0\). So \(\rho^2 < \rho_0^2\) for every \(\sigma_u > 0\), and \(\rho^2 \to \rho_0^2\) as \(\sigma_u \to 0\). The benchmark is the informativeness of the signal itself. It is not reached when the supply is noisy. This lesson does not treat \(\sigma_u = 0\) as a case of Proposition 6.7, which assumes \(\sigma_u > 0\).
00059 Remark 7.9 (price variance is a different object)
00060 The variance of the price is \(\Var(p) = \theta_s^{\ast 2}(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2) + \theta_u^{\ast 2}\sigma_u^2\), which includes the supply term. The supply term adds to \(\Var(p)\). Lab 1, Part B, prints both quantities across a sweep of the noise volume, and on that sweep \(\Var(p)\) rises while \(\rho^2\) falls, as Proposition 7.7 says for \(\rho^2\) in \(\sigma_u\).
00061 Lab 1 · Informativeness from exact moments, sweeps and a seeded check
00062 The lab uses the base instance of Lesson L06, with \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and noise volume \(\sigma_u = \tfrac12\). Parts A to E are exact, computed with Fraction values. Before running it, the hypothesis is this. The squared correlation from the moments equals the closed form \(\rho^2 = 1/3\) (Proposition 7.6). Over the noise volumes \(\sigma_u \in \{1/4, 1/2, 1, 2, 4\}\), \(\rho^2\) falls strictly, while \(\Var(p)\) rises. Over \(\lambda \in \{1/4, 1/2, 3/4\}\), \(\rho^2\) rises strictly, and over \(a \in \{1/2, 1, 2\}\) it falls strictly (Proposition 7.7). Every noise-sweep value lies below the benchmark \(1/2\) (Remark 7.8). Part F is SIMULATED: one seeded draw of 20 000 observations, with seed 7, and the sample squared correlation should lie within 0.02 of \(1/3\), a tolerance stated in this hypothesis.

00096 class Coefficients(NamedTuple):
00097 """The linear price p = theta_s * s - theta_u * u.
00098 sig_eta2 is the variance of eta = eps - kappa * u, the noise in p / theta_s.
00099 """
00100 sig_eta2: Fraction
00101 theta_s: Fraction
00102 theta_u: Fraction
00103 def check_inputs(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, a: Fraction, lam: Fraction, sd: Fraction) -> None:
00104 """Raise ValueError unless the variances are positive, a is positive, lam lies in (0, 1) and sd >= 0."""
00105 if sig_v2 <= 0 or sig_e2 <= 0:
00106 raise ValueError("payoff and signal variances must be positive")
00107 if a <= 0:
00108 raise ValueError("CARA coefficient must be positive")
00109 if not 0 < lam < 1:
00110 raise ValueError("informed fraction must lie strictly between 0 and 1")
00111 if sd < 0:
00112 raise ValueError("noise volume must be nonnegative")
00113 def coefficients(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, a: Fraction, lam: Fraction, sd: Fraction) -> Coefficients:
00114 """The closed-form price coefficients of lesson L06, valid for sd > 0 and, at sd = 0, as the limit they define."""
00115 check_inputs(sig_v2, sig_e2, a, lam, sd)
00116 tau_v = 1 / sig_v2
00117 tau_e = 1 / sig_e2
00118 kappa = a / (lam * tau_e)
00119 sig_eta2 = sig_e2 + kappa * kappa * sd * sd
00120 tau_eta = 1 / sig_eta2
00121 tau_bar = lam * tau_e + (1 - lam) * tau_eta
00122 theta_s = tau_bar / (tau_v + tau_bar)
00123 return Coefficients(sig_eta2, theta_s, kappa * theta_s)
00124 def rho2_from_moments(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, coef: Coefficients, sd: Fraction) -> Fraction:
00125 """Corr(v, p)^2 from the moments of (v, p): Cov(v, p)^2 / (Var(v) Var(p))."""
00126 cov_vp = coef.theta_s * sig_v2
00127 var_p = coef.theta_s * coef.theta_s * (sig_v2 + sig_e2) + coef.theta_u * coef.theta_u * sd * sd
00128 return cov_vp * cov_vp / (sig_v2 * var_p)
00129 def rho2_closed(sig_v2: Fraction, coef: Coefficients) -> Fraction:
00130 """Corr(v, p)^2 = sig_v2 / (sig_v2 + sig_eta2): the squared correlation of v with Y = p / theta_s."""
00131 return sig_v2 / (sig_v2 + coef.sig_eta2)
00132 def var_price(sig_v2: Fraction, sig_e2: Fraction, coef: Coefficients, sd: Fraction) -> Fraction:
00133 """Var(p) = theta_s^2 (sig_v2 + sig_e2) + theta_u^2 sd^2."""
00134 return coef.theta_s * coef.theta_s * (sig_v2 + sig_e2) + coef.theta_u * coef.theta_u * sd * sd

00136 """The base instance: the moment formula and the closed form agree exactly."""
00137 print("Part A: informativeness at the base instance")

00139 moments = rho2_from_moments(SIG_V2, SIG_E2, coef, SD_BASE)
00140 closed = rho2_closed(SIG_V2, coef)
00141 print(f"base sig_v2 {SIG_V2} sig_e2 {SIG_E2} a {A_BASE} lam {LAM_BASE} noise_volume_sd {SD_BASE}")
00142 print(f"sig_eta2 {coef.sig_eta2} theta_s {coef.theta_s} theta_u {coef.theta_u}")
00143 print(f"rho2_from_moments {moments} rho2_closed_form {closed} equal {moments == closed}")
00144 return coef

00258 3Where the canon breaks
00259 Linear measure. \(\rho^2\) measures linear association. A price can carry information through a nonlinear function of the payoff while \(\rho^2 = 0\). Witness: let \(v \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) and \(p = v^2\). Then \(\Var(p) = \E[v^4] - 1 = 2 > 0\), and \(\Cov(v, p) = \E[v^3] - \E[v]\,\E[v^2] = 0\) because \(\E[v] = \E[v^3] = 0\) by symmetry, so \(\rho^2 = 0\). Yet \(p\) determines \(|v|\). Lemma 7.4 gives the \(R^2\) of the best linear forecast only.
00260 Model dependence. Proposition 7.6 uses A1 to A4 and G of Lesson L06. Under non-normal primitives, the closed form of Proposition 7.6 is not established by this proof, and the formula is not claimed.
00261 Population object. \(\rho^2\) is a population quantity. A sample correlation from finite data differs from it by sampling error, which Lab 1, Part F, shows for one seed.
00262 Fixed informed fraction. Proposition 7.7 holds \(\lambda\) fixed. When the informed fraction is itself chosen, \(\lambda\) is no longer a fixed parameter, and Lesson L08 treats that case.
00263 Units of noise. The noise volume is the standard deviation \(\sigma_u\) in units of the asset (Definition 7.1). The sweeps use that scale. A strictly increasing reparametrisation of the noise volume, such as \(\sigma_u^2\), changes the numbers but not the decreasing sign of Proposition 7.7 in \(\sigma_u\).
00264 4Misconceptions
00265 “A price with more variance is a more informative price.”
00266 In Lab 1, Part B, the variance of the price rises from \(729/1444\) to \(35937/4802\) across the noise sweep, while \(\rho^2\) falls from \(4/9\) to \(1/66\). Price variance contains the supply term \(\theta_u^2\sigma_u^2\), which carries no payoff information (Remark 7.9).
00267 “Informativeness is the precision of the noise in the price.”
00268 The precision of the price's noise in the uninformed inference is \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\). At the base instance \(\tau_\eta = 1/2\) (Lesson L06, Lab 1, Part A), but \(\rho^2 = 1/3\). The correct formula is \(\rho^2 = \tau_\eta/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\), which also depends on the precision of the prior, \(\tau_v = 1\).
00269 “Noise makes the price uninformative, so any positive noise volume gives zero informativeness.”
00270 Proposition 7.6 gives \(\rho^2 > 0\) for every finite \(\sigma_u\). At the largest noise volume on the sweep, \(\sigma_u = 4\), \(\rho^2 = 1/66\) is small but positive (Lab 1, Part B).
00271 “More informed investors make the price less informative, because their demands add noise.”
00272 The mechanism runs the other way. The price's signal is \(Y = s - \kappa u\) with \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\), so a larger informed fraction lowers \(\kappa\) and the noise that the supply adds to \(Y\). Proposition 7.7 gives a strictly increasing \(\rho^2\) in \(\lambda\), and Lab 1, Part C, shows \(1/6\), \(1/3\) and \(9/22\) for \(\lambda = 1/4, 1/2, 3/4\).

00275 Mechanics
00276 M1 · Informativeness at a higher noise volume
00277 Take \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(\sigma_u = 1\). Compute \(\sigma_\eta^2\) and \(\rho^2\) from Proposition 7.6.
00278 Solution
00279 Here \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda = 2\), so \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + 4 \cdot 1 = 5\). Then \(\rho^2 = 1/(1 + 5) = 1/6\). This matches the \(\sigma_u = 1\) row of Lab 1, Part B.
00280 M2 · A higher informed fraction
00281 Take \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\sigma_u = \tfrac12\) and \(\lambda = \tfrac34\). Compute \(\rho^2\).
00282 Solution
00283 \(\kappa = 1/(3/4) = 4/3\), so \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + (16/9)(1/4) = 13/9\). Then \(\rho^2 = 1/(1 + 13/9) = 9/22\). This matches the \(\lambda = 3/4\) row of Lab 1, Part C, and it is larger than the value \(1/3\) at \(\lambda = 1/2\).
00284 M3 · Two payoff variances
00285 Take \(\sigma_v^2 = 2\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(\sigma_u = 1\). Compute \(\rho^2\), and state whether a larger payoff variance raises or lowers \(\rho^2\) at these values.
00286 Solution
00287 \(\kappa = 2\) and \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + 4 = 5\), so \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) = 2/7\). Holding the other values fixed, \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 5\) does not depend on \(\sigma_v^2\), so \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + 5)\). Its derivative in \(\sigma_v^2\) is \(5/(\sigma_v^2 + 5)^2 > 0\), so a larger payoff variance raises \(\rho^2\) here.
00288 Proof
00289 P1 · The discriminant bound
00290 Prove Lemma 7.3 without quoting the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. State where you use \(\Var(Y) > 0\), and say what happens if \(\Var(Y) = 0\).
00291 Solution
00292 Expand \(q(t) = \Var(X + tY) = \Var(X) + 2t\Cov(X, Y) + t^2\Var(Y)\), which is nonnegative for every \(t\), because it is a variance. The leading coefficient \(\Var(Y)\) is positive, which makes \(q\) a genuine quadratic that is nonnegative everywhere, so its discriminant must be nonpositive. That gives \(\Cov(X, Y)^2 \leq \Var(X)\Var(Y)\). If \(\Var(Y) = 0\), then \(q\) is linear in \(t\), and nonnegativity forces \(\Cov(X, Y) = 0\), so the inequality still holds, but the proof above does not cover that case.
00293 P2 · The explained proportion is the squared correlation
00294 Under the zero-mean hypotheses of Lemma 7.4, show that the residual variance of the best linear forecast equals \(\Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\), using only the expansion of \(\E(X - bY)^2\).
00295 Solution
00296 With zero means, \(\E(X - bY)^2 = \E X^2 - 2b\E(XY) + b^2\E Y^2 = \Var(X) - 2b\Cov(X, Y) + b^2\Var(Y)\). Substituting \(b = \Cov(X, Y)/\Var(Y)\) gives \(\Var(X) - 2\Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y) + \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y) = \Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\). Every step uses only the zero means and the expansion.
00297 P3 · Scaling leaves the squared correlation unchanged
00298 Prove Remark 7.5 for a constant \(\alpha \neq 0\), and state which property of the covariance and the variance it uses.
00299 Solution
00300 \(\Cov(X, \alpha Y) = \alpha\Cov(X, Y)\) and \(\Var(\alpha Y) = \alpha^2 \Var(Y)\), by bilinearity of covariance and the rule \(\Var(\alpha Y) = \alpha^2\Var(Y)\). Then \(\Corr(X, \alpha Y)^2 = \alpha^2\Cov(X, Y)^2/(\Var(X)\alpha^2\Var(Y))\). The factor \(\alpha^2\) cancels, which needs \(\alpha \neq 0\) so that \(\Var(\alpha Y) > 0\).
00301 Empirical
00302 E1 · Reading the seeded check
00303 Lab 1, Part F, prints a sample squared correlation of 0.330625, against the population value \(1/3\) and a tolerance of 0.02. Compute the difference in units of the tolerance, and state whether the check passes.
00304 Solution
00305 The difference is \(1/3 - 0.330625 = 13/4800 \approx 0.002708\), where \(0.330625\) is the sample value that Lab 1, Part F prints, rounded to six decimals, and \(\approx\) marks rounding to six decimals. In units of the tolerance it is \((13/4800)/0.02 = 13/96 \approx 0.135\), rounded to three decimals. The check passes, because the absolute difference is below the tolerance. The result is one simulated draw, labelled SIMULATED, so it does not measure the sampling distribution of the estimator.
00306 E2 · Recovering a noise volume from a reported \(R^2\)
00307 A hypothetical regression of the payoff on the price reports \(R^2 = 0.25\). The numbers are invented, not data. Assume the parameters \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\) and \(\lambda = \tfrac12\). What noise volume \(\sigma_u\) makes the population \(\rho^2\) equal to 0.25?
00308 Solution
00309 Treat the reported \(R^2\) as the population \(\rho^2\). This is an assumption, and Problem E3 explains why a sample \(R^2\) can differ from \(\rho^2\). Setting \(1/(1 + \sigma_\eta^2) = 1/4\) gives \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 3\). With \(\kappa = 2\), \(1 + 4\sigma_u^2 = 3\), so \(\sigma_u^2 = 1/2\) and \(\sigma_u = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.7071\). This inversion assumes the model is correct, which is what the regression cannot test by itself.
00310 E3 · Why a sample \(R^2\) is not the population measure
00311 Give two reasons why a regression \(R^2\) from a finite sample of prices can differ from \(\rho^2\), and say which of them Lab 1, Part F, can and cannot detect.
00312 Solution
00313 First, a finite sample has sampling error, so the sample \(R^2\) fluctuates around \(\rho^2\). Second, the regression may be misspecified, for example if the true forecast is nonlinear, so the linear \(R^2\) differs from the best forecast's explained share. Lab 1, Part F, detects only the first, and only for data generated by the model. It cannot detect misspecification, because the sample comes from the model itself.
00314 Research
00315 R1 · Informativeness as the CARA coefficient grows
00316 Hold the other parameters fixed and let \(a \to \infty\). Show that \(\rho^2 \to 0\), and identify the step of Proposition 7.7 that carries the limit.
00317 Solution
00318 Since \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\) grows without bound, \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2 \to \infty\) when \(\sigma_u > 0\). Then \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) \to 0\). The step that carries the limit is the increase of \(\chi\) with \(a\), which makes \(D\) grow without bound.
00319 R2 · Half of the benchmark
00320 Find the noise volume \(\sigma_u\), in closed form, at which \(\rho^2\) equals half of the full-revelation benchmark \(\rho_0^2\) of Remark 7.8. Then evaluate it at \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\) and \(\lambda = \tfrac12\).
00321 Solution
00322 Setting \(\sigma_v^2/D = \tfrac12\sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\) gives \(D = 2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\). Since \(D = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\sigma_u^2\), this gives \(\chi\sigma_u^2 = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2\), so \(\sigma_u = \sqrt{(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)/\chi}\) with \(\chi = a^2\sigma_\varepsilon^4/\lambda^2\). At the stated values \(\chi = 4\) and \(\sigma_u = \sqrt{2/4} = 1/\sqrt{2}\).
00323 R3 · The derivative in the noise volume
00324 Compute \(\partial\rho^2/\partial(\sigma_u^2)\) from Proposition 7.6, and state its sign for every parameter set of Proposition 7.7. Show that it is not a constant multiple of \(\rho^2\).
00325 Solution
00326 With \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/D\) and \(D = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\sigma_u^2\), the derivative is \(-\sigma_v^2 \chi/D^2\). Its sign is negative, because \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\), \(\chi > 0\) and \(D > 0\). The ratio of the derivative to \(\rho^2\) is \(-\chi/D\), which depends on \(D\), and \(D\) changes with \(\sigma_u\). So the ratio of the derivative to \(\rho^2\) is not constant in \(\sigma_u\), and the derivative is not a constant multiple of \(\rho^2\).
