0006| General Equilibrium & Information in Markets
0007| A price is an outcome of many decisions made at once, and a price also carries information that other traders can read. This module asks what a competitive equilibrium is when claims are contingent on the state of the world, when it is efficient, and what it implies for the information that prices reveal. It starts with Arrow–Debreu securities and equilibrium (Lessons 1 and 2), proves the first welfare theorem (Lesson 3), and compares complete with incomplete markets (Lesson 4). It then studies rational-expectations equilibria with and without noise traders (Lessons 5 and 6), the informativeness of the price (Lesson 7), the Grossman–Stiglitz paradox (Lesson 8) and the no-trade theorem (Lesson 9). It ends in a price-informativeness project: a solver for a noisy rational-expectations model, and a report on how the informed fraction (Lesson 8) and the price's informativeness (Lesson 7) respond to the noise and to the cost of information.

0009| Unlocks: AP1 No-Arbitrage & the Stochastic Discount Factor, AP6 Intermediary & Institutional Asset Pricing, MS1 Microstructure Theory and SR1 Runs, Liquidity & Fire Sales.

0025| Placement diagnostic
0026| Eight questions: six on the prerequisites, and two skill checks, marked as such, on what rungs 1 and 2 of the project teach. The mastery bar is 80 percent. For eight items that means seven correct: six of eight is 75 percent, and seven of eight is 87.5 percent. To skip rungs 1 and 2, you must reach the bar and answer both skill checks. A result below the bar is not a judgement of the rest of your work; it means that rungs 1 and 2 are worth reading.
0027| A lottery pays 0 cents with probability 1/4 and 8 cents with probability 3/4. What is its expected value, in cents?
0028| Answer
0029| \(0 \cdot \tfrac14 + 8 \cdot \tfrac34 = 6\) cents. This is the expectation of M4, which the first lesson uses for the expected payoff of a claim.
0030| In a market where sellers know the quality of what they sell and buyers do not, what is adverse selection?
0031| Options High-quality sellers withdraw, because the price a buyer pays reflects the average quality offered, and gains from trade are lost Buyers pay more for higher quality, so every seller trades A seller shows its quality through a costly action that a low-quality seller would not find worth taking
0032| Adverse selection, defined in E2 Lesson 3: when sellers know the quality and buyers do not, the price reflects the average quality of the goods offered at that price, high-quality sellers can withdraw, and gains from trade are lost. The costly action in the last option describes signaling (E2 Lesson 4), not adverse selection.
0033| At an interior maximum of \(f(x)\) subject to \(g(x) = c\), with multiplier \(\zeta\), which condition holds?
0034| Options \(\nabla f(x) = \zeta \nabla g(x)\) \(f(x) = \zeta g(x)\) \(\nabla f(x) = 0\) regardless of the constraint
0035| The Lagrange condition of M7. The constraint enters through its gradient and the multiplier, and the gradient of \(f\) need not vanish.
0036| In a competitive equilibrium, how does an agent treat the prices?
0037| Options As given, and it chooses the best bundle it can afford As a choice it can influence by its own trades As equal to the probabilities of the states
0038| The price-taking assumption of E1 competitive equilibrium. Lesson 2 uses it in Definition 2.2.
0039| A lottery pays 0 or 4 with probability 1/2 each. Under \(u(x) = x^{2}\), what is its expected utility?
0040| Answer
0041| \(\tfrac12 \cdot 0^2 + \tfrac12 \cdot 4^2 = 8\), the expected-utility sum of E3.
0042| Which of these maps from \(\R^{3}\) to \(\R\) is linear?
0043| Options \(x \mapsto q_1 x_1 + q_2 x_2 + q_3 x_3\) \(x \mapsto (x_1 + x_2 + x_3)^{2}\) \(x \mapsto 1 + x_1 + x_2 + x_3\)
0044| A linear map sends sums to sums and scalar multiples to scalar multiples (M3). The square and the constant 1 both fail, and the constant fails because it does not send the zero vector to zero.
0045| Skill check (rung 1): as a function of the informed fraction \(\lambda\), the gap is positive at \(\lambda = 0\), negative at \(\lambda = 1\) and negative at \(\lambda = \tfrac12\). In the bisection on \([0, 1]\), which bracket does the next step keep?
0046| Options \([0, \tfrac12]\) \([\tfrac12, 1]\) \([0, 1]\), because the gap changes sign somewhere on the interval
0047| The gap changes sign between \(0\) and \(\tfrac12\), so the bisection keeps the half \([0, \tfrac12]\) that contains the sign change. The worked example in rung 1 prints the bracket after the first three halvings.
0048| Skill check (rung 2): with risk aversion \(a\), information cost \(c\), and the uninformed and informed terms \(F_U\) and \(F_I\) of the project's Proposition 3, the expected utility net of cost is \(-e^{ac}F_I\) for an informed investor and \(-F_U\) for an uninformed one. What is the gap \(G\), the informed utility minus the uninformed utility?
0049| Options \(F_U - e^{ac} F_I\) \(e^{ac} F_I - F_U\) \(F_I - e^{ac} F_U\)
0050| \(G = -e^{ac}F_I - (-F_U) = F_U - e^{ac}F_I\), the gap of Proposition 3 on the project page. This is the return line of indifference_gap that rung 2 asks you to complete.
0051| Lessons
0052| Nine lessons of eight study hours each, which is 72 hours in total. Lessons 1 to 3 give the model of an Arrow–Debreu economy and its efficiency. Lessons 4 and 5 treat completeness and the rational-expectations equilibrium. Lessons 6 to 8 add noise traders, price informativeness and the cost of information. Lesson 9 proves the no-trade theorem in a finite model. Each lesson follows the same structure: definitions and proofs, inline labs, misconceptions, a problem set with worked solutions and a retrieval quiz that feeds your review deck.
0053| L01 · 8 hArrow–Debreu securities and state-contingent payoffsFinite state spaces, claims as payoff vectors, the Arrow–Debreu security of each state, the decomposition of any claim into securities, and the value of a claim as the sum of security prices times payoffs under the linear valuation of Assumption A2 in Lesson 1. L02 · 8 hArrow–Debreu equilibriumBudget sets and demands in an exchange economy with contingent goods, Walras' law from binding budgets, and the closed-form equilibrium with logarithmic utility, with a two-agent example checked exactly. L03 · 8 hThe first welfare theoremFeasible allocations, Pareto improvements, the marginal rate of substitution between states, the proof that an equilibrium admits no Pareto improvement, and the equality of marginal rates with price ratios at interior equilibrium bundles. L04 · 8 hComplete and Incomplete MarketsThe payoff matrix of traded securities, its rank, the criterion for complete markets, and an exact rank computation on a complete and an incomplete example. L05 · 8 hRational-Expectations EquilibriumA linear-normal one-period model in which agents' price expectations are conditional expectations under the equilibrium price map, the fixed point of the coefficient map, and its exact check. L06 · 8 hNoisy rational-expectations equilibriumNoise-trader supply added to the price map, the linear projection of the payoff on the price, the population price coefficients, and a seeded sample check labelled SIMULATED. L07 · 8 hPrice informativenessThe squared correlation between the payoff and the price as the measure of informativeness, its range, its dependence on the noise volume from the population formula, and a seeded check labelled SIMULATED. L08 · 8 hThe Grossman–Stiglitz ParadoxInformed and uninformed expected utilities, the indifference condition that fixes the informed fraction, its computation by bisection with a stated tolerance, and what the model implies for the informativeness of prices when information is costly. L09 · 8 hThe No-Trade TheoremTwo agents with a common prior and partitions of a finite state space, posteriors as conditional probabilities, and the finite-model no-trade result under its stated assumptions.
