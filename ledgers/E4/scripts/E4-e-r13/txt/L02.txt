Lesson L01 priced claims once the security prices were known. This lesson asks where those prices come from. Two agents trade one good in each state. Each agent chooses the best affordable bundle at the prices, and an equilibrium is a price vector together with chosen bundles that add up to what the economy holds. The lesson proves that budgets bind, and, under A1, A3 and A4, that the value of the excess demand (Definition 2.3) is zero at any positive price vector at which each agent has a demand (Proposition 2.5). It then solves one economy in closed form, with logarithmic utility. Existence in general is not proved here, and the lesson says where it is assumed.
1Economy, budgets and demand
There are two agents, \(i = 1, 2\), and the states of Lesson L01, with probabilities \(\pi_s\) and A1 in force. Each agent starts with an endowment \(e^i \in \R^{S}\), the bundle it holds before any trade, and the aggregate endowment is \(\bar e = e^1 + e^2\). The letter \(e\) with an agent superscript names an endowment in this lesson; the Arrow–Debreu security \(e_s\) of Lesson L01 carries only a state subscript and is not used in this lesson. A bundle is a vector \(x \in \R^{S}_{+}\) of nonnegative amounts of the good in each state.
        Assumption A3 (strictly positive endowments)
For each agent \(i\) and each state \(s\), \(e^i_s > 0\). Hence \(\bar e_s > 0\) for every \(s\).
        Assumption A4 (utility)
Agent \(i\) has a function \(u_i\) on \((0, \infty)\) that is strictly increasing, strictly concave and differentiable. Its expected utility of a bundle is
        \[
          U_i(x) = \sum_{s=1}^{S} \pi_s\, u_i(x_s) \quad \text{when } x_s > 0 \text{ for all } s, \qquad U_i(x) = -\infty \text{ when some } x_s = 0 .
        \]
        The convention \(U_i = -\infty\) at a bundle with a zero entry is the one that the logarithm needs. The display in A4 sets \(U_i = -\infty\) at a zero entry, so the value of \(u_i\) at zero never enters; no result below uses it.
Assumption A4 holds for \(u_i(x) = \ln x\), which is the example of §3 and §4. Its expected utility at a bundle with a zero entry is \(-\infty\), which is the convention above. The model assumes that both agents use the same probabilities \(\pi_s\). The agents may differ in their utilities and endowments.
        Definition 2.1 (budget set and wealth)
At a price vector \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\), the wealth of agent \(i\) is \(w_i = q \cdot e^i\), and its budget set is
        \[
          B_i(q) = \{ x \in \R^{S}_{+} : q \cdot x \le w_i \}.
        \]
        The price \(q_s\) is the number of units of value an agent must give up to receive one unit of the good in state \(s\), and the wealth is the value of the endowment at those prices.
        Definition 2.2 (demand)
A demand of agent \(i\) at prices \(q\) is a bundle \(x^i(q) \in B_i(q)\) with \(U_i(x^i(q)) \ge U_i(x)\) for every \(x \in B_i(q)\). Agents take prices as given, which is the price-taking assumption behind the competitive equilibrium of the prerequisite module E1.
        Definition 2.3 (Arrow–Debreu equilibrium)
An Arrow–Debreu equilibrium is a pair \((q^*, (x^{1*}, x^{2*}))\) with \(q^* \in (0, \infty)^{S}\) such that each \(x^{i*}\) is a demand of agent \(i\) at \(q^*\), and markets clear: \(x^{1*} + x^{2*} = \bar e\). Here \(x^i(q)\) denotes the demand chosen for agent \(i\) at \(q\), and \(z(q) = x^1(q) + x^2(q) - \bar e\) is the excess demand computed from those choices, so markets clear at \(q^*\) exactly when \(z(q^*) = 0\).
        Assumption A5 (existence)
The economy under study has an Arrow–Debreu equilibrium. A5 is a hypothesis, not a result. No proposition below proves A5 for a general economy. Where a result needs an equilibrium, its statement names A5 or a given equilibrium. The example of §4 exhibits an equilibrium by construction, so A5 is not assumed there.
2Budgets that bind and the value of excess demand
        Lemma 2.4 (an optimal bundle is strictly positive)
Assume A1, A3 and A4. Let \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\) and \(w_i > 0\). Every demand of agent \(i\) at \(q\) has \(x_s > 0\) for every state \(s\).
        Proof
Let \(\iota\) be the vector of ones, \(\iota = (1, \ldots, 1)\), and let \(y = \bigl(w_i / (2\, q \cdot \iota)\bigr)\iota\). Then \(q \cdot y = w_i/2 \le w_i\), so \(y \in B_i(q)\), and \(y\) has positive entries, so \(U_i(y)\) is finite. A demand \(x\) with a zero entry has \(U_i(x) = -\infty < U_i(y)\) by A4, which contradicts the definition of a demand. So every demand is strictly positive.
        Proposition 2.5 (Walras' law)
Assume A1, A3 and A4. Let \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\), and let \(x^1\) and \(x^2\) be demands at \(q\). Then
        \[
          q \cdot \bigl(x^1 + x^2 - \bar e\bigr) = 0, \qquad \text{that is,} \qquad q \cdot z(q) = 0 .
        \]
        The budgets bind: \(q \cdot x^i = w_i\) for each \(i\).
        Proof
Fix \(i\) and write \(x = x^i\). Here \(w_i = q \cdot e^i > 0\), because \(q\) is strictly positive and \(e^i\) is strictly positive by A3, so Lemma 2.4 applies. By Lemma 2.4, \(x\) is strictly positive, so \(U_i(x)\) is finite. Suppose \(q \cdot x < w_i\). Let \(\delta > 0\) be small enough that \(q \cdot (x + \delta \iota) \le w_i\), which is possible because \(q \cdot (x + \delta \iota)\) tends to \(q \cdot x < w_i\) as \(\delta\) tends to 0. Then \(x + \delta \iota \in B_i(q)\). Since \(x + \delta \iota\) has positive entries and exceeds \(x\) in every state, A4 and A1 give \(U_i(x + \delta \iota) > U_i(x)\). This contradicts the demand property of \(x\). Hence \(q \cdot x = w_i\). Summing over the two agents, \(q \cdot (x^1 + x^2) = w_1 + w_2 = q \cdot (e^1 + e^2) = q \cdot \bar e\), which is the displayed identity.
        Remark 2.6 (what Walras' law does not say)
Proposition 2.5 is a statement about the value of the excess demand, not about each market. The unweighted excess demands need not add to zero. At \(q = (2, 1, 1)\) in the economy of Example 2.11 (§4), the wealths are \(w_1 = 11\) and \(w_2 = 9\), and Proposition 2.8 (§3) gives the demands \((\tfrac{11}{4}, \tfrac{11}{4}, \tfrac{11}{4})\) and \((\tfrac94, \tfrac94, \tfrac94)\). The excess demand is therefore \((1, -3, 1)\), whose unweighted sum is \(-1\), while \(q \cdot z = 0\). Misconception 2 of this lesson gives a vector with zero unweighted sum that is not an excess demand at the prices of Example 2.11.
        Corollary 2.7 (the last market clears)
Assume A1, A3 and A4, let \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\), and let \(x^1\) and \(x^2\) be demands at \(q\), so that \(z(q)\) is defined. If \(z_t(q) = 0\) for every state \(t\) other than \(s\), then \(z_s(q) = 0\).
        Proof
By Proposition 2.5, \(\sum_{t} q_t z_t(q) = 0\). Every term with \(t \ne s\) vanishes by hypothesis, so \(q_s z_s(q) = 0\). Since \(q_s > 0\), \(z_s(q) = 0\).
3Logarithmic utility and the closed-form demand
The demand under logarithmic utility has an explicit form. Take \(u_i(x) = \ln x\), which satisfies A4, so that \(U_i(x) = \sum_s \pi_s \ln x_s\) on the strictly positive bundles.
        Proposition 2.8 (logarithmic demand)
Assume A1. Let \(q \in (0, \infty)^{S}\) and \(w > 0\), and let \(U(x) = \sum_s \pi_s \ln x_s\). Then the budget set \(\{x \in \R^{S}_{+} : q \cdot x \le w\}\) has exactly one maximizer of \(U\), namely
        \[
          x^*_s = \frac{\pi_s\, w}{q_s}, \qquad s = 1, \ldots, S.
        \]
        Hence the demand is unique, and it spends the share \(\pi_s\) of wealth on each state, because \(q_s x^*_s = \pi_s w\).
        Proof
The bundle \(x^*\) is strictly positive and satisfies \(q \cdot x^* = \sum_s \pi_s w = w\), using A1 for \(\sum_s \pi_s = 1\). So \(x^*\) lies in the budget set. Let \(x\) be any bundle in the budget set with \(U(x) > -\infty\), so every \(x_s > 0\). The tangent-line inequality for the strictly concave function \(\ln\) gives
        \[
          \ln x_s \le \ln x^*_s + \frac{x_s - x^*_s}{x^*_s}, \qquad \text{with equality if and only if } x_s = x^*_s .
        \]
        Multiply each inequality by \(\pi_s\) and sum. Since \(\pi_s / x^*_s = q_s / w\) by the formula for \(x^*\),
        \[
          U(x) \le U(x^*) + \sum_s \frac{\pi_s}{x^*_s} (x_s - x^*_s) = U(x^*) + \frac{1}{w} \sum_s q_s (x_s - x^*_s) = U(x^*) + \frac{q \cdot x - w}{w} \le U(x^*).
        \]
        Hence \(U(x) \le U(x^*)\), and \(U(x) = U(x^*)\) forces equality in every tangent inequality, so \(x_s = x^*_s\) for every \(s\). A bundle with a zero entry has \(U(x) = -\infty < U(x^*)\). So \(x^*\) is the unique maximizer.
The maximizer also satisfies the KKT conditions of the problem \(\max U(x)\) subject to \(q \cdot x - w \le 0\) and \(x \ge 0\). These conditions are \(\pi_s / x_s = \mu\, q_s\) for each state, \(\mu \ge 0\), and \(\mu\,(q \cdot x - w) = 0\). The bundle \(x^*\) satisfies them with the multiplier \(\mu = 1/w\). Indeed \(\pi_s / x^*_s = q_s / w\) by the formula for \(x^*\), the budget binds, so the complementary slackness condition holds, and the nonnegativity constraints are slack because every \(x^*_s > 0\). Lab 2, Part D prints the ratio \(\pi_s / (x_s q_s)\), which is \(1/w\). The proof above does not use these conditions, and the lesson does not claim here that they are necessary or sufficient.
4An equilibrium in closed form
        Proposition 2.9 (equilibrium with logarithmic utility)
Assume A1 and A3, and that both agents have the utility \(u_i(x) = \ln x\), which satisfies A4. Define, for each agent,
        \[
          \theta_i = \sum_{s=1}^{S} \pi_s \frac{e^i_s}{\bar e_s}, \qquad q^*_s = \frac{\pi_s}{\bar e_s}.
        \]
        Then \(\theta_1 + \theta_2 = 1\), \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\), and \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\) for each agent and each state is an Arrow–Debreu equilibrium with \(q^*\). Each agent's wealth at \(q^*\) is \(w_i = \theta_i\).
        Proof
First, \(\theta_1 + \theta_2 = \sum_s \pi_s (e^1_s + e^2_s)/\bar e_s = \sum_s \pi_s = 1\), using \(e^1_s + e^2_s = \bar e_s\). Next, \(q^* \cdot \bar e = \sum_s (\pi_s / \bar e_s) \bar e_s = \sum_s \pi_s = 1\). The price vector is strictly positive by A3. The wealth of agent \(i\) at \(q^*\) is \(w_i = \sum_s q^*_s e^i_s = \sum_s \pi_s e^i_s / \bar e_s = \theta_i\), which is positive by A3. By Proposition 2.8, the unique demand of agent \(i\) is \(x^{i*}_s = \pi_s \theta_i / q^*_s = \pi_s \theta_i \bar e_s / \pi_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\). Market clearing holds because \(x^{1*}_s + x^{2*}_s = (\theta_1 + \theta_2) \bar e_s = \bar e_s\) for each state.
        Remark 2.10 (the scale of the price is not fixed by demand)
Under logarithmic utility, demand depends on the price and wealth only through \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\). Replacing \((q, w)\) by \((\psi q, \psi w)\) for \(\psi > 0\) leaves \(x\) unchanged. The normalization \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\) is a choice of scale, and it fixes the wealth shares \(w_i = \theta_i\). Proposition 2.9 does not prove that \(q^*\) is the only equilibrium price vector.
        Example 2.11 (three states, two agents)
Let \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)\), \(e^1 = (3, 4, 1)\) and \(e^2 = (1, 4, 3)\). The aggregate is \(\bar e = (4, 8, 4)\). Proposition 2.9 gives \(\theta_1 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac34 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac{9}{16}\) and \(\theta_2 = \tfrac{7}{16}\). The prices are \(q^* = (\tfrac18, \tfrac{1}{32}, \tfrac1{16})\), and the equilibrium bundles are \(x^{1*} = (\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) and \(x^{2*} = (\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\). Lab 2 computes these values in exact arithmetic.
Lab 2 · Equilibrium of the three-state economy, exact
The lab uses no data. It solves the economy of Example 2.11 in exact Fractions, with the closed forms of Propositions 2.8 and 2.9. Before running it, the hypothesis is this. The aggregate is \((4, 8, 4)\), the wealth shares are \(\tfrac9{16}\) and \(\tfrac7{16}\), which sum to 1, and the prices are \((\tfrac18, \tfrac1{32}, \tfrac1{16})\), with \(q \cdot \bar e = 1\). The demands are \((\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) for agent 1 and \((\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\) for agent 2. The budget residuals, the market-clearing residuals in each state, and the Walras residual are all zero (Proposition 2.5). The ratio \(\pi_s / (x_s q_s)\) equals \(\tfrac{16}{9}\) for agent 1 and \(\tfrac{16}{7}\) for agent 2 in every state, which is one over the agent's wealth. Scaling every price by 3 leaves both demands unchanged when wealth is re-valued at the new prices, so that wealth equals the value of the endowment (Remark 2.10). An economy with a zero endowment entry is rejected with a ValueError. No random draws are used.
        """E4 L02 lab 1: an Arrow-Debreu equilibrium of a two-agent exchange economy with logarithmic utility, exact.
Inputs are illustrative numbers, not data. There are three states with probabilities PROBS, which sum to exactly 1.
One good is delivered in each state. Agent i has the strictly positive endowment ENDOWMENTS[i] in those three states,
and the expected utility of a bundle x is the sum over states of PROBS[s] times ln(x[s]). All quantities are exact
Fractions, and no rounding occurs.
The economy is solved in closed form. The price of the good in state s is q[s] = PROBS[s] / aggregate[s], which
gives q times the aggregate endowment equal to 1 (the normalization). Each agent's wealth is w = q times its
endowment, and its demand is x[s] = PROBS[s] * w / q[s], with multiplier 1 / w.
Part A: the economy, the aggregate endowment and each agent's share of wealth, which sum to 1.
Part B: the equilibrium price vector and the normalization.
Part C: each agent's demand, with its budget residual, and the market-clearing residual in each state, all zero.
Part D: the first-order ratio PROBS[s] / (x[s] q[s]) for each agent in each state, which equals the agent's multiplier.
Part E: demand is unchanged when every price is multiplied by 3 and wealth is re-valued at the new prices (wealth equals
the value of the endowment), so demand does not fix the scale of the prices.
Part F: an economy with a non-positive (zero) endowment entry is rejected with a ValueError.
No random draws are used, so every run prints the same lines.
"""
from __future__ import annotations
from fractions import Fraction
Payoff = tuple[Fraction, ...]
PROBS: Payoff = (Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1, 4), Fraction(1, 4))
ENDOWMENTS: tuple[Payoff, ...] = (
    (Fraction(3), Fraction(4), Fraction(1)),
    (Fraction(1), Fraction(4), Fraction(3)),
)
def check_economy(probabilities: Payoff, endowments: tuple[Payoff, ...]) -> None:
    """Raise ValueError unless the probabilities are positive and sum to 1, and every endowment entry is positive."""
    if any(p <= 0 for p in probabilities):
        raise ValueError("state probabilities must be positive")
    if sum(probabilities, Fraction(0)) != 1:
        raise ValueError("state probabilities must sum to 1")
    for endowment in endowments:
        if any(e <= 0 for e in endowment):
            raise ValueError("endowments must be strictly positive in every state")
def aggregate(endowments: tuple[Payoff, ...]) -> Payoff:
    """The aggregate endowment in each state, the sum of the agents' endowments."""
    n_states = len(endowments[0])
    return tuple(sum((e[s] for e in endowments), Fraction(0)) for s in range(n_states))
def dot(a: Payoff, b: Payoff) -> Fraction:
    """The inner product of two vectors with one entry per state, as an exact Fraction."""
    return sum((x * y for x, y in zip(a, b, strict=True)), Fraction(0))
def equilibrium_prices(probabilities: Payoff, total: Payoff) -> Payoff:
    """The price of the good in each state, q[s] = probabilities[s] / total[s], normalized so that q . total = 1."""
    return tuple(p / t for p, t in zip(probabilities, total, strict=True))
def demand(probabilities: Payoff, prices: Payoff, wealth: Fraction) -> Payoff:
    """The logarithmic-utility demand x[s] = probabilities[s] * wealth / prices[s], for a positive wealth."""
    if wealth <= 0:
        raise ValueError("wealth must be positive")
    return tuple(p * wealth / q for p, q in zip(probabilities, prices, strict=True))
def main() -> None:
    """Print Parts A to F. Every value is an exact Fraction, so the output is reproducible."""
    check_economy(PROBS, ENDOWMENTS)
    total = aggregate(ENDOWMENTS)
    prices = equilibrium_prices(PROBS, total)
    wealth = [dot(prices, e) for e in ENDOWMENTS]
    print("Part A: economy, aggregate endowment and wealth shares")
    print(f"probabilities {tuple(str(p) for p in PROBS)} aggregate {tuple(str(z) for z in total)}")
    for i, e in enumerate(ENDOWMENTS, start=1):
        print(f"agent {i} endowment {tuple(str(z) for z in e)} wealth_share {wealth[i - 1]}")
    print(f"shares_sum_to_one {sum(wealth, Fraction(0)) == 1}")
    print("Part B: equilibrium prices and the normalization")
    print(f"prices {tuple(str(q) for q in prices)}")
    print(f"prices_dot_aggregate {dot(prices, total)}")
    print("Part C: demands, budget residuals and market clearing")
    demands = [demand(PROBS, prices, w) for w in wealth]
    for i, (x, e) in enumerate(zip(demands, ENDOWMENTS, strict=True), start=1):
        print(f"agent {i} demand {tuple(str(z) for z in x)}")
        print(f"agent {i} budget_residual {dot(prices, x) - dot(prices, e)}")
    for s in range(len(PROBS)):
        clearing = sum((x[s] for x in demands), Fraction(0)) - total[s]
        print(f"state {s} market_clearing_residual {clearing}")
    excess = tuple(
        sum((x[s] - e[s] for x, e in zip(demands, ENDOWMENTS, strict=True)), Fraction(0)) for s in range(len(PROBS))
    )
    print(f"walras_law_residual {dot(prices, excess)}")
    print("Part D: first-order ratio PROBS[s] / (x[s] q[s]) for each agent")
    for i, x in enumerate(demands, start=1):
        ratios = tuple(PROBS[s] / (x[s] * prices[s]) for s in range(len(PROBS)))
        same = all(r == 1 / wealth[i - 1] for r in ratios)
        print(f"agent {i} ratios {tuple(str(r) for r in ratios)} equal_to_one_over_wealth {same}")
    print("Part E: scaling every price by 3, with wealth re-valued at the new prices, leaves demand unchanged")
    scaled = tuple(3 * q for q in prices)
    for i, e in enumerate(ENDOWMENTS, start=1):
        again = demand(PROBS, scaled, dot(scaled, e))
        print(f"agent {i} demand_unchanged {again == demands[i - 1]}")
    print("Part F: an economy with a non-positive endowment is rejected")
    bad = ((Fraction(3), Fraction(4), Fraction(1)), (Fraction(1), Fraction(0), Fraction(3)))
    try:
        check_economy(PROBS, bad)
    except ValueError as exc:
        print(f"rejected message {exc}")
main()

Part A prints the wealth shares, \(\tfrac9{16}\) and \(\tfrac7{16}\), and the flag shows that they sum to 1. Part B prints the prices and the value \(q \cdot \bar e = 1\). Part C prints both demands and every residual. The five budget and market-clearing residuals (two budget, three market-clearing) and the Walras residual are 0 exactly, so with the demands given by the formula of Proposition 2.8, these bundles satisfy Definition 2.3 for this economy. Part D prints the ratio \(\pi_s / (x_s q_s)\) for each agent and states that it equals one over the wealth. Part E shows that the demands survive a scaling of every price by 3 when wealth is re-valued at the new prices. With wealth held fixed, the demand would fall by a factor of 3, so the flag depends on that re-valuation. Part F prints the rejection message. The lab verifies these identities for one economy. It does not show that the equilibrium is unique.
5Where the canon breaks
Two agents. Propositions 2.5 and 2.9 are stated for two agents. Research problem R1 extends Proposition 2.9 to any number of agents with the same proof.
Existence is assumed. A5 is a hypothesis. For a general economy with A3 and A4, this lesson does not show that an equilibrium exists, and it names A5 wherever it is needed.
The logarithm is one utility. Proposition 2.8 and Proposition 2.9 use \(\ln\). For other utilities in A4 the formula \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\) need not give the demand: the utility \(-1/x\) on \((0, \infty)\) satisfies A4. Its first-order condition \(\pi_s / x_s^2 = \mu\, q_s\) differs from the one behind Proposition 2.8, so the formulas of this section are not, in general, its demand.
A constant share of the aggregate is a property of the logarithmic economy of Proposition 2.9. Proposition 2.9 gives \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\), so each agent receives a fixed share \(\theta_i\) of the aggregate in every state. That follows from the proof of Proposition 2.9, which uses the logarithmic demand. The lesson does not claim it for other utilities.
6Misconceptions
“The price of a state is its probability.”
Proposition 2.9 gives \(q^*_s = \pi_s / \bar e_s\), so the price also depends on how much of the good the economy holds in that state. In Example 2.11, state 2 has probability \(\tfrac14\), the same as state 3, but its price is \(\tfrac1{32}\) against \(\tfrac1{16}\), because \(\bar e_2 = 8\) is larger than \(\bar e_3 = 4\). Lab 2 Part B prints the three prices.
“Walras' law says that the unweighted excess demands add to zero.”
Proposition 2.5 gives \(q \cdot z = 0\), a weighted sum with the prices as weights. The vector \(v = (1, -1, 0)\) has unweighted sum zero, but at the prices of Example 2.11 its value is \(\tfrac18 - \tfrac1{32} = \tfrac{3}{32} \ne 0\). So no excess demand of that economy at those prices equals \(v\).
“Any bundle the agent can afford is a demand.”
The endowment \(e^1 = (3, 4, 1)\) costs exactly \(w_1 = \tfrac{9}{16}\) at \(q^*\), so it lies on the budget boundary. It is not the demand, which Lab 2 Part C prints as \((\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\). A demand is the best affordable bundle (Definition 2.2), and affordability is only the constraint.

Mechanics
            M1 · A logarithmic demand at given prices
Let \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(q = (\tfrac14, \tfrac18)\) and wealth \(w = \tfrac12\). Find the demand of an agent with logarithmic utility, and check that it spends its wealth.
            Solution
By Proposition 2.8, \(x_1 = \pi_1 w / q_1 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 / \tfrac14 = 1\) and \(x_2 = \pi_2 w / q_2 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 / \tfrac18 = 2\). The spending is \(q \cdot x = \tfrac14 \cdot 1 + \tfrac18 \cdot 2 = \tfrac12 = w\), so the budget binds, as Proposition 2.5 requires. The state probabilities are the spending shares, since \(q_s x_s = \pi_s w\) in each state.
            M2 · Demand with three states
Let \(\pi = (\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac12)\), \(q = (1, 2, 1)\) and \(w = 3\). Compute the logarithmic demand and check the budget.
            Solution
The formula \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\) gives \(x = (\tfrac14 \cdot 3 / 1, \tfrac14 \cdot 3 / 2, \tfrac12 \cdot 3 / 1) = (\tfrac34, \tfrac38, \tfrac32)\). The spending is \(\tfrac34 + 2 \cdot \tfrac38 + \tfrac32 = \tfrac34 + \tfrac34 + \tfrac32 = 3\), which equals \(w\). The spending in each state is \(\pi_s w\), that is \(\tfrac34\), \(\tfrac34\) and \(\tfrac32\).
            M3 · A two-state equilibrium by hand
Let \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(e^1 = (1, 3)\) and \(e^2 = (3, 1)\). Find \(\bar e\), the equilibrium prices of Proposition 2.9, the wealth shares, and the equilibrium bundles, and check market clearing.
            Solution
The aggregate is \(\bar e = (4, 4)\). The prices are \(q^* = (\tfrac18, \tfrac18)\), and \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\). The share of agent 1 is \(\theta_1 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac14 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac34 = \tfrac12\), and \(\theta_2 = \tfrac12\). So \(x^{1*} = \tfrac12 \bar e = (2, 2)\) and \(x^{2*} = (2, 2)\). Their sum is \((4, 4) = \bar e\), so markets clear.
Proof
            P1 · The last market with three states
Let \(S = 3\), and suppose \(z_1(q) = z_2(q) = 0\) at some \(q \in (0, \infty)^{3}\), with demands as in Proposition 2.5. Show that \(z_3(q) = 0\).
            Solution
This is Corollary 2.7 with \(s = 3\). By Proposition 2.5, \(q_1 z_1 + q_2 z_2 + q_3 z_3 = 0\). The first two terms vanish, so \(q_3 z_3 = 0\). Since \(q_3 > 0\), \(z_3 = 0\). The argument uses the strict positivity of the price in the last state, and Proposition 2.5, which holds under A1, A3 and A4.
            P2 · Scaling prices and wealth together
In the economy of Proposition 2.9, let \(\psi > 0\). Show that \((\psi q^*, \psi w)\) gives the same demands, so that \(\psi q^*\) with wealth \(\psi \theta_i\) is an equilibrium with the same bundles.
            Solution
At prices \(\psi q^*\) and wealth \(\psi \theta_i\), Proposition 2.8 gives \(x_s = \pi_s \psi \theta_i / (\psi q^*_s) = \pi_s \theta_i / q^*_s\), which is the earlier demand. The bundles are unchanged, so market clearing is unchanged too. The scaled price vector is therefore also an equilibrium price vector for the same bundles, and the normalization \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\) is one choice among these.
            P3 · Shares of the aggregate are state-independent
In the economy of Proposition 2.9, show that \(x^{i*}_s / \bar e_s = \theta_i\) for every state \(s\), and explain what this says about how the aggregate is divided among the agents in each state.
            Solution
By Proposition 2.9, \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\), so the ratio is \(\theta_i\) in every state. Agent \(i\) therefore consumes the same fraction of the aggregate in each state. In this economy the fraction does not depend on the state, so the agent's consumption moves in proportion to the aggregate. The statement is about this logarithmic economy, and the lesson does not claim it for other utilities.
Empirical
            E1 · The normalization in Lab 2
From Part B of Lab 2, what are the three prices, and what does the line "prices_dot_aggregate 1" check?
            Solution
The prices printed are \((\tfrac18, \tfrac1{32}, \tfrac1{16})\). The line checks that \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\). Since \(\bar e = (4, 8, 4)\), the sum is \(\tfrac48 + \tfrac{8}{32} + \tfrac4{16} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = 1\), which is the normalization of Proposition 2.9.
            E2 · Residuals in Part C of Lab 2
Part C prints a budget residual for each agent, three market-clearing residuals and a Walras residual. Which equation does each residual test, and what are the printed values?
            Solution
Each agent's budget residual tests \(q^* \cdot x^i - w_i = 0\). The three market-clearing residuals test \(x^{1}_s + x^{2}_s - \bar e_s = 0\) for each state. The Walras residual tests \(q^* \cdot z = 0\) (Proposition 2.5). All printed values are 0, so each equation holds exactly for these bundles.
            E3 · Multipliers and the scale check in Lab 2
Part D prints the ratio \(\pi_s / (x_s q_s)\) for each agent, and Part E prints whether the demands are unchanged when every price is multiplied by 3 with wealth re-valued at the new prices. What values does Part D print, and what does Part E show?
            Solution
Part D prints \(\tfrac{16}{9}\) in each state for agent 1 and \(\tfrac{16}{7}\) for agent 2. These equal \(1/w_1\) and \(1/w_2\), as the first-order condition predicts, and the flag reads True for both. Part E prints "demand_unchanged True" for both agents. The logarithmic demand formula depends on prices only through \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\), so a common rescaling of prices and wealth leaves the bundles unchanged (Remark 2.10).
Research
            R1 · Any number of agents
Let there be \(I\) agents with logarithmic utility, endowments \(e^i\) with \(e^i_s > 0\), and \(\bar e = \sum_i e^i\). Define \(\theta_i\) and \(q^*\) as in Proposition 2.9. Show that the same formulas give an equilibrium.
            Solution
Summing \(\theta_i = \sum_s \pi_s e^i_s / \bar e_s\) over \(i\) gives \(\sum_i \theta_i = \sum_s \pi_s \sum_i e^i_s / \bar e_s = \sum_s \pi_s = 1\). Also \(q^* \cdot \bar e = 1\) and each wealth is \(w_i = \theta_i > 0\). Proposition 2.8 gives the demands \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\), and \(\sum_i x^{i*}_s = \bar e_s\) for every state. So the proof of Proposition 2.9 uses no property specific to two agents.
            R2 · Own-price effect of a logarithmic demand
For fixed wealth \(w\), find \(\partial x_s / \partial q_s\) for the demand of Proposition 2.8. What happens to the demand of state 1 when \(q_1\) doubles, with wealth fixed?
            Solution
From \(x_s = \pi_s w / q_s\), \(\partial x_s / \partial q_s = -\pi_s w / q_s^2 = -x_s / q_s\). So the elasticity of demand with respect to the own price is \(-1\). Doubling \(q_1\) at fixed \(w\) gives \(x_1 \mapsto \tfrac12 x_1\), while \(x_t\) for \(t \ne 1\) does not change, because \(x_t = \pi_t w / q_t\) does not depend on \(q_1\).
            R3 · Where A3 is used
Identify the exact places in the proofs of Propositions 2.8 and 2.9 that use A3, and say which of them require every entry of \(\bar e\) to be strictly positive and which require \(w_i > 0\).
            Solution
Proposition 2.9 divides by \(\bar e_s\) in \(q^*_s = \pi_s / \bar e_s\) and in \(\theta_i\), so it needs \(\bar e_s > 0\) for every state. It divides by \(q^*_s\) when it computes \(x^{i*}_s\), which needs \(q^*_s > 0\), and the wealth \(w_i = \theta_i\) must be positive for Proposition 2.8. Beyond A1, Proposition 2.8 needs only \(w > 0\) and \(q \in (0,\infty)^S\). An agent with a zero endowment in one state can still have positive wealth, so A3 as a componentwise condition on each agent is stronger than the proof needs.
