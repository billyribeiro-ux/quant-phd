0014| Xrefuted
0015| An equilibrium is a claim about prices, and it is also a claim about outcomes. The efficiency result proved below (Proposition 3.4) says that, in the economy of Lesson L02, no feasible reallocation (Definition 3.1) can make some agent better off without making any agent worse off, at a competitive equilibrium. It states its assumptions and proves both parts. Part (a) is that statement, in the terms of Definition 3.2. Part (b) says that, at an interior equilibrium (one whose bundles have every entry strictly positive), the rate at which an agent trades one state's good for another's at the same utility (Definition 3.3) equals the price ratio for both agents. A grid search is a check, not a proof.
0016| 1Feasibility, Pareto improvements and marginal rates
0017| The economy is the one of Lesson L02: \(S\) states, probabilities \(\pi_s\), two agents with endowments \(e^1, e^2\), aggregate \(\bar e = e^1 + e^2\), and utilities \(U_i\). The assumptions used in this lesson are A1, A3 and A4, which are stated in Lesson L02 and keep their numbers here. The symbol \(e^i\) with an agent superscript is an endowment, as in Lesson L02. The security \(e_s\) of Definition 1.3 is used only in the proof of part (b) of Proposition 3.4, where it is named.
0018| Definition 3.1 (feasible allocation)
0019| A feasible allocation is a pair \((y^1, y^2)\) of bundles \(y^i \in \R^{S}_{+}\) with \(y^1 + y^2 = \bar e\). The condition \(y^1 + y^2 = \bar e\) says that the whole aggregate is distributed, with no good destroyed. Research problem R1 treats the case with free disposal.
0020| Definition 3.2 (Pareto improvement)
0021| Let \((x^1, x^2)\) be a feasible allocation. A feasible allocation \((y^1, y^2)\) is a Pareto improvement of it when \(U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^i)\) for both agents, with strict inequality for at least one. An allocation with no Pareto improvement is Pareto efficient, the notion of E1 applied to this economy.
0022| Definition 3.3 (marginal rate of substitution between states)
0023| Let \(u_i\) be differentiable as in A4, and let \(x\) be a strictly positive bundle (\(x_r > 0\) for every state \(r\)). A bundle with a zero entry has \(U_i = -\infty\) under A4, so the partial derivatives below are defined only at strictly positive bundles. The derivative \(u_i'\) is positive on \((0, \infty)\), as shown in the proof of Proposition 3.4 (b), so the ratio is well defined. The marginal rate of substitution of agent \(i\) between states \(s\) and \(t\) at \(x\) is \[ \mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x) = \frac{\partial U_i / \partial x_s}{\partial U_i / \partial x_t} = \frac{\pi_s\, u_i'(x_s)}{\pi_t\, u_i'(x_t)} . \] It is the number of units of the good in state \(t\) that agent \(i\) would give up for one more unit in state \(s\), at the same utility.
0024| 2The first welfare theorem
0025| The statement below is the first welfare theorem for the economy of Definition 2.3. Its name is conventional. The result is a proposition that this lesson proves.
0026| Proposition 3.4 (first welfare theorem)
0027| Assume A1, A3 and A4, and let \((q^*, (x^{1*}, x^{2*}))\) be an Arrow–Debreu equilibrium in the sense of Definition 2.3, with prices \(q^* \in (0, \infty)^{S}\). Then: (a) no feasible allocation is a Pareto improvement of \((x^{1*}, x^{2*})\); and (b) for every agent \(i\) and every pair of states \(s\) and \(t\), \[ \mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x^{i*}) = \frac{q^*_s}{q^*_t}. \] In particular, the two agents have the same marginal rate of substitution between any two states.
0028| Proof of (a)
0029| Write \(w_i = q^* \cdot e^i\), so that \(w_1 + w_2 = q^* \cdot \bar e\). Here \(w_i > 0\), because \(q^*\) is strictly positive and \(e^i\) is strictly positive by A3. Lemma 2.4 therefore gives that every entry of \(x^{i*}\) is strictly positive, so \(U_i(x^{i*})\) is finite, and \(q^* \cdot x^{i*} = w_i\) by Proposition 2.5. Suppose \((y^1, y^2)\) is a Pareto improvement.
0030| Step 1. If \(U_i(y^i) > U_i(x^{i*})\), then \(q^* \cdot y^i > w_i\). Otherwise \(y^i\) lies in the budget set \(B_i(q^*)\) and has a strictly higher utility than the demand \(x^{i*}\), which is impossible.
0031| Step 2. If \(U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^{i*})\), then \(q^* \cdot y^i \ge w_i\). Suppose instead \(q^* \cdot y^i < w_i\). Since \(U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^{i*}) > -\infty\), the bundle \(y^i\) has positive entries. Here \(\iota = (1, \ldots, 1)\) is the vector of ones. Choose \(\delta > 0\) with \(q^* \cdot (y^i + \delta \iota) \le w_i\). Then \(y^i + \delta \iota \in B_i(q^*)\), and \(U_i(y^i + \delta \iota) > U_i(y^i) \ge U_i(x^{i*})\) by A4 and A1, which contradicts the demand property of \(x^{i*}\).
0032| Step 3. Since the allocation is a Pareto improvement, some agent \(k\) satisfies \(U_k(y^k) > U_k(x^{k*})\), so \(q^* \cdot y^k > w_k\) by Step 1. The other agent \(j\) satisfies \(q^* \cdot y^j \ge w_j\) by Step 2. Adding, \[ q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) > w_1 + w_2 = q^* \cdot \bar e . \] But \(y^1 + y^2 = \bar e\) by feasibility, so the left side equals \(q^* \cdot \bar e\), which is a contradiction.
0033| Proof of (b)
0034| Fix an agent \(i\), two states \(s\) and \(t\), and write \(x = x^{i*}\), which is strictly positive by Lemma 2.4 (its wealth is positive, as in the proof of (a)). Define the bundle change \(d = e_s / q^*_s - e_t / q^*_t\), where \(e_s\) is the Arrow–Debreu security of Definition 1.3. Then \(q^* \cdot d = 1 - 1 = 0\), so \(x + \varepsilon d\) costs the same as \(x\) for every \(\varepsilon\). For \(|\varepsilon|\) small enough, \(x + \varepsilon d\) is strictly positive, so it lies in the budget set. Since \(u_i\) is differentiable, \(\varepsilon \mapsto U_i(x + \varepsilon d)\) has derivative at 0 equal to \[ G = \frac{\pi_s u_i'(x_s)}{q^*_s} - \frac{\pi_t u_i'(x_t)}{q^*_t} . \] If \(G > 0\), then \(U_i(x + \varepsilon d) > U_i(x)\) for small \(\varepsilon > 0\), and the bundle is affordable, which contradicts the demand property. If \(G < 0\), the same holds for small \(\varepsilon < 0\). So \(G = 0\). The derivative \(u_i'(x_t)\) is positive. Indeed, \(u_i'\) is nonnegative because \(u_i\) is strictly increasing, and nonincreasing because \(u_i\) is concave. If \(u_i'(x_t) = 0\), then \(0 \le u_i'(y) \le 0\) for every \(y > x_t\), so \(u_i\) is constant on \((x_t, \infty)\), which contradicts strict monotonicity. Hence \(\pi_t u_i'(x_t) / q^*_t > 0\), and dividing \(G = 0\) by it gives \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x) = q^*_s / q^*_t\), which is (b). The two agents share the price ratio, so their marginal rates agree.
0035| Remark 3.5 (what each part uses)
0036| Part (a) uses A4's strict monotonicity, A1 and A3 through Lemma 2.4, and the positivity of the prices. It uses no derivative. Part (b) uses the differentiability in A4, the strict positivity of the equilibrium bundle, which Lemma 2.4 supplies, and the strict monotonicity and concavity of A4, which show that \(u_i'\) is positive (proof of (b)). A boundary allocation with a zero entry is outside (b), and the lesson does not assert the MRS condition there.
0037| Example 3.6 (the equilibrium of Lesson L02)
0038| Take the economy of Example 2.11, with \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)\), prices \(q^* = (\tfrac18, \tfrac1{32}, \tfrac1{16})\) and bundles \(x^{1*} = (\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) and \(x^{2*} = (\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\). With \(u_i = \ln\), Definition 3.3 gives \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x) = (\pi_s / x_s) / (\pi_t / x_t)\). States are numbered 1, 2 and 3, as in Example 2.11. For agent 1, \(\mathrm{MRS}_{12} = 4\), \(\mathrm{MRS}_{13} = 2\) and \(\mathrm{MRS}_{23} = \tfrac12\). For agent 2 the same three numbers hold, and they equal \(q^*_1 / q^*_2 = 4\), \(q^*_1 / q^*_3 = 2\) and \(q^*_2 / q^*_3 = \tfrac12\). Lab 3 Part A checks these equalities in exact arithmetic.

0044| 3Where the canon breaks
0045| Efficiency is not fairness. Definition 3.2 counts an allocation as better only when no agent is worse off. It does not rank an allocation that helps one agent and hurts another. Misconception 1 gives the consequence.
0046| The equilibrium is taken as given. Proposition 3.4 assumes an equilibrium. Existence is Assumption A5 of Lesson L02, which this lesson does not prove.
0047| Complete Arrow–Debreu markets. The theorem is about the economy of Definition 2.3, in which a good is traded in every state. It is not a statement about markets in which some claims cannot be traded. Lesson L04 studies that case.
0048| Complete preferences. Proposition 3.4 compares bundles only through the utilities \(U_i\) of A4, which take each agent's ranking of bundles to be complete and represented by \(U_i\). An incomplete ranking, or one that no function of the form in A4 represents, is outside the theorem.
0049| No externalities and no frictions. Each \(U_i\) depends only on agent \(i\)'s own bundle, and the budget set has no transaction costs. An externality, in the sense of E1, is outside the theorem.
0050| Interior equilibria only for (b). Part (b) needs differentiability and a strictly positive bundle. It says nothing about a boundary allocation with zero consumption in some state.
0051| 4Misconceptions
0052| “A competitive equilibrium is the best allocation for everyone.”
0053| Definition 3.2 says that a Pareto improvement needs no agent to be worse off. So Proposition 3.4 (a) rules out a Pareto improvement, which leaves every agent at least as well off and one agent strictly better off. It says nothing about an allocation that makes one agent better off and another worse off. Lab 3 Part B counts the grid allocations that satisfy Definition 3.2.
0054| “Equal marginal rates of substitution mean equal consumption.”
0055| Proposition 3.4 (b) says the rates are equal, and it says nothing about the bundles. In Example 3.6 both agents have the marginal rate 4 between states 1 and 2, but their bundles differ, \((\tfrac94, \tfrac92, \tfrac94)\) and \((\tfrac74, \tfrac72, \tfrac74)\), as Lab 2 Part C prints. Equal rates at different bundles are consistent, because the rate depends on the bundle.
0056| “Efficiency is a property of allocations, so the theorem holds in any market.”
0057| The theorem is about the economy of Definition 2.3, with a price for each state's good and with the assumptions of Lesson L02. The proof uses the budget sets that those prices define (Step 1 and Step 2 of Proposition 3.4 (a)). Without that market structure the proof has no budget set to work with, so the theorem does not apply.

0060| Mechanics
0061| M1 · A marginal rate between two states
0062| Let \(\pi = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23)\) and \(x = (1, 4)\). Compute \(\mathrm{MRS}_{12}(x)\) for an agent with logarithmic utility.
0063| Solution
0064| The marginal rate is \(\mathrm{MRS}_{12} = (\pi_1 / x_1) / (\pi_2 / x_2) = (\tfrac13 / 1) / (\tfrac23 / 4) = \tfrac13 / \tfrac16 = 2\). So the agent would give up 2 units of the good in state 2 for one more unit in state 1, at the same utility. If \(q_1 / q_2 = 2\) are the equilibrium prices and this bundle is the agent's equilibrium bundle, Proposition 3.4 (b) says that the rate equals the price ratio, as here.
0065| M2 · A demand at prices with ratio 2
0066| Let \(\pi = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\), \(q = (2, 1)\) and wealth \(w = 6\). Find the logarithmic demand, and check that its marginal rate equals the price ratio.
0067| Solution
0068| By Proposition 2.8, \(x = (\tfrac12 \cdot 6 / 2, \tfrac12 \cdot 6 / 1) = (\tfrac32, 3)\). The spending is \(2 \cdot \tfrac32 + 3 = 6 = w\). The marginal rate is \((\tfrac12 / \tfrac32) / (\tfrac12 / 3) = \tfrac13 / \tfrac16 = 2\), which equals \(q_1 / q_2 = 2\). The proof of Proposition 3.4 (b) derives this equality for a strictly positive demand at prices \(q\). It uses the demand property, the budget, and the differentiability of A4, together with \(u_i' > 0\) from strict monotonicity and concavity. Proposition 3.4 itself is stated for equilibrium bundles, and this problem is one agent's demand at given prices.
0069| M3 · Feasibility of a split
0070| Let \(\bar e = (2, 2)\). Check whether \(y^1 = (1, 1)\) and \(y^2 = (1, 1)\) form a feasible allocation in the sense of Definition 3.1.
0071| Solution
0072| Both bundles are nonnegative, and \(y^1 + y^2 = (2, 2) = \bar e\). Definition 3.1 requires exactly this, so the split is feasible. It gives each agent half of the aggregate in each state, which is an equal split that is not necessarily an equilibrium allocation.

0074| P1 · The case in which every agent gains
0075| Under the assumptions of Proposition 3.4, show directly that no feasible allocation makes every agent strictly better off than the equilibrium.
0076| Solution
0077| Let \((y^1, y^2)\) be feasible with \(U_i(y^i) > U_i(x^{i*})\) for both agents. By Step 1 of the proof of Proposition 3.4 (a), \(q^* \cdot y^i > w_i\) for each \(i\). Adding gives \(q^* \cdot \bar e = q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) > w_1 + w_2 = q^* \cdot \bar e\), which is impossible. So no such allocation is feasible.
0078| P2 · The marginal rates of the two-agent example
0079| In the economy of Proposition 2.9, show that \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st}(x^{i*}) = q^*_s / q^*_t\) for both agents and all states \(s\), \(t\), using the formulas of Proposition 2.9 and the derivative of the logarithm, \(u_i'(x) = 1/x\).
0080| Solution
0081| Here \(x^{i*}_s = \theta_i \bar e_s\), and \(u_i'(x) = 1/x\). So \(\mathrm{MRS}^i_{st} = (\pi_s / (\theta_i \bar e_s)) / (\pi_t / (\theta_i \bar e_t)) = (\pi_s \bar e_t) / (\pi_t \bar e_s)\). The share \(\theta_i\) cancels. Also \(q^*_s / q^*_t = (\pi_s / \bar e_s) / (\pi_t / \bar e_t) = (\pi_s \bar e_t) / (\pi_t \bar e_s)\). The two expressions are equal for both agents, which is (b) for this economy.
0082| P3 · Which hypotheses each part uses
0083| List the hypotheses that part (a) of Proposition 3.4 uses, and the hypotheses that part (b) uses in addition. Say which part would still hold if utilities were not differentiable.
0084| Solution
0085| Part (a) uses A1 and A3 through Lemma 2.4 (which gives positive optimal bundles), the strict monotonicity in A4, the positivity of \(q^*\), and feasibility. Part (b) uses in addition the differentiability in A4, the strict positivity of \(x^{i*}\), and \(u_i' > 0\), which follows from the strict monotonicity and the concavity in A4. Part (a) would still hold without differentiability, because its proof compares utilities and budgets and takes no derivative. Part (b) would not be established by this argument.

0091| E2 · What the endowment improvements show
0092| Part B of Lab 3 prints 2 Pareto improvements of the endowment and gives the first one. What does this count show about the endowment, and what is the witness?
0093| Solution
0094| The count of 2 shows that the grid search finds allocations that make both agents at least as well off and one strictly better off, relative to the endowment. The conclusion that the endowment is not Pareto efficient rests on the hand witness of Problem R2, not on the count. The first witness gives agent 1 the bundle \((2, 4, 3)\) and agent 2 the bundle \((2, 4, 1)\), which is feasible because the two bundles add to \((4, 8, 4)\). Relative to the endowment, each agent's expected utility rises by \(\tfrac14 \ln\tfrac43 > 0\) at this witness, which is checked by hand (grade T). The count of 2 is a finite grid enumeration (grade C), and it does not show the full set of improvements.
0095| E3 · The zero count at the equilibrium
0096| Part B of Lab 3 prints 0 Pareto improvements of the equilibrium among the 125 grid allocations. Why does this count not prove efficiency, and which result does prove it?
0097| Solution
0098| The equilibrium allocation is not on the grid, and the grid has only 125 feasible points out of the continuum of feasible allocations. A zero count is therefore a finite enumeration, grade C with that basis. The efficiency claim is proved by Proposition 3.4 (a), which covers every feasible allocation.

0100| R1 · Free disposal
0101| Replace the feasibility condition of Definition 3.1 by \(y^1 + y^2 \le \bar e\). Show that Proposition 3.4 (a) still holds, and identify the step that uses the strict positivity of every price \(q^*_s\).
0102| Solution
0103| Steps 1 and 2 of the proof give \(q^* \cdot y^i \ge w_i\) for each agent, with a strict inequality for the improved agent. Adding, \(q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) > q^* \cdot \bar e\). Now \(\bar e - (y^1 + y^2) \ge 0\), and \(q^* \cdot (\bar e - (y^1 + y^2)) \ge 0\) because every price is nonnegative. This gives \(q^* \cdot (y^1 + y^2) \le q^* \cdot \bar e\), which contradicts the strict inequality. So the last step needs only \(q^*_s \ge 0\). The strict positivity of the prices enters earlier, through Lemma 2.4, which makes each equilibrium utility finite.
0104| R2 · A proof that the endowment is not efficient
0105| In the economy of Example 2.11, show that the endowment \((e^1, e^2)\) is not Pareto efficient, by exhibiting a feasible allocation that is a Pareto improvement for small \(\varepsilon > 0\).
0106| Solution
0107| Let agent 1 give \(\varepsilon\) units of state 1 and receive \(4\varepsilon\) units of state 2, and agent 2 do the reverse. The new bundles are \(y^1 = (3 - \varepsilon, 4 + 4\varepsilon, 1)\) and \(y^2 = (1 + \varepsilon, 4 - 4\varepsilon, 3)\), which add to \(\bar e\) and are strictly positive for small \(\varepsilon\). The derivative of \(U_1(y^1)\) at \(\varepsilon = 0\) is \(\tfrac12 \cdot (-\tfrac13) + \tfrac14 \cdot 1 = \tfrac{1}{12} > 0\). The derivative of \(U_2(y^2)\) is \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac14 \cdot (-1) = \tfrac14 > 0\). So both utilities rise for small \(\varepsilon > 0\), and the endowment is not efficient. This is consistent with the marginal rates of the endowment between states 1 and 2, which are \(\tfrac83\) for agent 1 and 8 for agent 2 and differ. Agent 1 needs \(\tfrac83\) units of state 2 to give up one unit of state 1, and the trade gives it 4 units of state 2 for each unit of state 1 it gives up, so agent 1 gains. Agent 2 would give up 8 units of state 2 for one more unit of state 1, and the trade costs it only 4 units of state 2 per unit, so agent 2 gains.

0109| Explain why the proof of part (b) of Proposition 3.4 does not extend to a boundary allocation in which some agent consumes zero in some state, under A4.
0110| Solution
0111| The argument in the proof of (b) moves the bundle in both directions, with \(\varepsilon > 0\) and \(\varepsilon < 0\). At a bundle with a zero entry, a move that lowers that entry leaves the nonnegative orthant. Under the convention of A4 the utility there is \(-\infty\), so the derivative at \(\varepsilon = 0\) does not exist for that move. Lemma 2.4 ensures that equilibrium bundles are strictly positive when wealth is positive, so the argument applies to them.
