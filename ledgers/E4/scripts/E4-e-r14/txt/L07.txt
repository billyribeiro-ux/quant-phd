0015| A price is informative when a trader who sees it knows more about the payoff. That needs a number, and the number must respond to the things that change informativeness (Definition 7.2 below). This lesson uses the squared correlation between the payoff and the price. It shows that the number is the \(R^2\) of the best linear forecast of the payoff from the price (Lemma 7.4, for variables with zero means), that its closed form follows from the equilibrium of Lesson L06, and that, with the other parameters fixed, it falls as the noise supply grows and rises as more investors are informed (Proposition 7.7). Lesson L08 makes the informed fraction a choice, using the same measure.
0016| 1A measure of informativeness
0017| Definition 7.1 (covariance, correlation and noise volume)
0018| For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) with \(\Var(Y) > 0\) and \(\Var(X) > 0\), the correlation is \(\Corr(X, Y) = \Cov(X, Y)/\sqrt{\Var(X)\Var(Y)}\). In the model of Lesson L06, the noise volume is the standard deviation \(\sigma_u\) of the net supply \(u\) of noise traders, so the noise volume is measured in units of the asset.
0019| Definition 7.2 (price informativeness)
0020| The price informativeness is the population squared correlation between the payoff and the price, \[ \rho^2 = \Corr(v, p)^2 = \frac{\Cov(v, p)^2}{\Var(v)\, \Var(p)} . \] It is a number in \([0, 1]\) by Lemma 7.3, and it does not depend on the units of \(p\), by Remark 7.5.
0021| Lemma 7.3 (bounds on correlation)
0022| For random variables \(X\) and \(Y\) with \(\Var(X) > 0\) and \(\Var(Y) > 0\), \(\Cov(X, Y)^2 \leq \Var(X)\Var(Y)\). Hence \(0 \leq \Corr(X, Y)^2 \leq 1\).
0023| Proof
0024| For every real \(t\), the variance \(q(t) = \Var(X + tY) = \Var(X) + 2t\,\Cov(X, Y) + t^2 \Var(Y)\) is nonnegative. This is a quadratic in \(t\) with leading coefficient \(\Var(Y) > 0\). A quadratic that is nonnegative for every real \(t\) has a discriminant that is not positive. The discriminant is \(4\Cov(X, Y)^2 - 4 \Var(X)\Var(Y)\), so \(\Cov(X, Y)^2 \leq \Var(X)\Var(Y)\). Dividing by \(\Var(X)\Var(Y) > 0\) gives the bounds.
0025| Lemma 7.4 (squared correlation is the \(R^2\) of the best linear forecast)
0026| Let \(X\) and \(Y\) have zero means, with \(\Var(X) > 0\) and \(\Var(Y) > 0\). The linear forecast \(bY\) with the smallest mean squared error \(\E(X - bY)^2\) has \(b = \Cov(X, Y)/\Var(Y)\). Its residual variance is \(\Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\), so the proportion of the variance of \(X\) that the forecast explains is \(1 - \Var(X - bY)/\Var(X) = \Corr(X, Y)^2\).
0027| Proof
0028| Since the means are zero, \(\E(X - bY)^2 = \Var(X) - 2b\,\Cov(X, Y) + b^2 \Var(Y)\). This quadratic in \(b\) has leading coefficient \(\Var(Y) > 0\), so it has a unique minimizer, at which the derivative \(-2\Cov(X, Y) + 2b\Var(Y)\) is zero. That gives \(b = \Cov(X, Y)/\Var(Y)\). At this \(b\), the value is \(\Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\). Dividing by \(\Var(X)\) and subtracting from 1 gives \(\Cov(X, Y)^2/(\Var(X)\Var(Y)) = \Corr(X, Y)^2\).
0029| Remark 7.5 (scaling)
0030| For any constant \(\alpha \neq 0\), \(\Corr(X, \alpha Y)^2 = \Corr(X, Y)^2\). The covariance is multiplied by \(\alpha\), and \(\Var(\alpha Y)\) is multiplied by \(\alpha^2\). The factors \(\alpha^2\) cancel in the ratio. So the informativeness of \(p\) and of \(p/\theta_s\) is the same, for any \(\theta_s \neq 0\).
0031| 2Informativeness in the model
0032| Proposition 7.6 (closed form)
0033| Assume A1 to A4 and G of Lesson L06, with \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\) and the equilibrium price \(p = \theta_s^\ast s - \theta_u^\ast u\) of Proposition 6.7 of that lesson. Then \[ \rho^2 = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2} = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \dfrac{a^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^4}{\lambda^2}\,\sigma_u^2} . \] Equivalently, \(\rho^2 = \tau_\eta/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\), where \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\) as defined in Proposition 6.7 of Lesson L06.
0034| Proof
0035| Let \(Y = p/\theta_s^\ast\). Since \(\theta_s^\ast = \bar\tau/(\tau_v + \bar\tau) > 0\), with \(\bar\tau = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta\) as defined in Proposition 6.7 of Lesson L06, the division is valid, and Step 2 of the proof of Proposition 6.7 gives \(Y = v + \eta\), where \(\eta = \varepsilon - \kappa u\) is independent of \(v\) and has variance \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2\). By Remark 7.5, \(\Corr(v, p)^2 = \Corr(v, Y)^2\). Since \(\Cov(v, Y) = \Cov(v, v) + \Cov(v, \eta) = \sigma_v^2\) and \(\Var(Y) = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2\), the squared correlation is \(\sigma_v^4/(\sigma_v^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)) = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)\). Substituting \(\kappa^2 = a^2\sigma_\varepsilon^4/\lambda^2\) gives the explicit form. The equivalent form follows because \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\) and \(\sigma_v^2 = 1/\tau_v\).
0036| Proposition 7.7 (the signs of the responses)
0037| Assume the hypotheses of Proposition 7.6, with \(\sigma_v > 0\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon > 0\), \(a > 0\), \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) and \(\sigma_u > 0\). Hold the other parameters fixed. Then \(\rho^2\) is strictly decreasing in \(\sigma_u\), strictly decreasing in \(a\), strictly decreasing in \(\sigma_\varepsilon\), and strictly increasing in \(\lambda\).
0038| Proof
0039| Write \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/D\) with \(D = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\,\sigma_u^2\) and \(\chi = a^2\sigma_\varepsilon^4/\lambda^2\). Since \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\), \(\rho^2\) is strictly decreasing in \(D\) and strictly increasing in \(1/D\). Each claim follows from the sign of the change in \(D\), because \(D > 0\) throughout. Increasing \(\sigma_u\) raises \(\chi\sigma_u^2\) if \(\chi > 0\), which is true here, so \(D\) rises. Increasing \(a\) raises \(\chi\), since \(\chi\) is proportional to \(a^2\) with \(a > 0\), so \(D\) rises. Increasing \(\lambda\) lowers \(\chi\), since \(\chi\) is proportional to \(\lambda^{-2}\), so \(D\) falls. Increasing \(\sigma_\varepsilon > 0\) raises \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) and \(\sigma_\varepsilon^4\), and both enter \(D\) with nonnegative coefficients, so \(D\) rises. Each conclusion is strict, because the changed term is strictly monotone in its argument.
0040| Remark 7.8 (the full-revelation benchmark)
0041| Let \(\rho_0^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\), which is \(\Corr(v, s)^2\) because \(\Cov(v, s) = \sigma_v^2\) and \(\Var(s) = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2\). Proposition 7.6 gives \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\sigma_u^2)\) with \(\chi > 0\). So \(\rho^2 < \rho_0^2\) for every \(\sigma_u > 0\), and \(\rho^2 \to \rho_0^2\) as \(\sigma_u \to 0\). The benchmark is the informativeness of the signal itself. It is not reached when the supply is noisy. This lesson does not treat \(\sigma_u = 0\) as a case of Proposition 6.7, which assumes \(\sigma_u > 0\).
0042| Remark 7.9 (price variance is a different object)
0043| The variance of the price is \(\Var(p) = \theta_s^{\ast 2}(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2) + \theta_u^{\ast 2}\sigma_u^2\), which includes the supply term. The supply term adds to \(\Var(p)\). Lab 1, Part B, prints both quantities across a sweep of the noise volume, and on that sweep \(\Var(p)\) rises while \(\rho^2\) falls, as Proposition 7.7 says for \(\rho^2\) in \(\sigma_u\).

0049| 3Where the canon breaks
0050| Linear measure. \(\rho^2\) measures linear association. A price can carry information through a nonlinear function of the payoff while \(\rho^2 = 0\). Witness: let \(v \sim \Normal(0, 1)\) and \(p = v^2\). Then \(\Var(p) = \E[v^4] - 1 = 2 > 0\), and \(\Cov(v, p) = \E[v^3] - \E[v]\,\E[v^2] = 0\) because \(\E[v] = \E[v^3] = 0\) by symmetry, so \(\rho^2 = 0\). Yet \(p\) determines \(|v|\). Lemma 7.4 gives the \(R^2\) of the best linear forecast only.
0051| Model dependence. Proposition 7.6 uses A1 to A4 and G of Lesson L06. Under non-normal primitives, the closed form of Proposition 7.6 is not established by this proof, and the formula is not claimed.
0052| Population object. \(\rho^2\) is a population quantity. A sample correlation from finite data differs from it by sampling error, which Lab 1, Part F, shows for one seed.
0053| Fixed informed fraction. Proposition 7.7 holds \(\lambda\) fixed. When the informed fraction is itself chosen, \(\lambda\) is no longer a fixed parameter, and Lesson L08 treats that case.
0054| Units of noise. The noise volume is the standard deviation \(\sigma_u\) in units of the asset (Definition 7.1). The sweeps use that scale. A strictly increasing reparametrisation of the noise volume, such as \(\sigma_u^2\), changes the numbers but not the decreasing sign of Proposition 7.7 in \(\sigma_u\).
0055| 4Misconceptions
0056| “A price with more variance is a more informative price.”
0057| In Lab 1, Part B, the variance of the price rises from \(729/1444\) to \(35937/4802\) across the noise sweep, while \(\rho^2\) falls from \(4/9\) to \(1/66\). Price variance contains the supply term \(\theta_u^2\sigma_u^2\), which carries no payoff information (Remark 7.9).
0058| “Informativeness is the precision of the noise in the price.”
0059| The precision of the price's noise in the uninformed inference is \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\). At the base instance \(\tau_\eta = 1/2\) (Lesson L06, Lab 1, Part A), but \(\rho^2 = 1/3\). The correct formula is \(\rho^2 = \tau_\eta/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\), which also depends on the precision of the prior, \(\tau_v = 1\).
0060| “Noise makes the price uninformative, so any positive noise volume gives zero informativeness.”
0061| Proposition 7.6 gives \(\rho^2 > 0\) for every finite \(\sigma_u\). At the largest noise volume on the sweep, \(\sigma_u = 4\), \(\rho^2 = 1/66\) is small but positive (Lab 1, Part B).
0062| “More informed investors make the price less informative, because their demands add noise.”
0063| The mechanism runs the other way. The price's signal is \(Y = s - \kappa u\) with \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\), so a larger informed fraction lowers \(\kappa\) and the noise that the supply adds to \(Y\). Proposition 7.7 gives a strictly increasing \(\rho^2\) in \(\lambda\), and Lab 1, Part C, shows \(1/6\), \(1/3\) and \(9/22\) for \(\lambda = 1/4, 1/2, 3/4\).

0072| Take \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\sigma_u = \tfrac12\) and \(\lambda = \tfrac34\). Compute \(\rho^2\).
0073| Solution
0074| \(\kappa = 1/(3/4) = 4/3\), so \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + (16/9)(1/4) = 13/9\). Then \(\rho^2 = 1/(1 + 13/9) = 9/22\). This matches the \(\lambda = 3/4\) row of Lab 1, Part C, and it is larger than the value \(1/3\) at \(\lambda = 1/2\).
0075| M3 · Two payoff variances
0076| Take \(\sigma_v^2 = 2\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(\sigma_u = 1\). Compute \(\rho^2\), and state whether a larger payoff variance raises or lowers \(\rho^2\) at these values.
0077| Solution
0078| \(\kappa = 2\) and \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + 4 = 5\), so \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) = 2/7\). Holding the other values fixed, \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 5\) does not depend on \(\sigma_v^2\), so \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + 5)\). Its derivative in \(\sigma_v^2\) is \(5/(\sigma_v^2 + 5)^2 > 0\), so a larger payoff variance raises \(\rho^2\) here.
0079| Proof
0080| P1 · The discriminant bound
0081| Prove Lemma 7.3 without quoting the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. State where you use \(\Var(Y) > 0\), and say what happens if \(\Var(Y) = 0\).
0082| Solution
0083| Expand \(q(t) = \Var(X + tY) = \Var(X) + 2t\Cov(X, Y) + t^2\Var(Y)\), which is nonnegative for every \(t\), because it is a variance. The leading coefficient \(\Var(Y)\) is positive, which makes \(q\) a genuine quadratic that is nonnegative everywhere, so its discriminant must be nonpositive. That gives \(\Cov(X, Y)^2 \leq \Var(X)\Var(Y)\). If \(\Var(Y) = 0\), then \(q\) is linear in \(t\), and nonnegativity forces \(\Cov(X, Y) = 0\), so the inequality still holds, but the proof above does not cover that case.
0084| P2 · The explained proportion is the squared correlation
0085| Under the zero-mean hypotheses of Lemma 7.4, show that the residual variance of the best linear forecast equals \(\Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\), using only the expansion of \(\E(X - bY)^2\).
0086| Solution
0087| With zero means, \(\E(X - bY)^2 = \E X^2 - 2b\E(XY) + b^2\E Y^2 = \Var(X) - 2b\Cov(X, Y) + b^2\Var(Y)\). Substituting \(b = \Cov(X, Y)/\Var(Y)\) gives \(\Var(X) - 2\Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y) + \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y) = \Var(X) - \Cov(X, Y)^2/\Var(Y)\). Every step uses only the zero means and the expansion.
0088| P3 · Scaling leaves the squared correlation unchanged
0089| Prove Remark 7.5 for a constant \(\alpha \neq 0\), and state which property of the covariance and the variance it uses.
0090| Solution
0091| \(\Cov(X, \alpha Y) = \alpha\Cov(X, Y)\) and \(\Var(\alpha Y) = \alpha^2 \Var(Y)\), by bilinearity of covariance and the rule \(\Var(\alpha Y) = \alpha^2\Var(Y)\). Then \(\Corr(X, \alpha Y)^2 = \alpha^2\Cov(X, Y)^2/(\Var(X)\alpha^2\Var(Y))\). The factor \(\alpha^2\) cancels, which needs \(\alpha \neq 0\) so that \(\Var(\alpha Y) > 0\).
0092| Empirical
0093| E1 · Reading the seeded check
0094| Lab 1, Part F, prints a sample squared correlation of 0.330625, against the population value \(1/3\) and a tolerance of 0.02. Compute the difference in units of the tolerance, and state whether the check passes.
0095| Solution
0096| The difference is \(1/3 - 0.330625 = 13/4800 \approx 0.002708\), where \(0.330625\) is the sample value that Lab 1, Part F prints, rounded to six decimals, and \(\approx\) marks rounding to six decimals. In units of the tolerance it is \((13/4800)/0.02 = 13/96 \approx 0.135\), rounded to three decimals. The check passes, because the absolute difference is below the tolerance. The result is one simulated draw, labelled SIMULATED, so it does not measure the sampling distribution of the estimator.
0097| E2 · Recovering a noise volume from a reported \(R^2\)
0098| A hypothetical regression of the payoff on the price reports \(R^2 = 0.25\). The numbers are invented, not data. Assume the parameters \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\) and \(\lambda = \tfrac12\). What noise volume \(\sigma_u\) makes the population \(\rho^2\) equal to 0.25?
0099| Solution
0100| Treat the reported \(R^2\) as the population \(\rho^2\). This is an assumption, and Problem E3 explains why a sample \(R^2\) can differ from \(\rho^2\). Setting \(1/(1 + \sigma_\eta^2) = 1/4\) gives \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 3\). With \(\kappa = 2\), \(1 + 4\sigma_u^2 = 3\), so \(\sigma_u^2 = 1/2\) and \(\sigma_u = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.7071\). This inversion assumes the model is correct, which is what the regression cannot test by itself.
0101| E3 · Why a sample \(R^2\) is not the population measure
0102| Give two reasons why a regression \(R^2\) from a finite sample of prices can differ from \(\rho^2\), and say which of them Lab 1, Part F, can and cannot detect.
0103| Solution
0104| First, a finite sample has sampling error, so the sample \(R^2\) fluctuates around \(\rho^2\). Second, the regression may be misspecified, for example if the true forecast is nonlinear, so the linear \(R^2\) differs from the best forecast's explained share. Lab 1, Part F, detects only the first, and only for data generated by the model. It cannot detect misspecification, because the sample comes from the model itself.
0105| Research
0106| R1 · Informativeness as the CARA coefficient grows
0107| Hold the other parameters fixed and let \(a \to \infty\). Show that \(\rho^2 \to 0\), and identify the step of Proposition 7.7 that carries the limit.
0108| Solution
0109| Since \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\) grows without bound, \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2 \to \infty\) when \(\sigma_u > 0\). Then \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) \to 0\). The step that carries the limit is the increase of \(\chi\) with \(a\), which makes \(D\) grow without bound.
0110| R2 · Half of the benchmark
0111| Find the noise volume \(\sigma_u\), in closed form, at which \(\rho^2\) equals half of the full-revelation benchmark \(\rho_0^2\) of Remark 7.8. Then evaluate it at \(\sigma_v^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\) and \(\lambda = \tfrac12\).
0112| Solution
0113| Setting \(\sigma_v^2/D = \tfrac12\sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\) gives \(D = 2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)\). Since \(D = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\sigma_u^2\), this gives \(\chi\sigma_u^2 = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2\), so \(\sigma_u = \sqrt{(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2)/\chi}\) with \(\chi = a^2\sigma_\varepsilon^4/\lambda^2\). At the stated values \(\chi = 4\) and \(\sigma_u = \sqrt{2/4} = 1/\sqrt{2}\).
0114| R3 · The derivative in the noise volume
0115| Compute \(\partial\rho^2/\partial(\sigma_u^2)\) from Proposition 7.6, and state its sign for every parameter set of Proposition 7.7. Show that it is not a constant multiple of \(\rho^2\).
0116| Solution
0117| With \(\rho^2 = \sigma_v^2/D\) and \(D = \sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \chi\sigma_u^2\), the derivative is \(-\sigma_v^2 \chi/D^2\). Its sign is negative, because \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\), \(\chi > 0\) and \(D > 0\). The ratio of the derivative to \(\rho^2\) is \(-\chi/D\), which depends on \(D\), and \(D\) changes with \(\sigma_u\). So the ratio of the derivative to \(\rho^2\) is not constant in \(\sigma_u\), and the derivative is not a constant multiple of \(\rho^2\).
