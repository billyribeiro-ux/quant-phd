0015| In this model a price can aggregate the information that investors hold. It also absorbs a supply of the asset that carries no payoff information. If that supply is random, the price no longer reveals the signal exactly. This lesson works in a one-period model with some investors who observe more than the price and a random supply of that kind. It derives the linear equilibrium in closed form, shows that investors who observe only the price learn from it through a linear projection, and gives the coefficients that Lesson L07 uses to measure how informative the price is. Section 1 defines the model's terms and assumptions (Definitions 6.1 and 6.2, Assumptions A1 to A4 and G).
0016| 1The model
0017| Definition 6.1 (primitives)
0018| There is one risky asset. Its payoff is a random variable \(v\) with prior variance \(\sigma_v^2\). A signal is \(s = v + \varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\) is the signal noise with variance \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). A noise trader is an investor whose trade is not a function of the payoff information. In this model the noise traders are represented by a random net supply \(u\) of the asset: \(u > 0\) means that noise traders sell \(u\) units to the market, and \(u < 0\) means that they buy. The variance of \(u\) is \(\sigma_u^2\). The precisions are the reciprocals \(\tau_v = 1/\sigma_v^2\) and \(\tau_\varepsilon = 1/\sigma_\varepsilon^2\). The informed fraction is \(\lambda\), and the coefficient of absolute risk aversion is \(a\) (Definition 6.2 in this lesson).
0019| Assumption A1 (normal primitives)
0020| The random variables \(v\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(u\) are jointly normal, mean zero, and mutually independent, with \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 > 0\) and \(\sigma_u^2 > 0\). Hence \((v, s, u)\) is jointly normal. The zero prior mean is a normalization used throughout this lesson, and this lesson does not treat a nonzero mean.
0021| Definition 6.2 (CARA utility)
0022| An investor has constant absolute risk aversion (CARA) utility with coefficient \(a > 0\) if the utility of terminal wealth \(w\) is \(-\exp(-a w)\). Wealth is \(w = w_0 + x(v - p)\), where \(w_0\) is a constant, \(x\) is the risky holding and \(p\) is the price. The investor has no other random wealth.
0023| Assumption A2 (information)
0024| A fraction \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\) of the investors is informed. An informed investor observes \(s\) and the price \(p\). An uninformed investor observes only \(p\). The fraction \(\lambda\) is given. It is not chosen by any investor in this lesson, and Lesson L08 endogenizes it.
0025| Assumption A3 (price taking and linear rule)
0026| Every investor takes the price \(p\) as given. A price is a function \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) of the signal and the supply, for real coefficients \(\theta_s\) and \(\theta_u\).
0027| Assumption A4 (market clearing at every pair \((s, u)\))
0028| For every pair \((s, u) \in \R^2\), with \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\), the informed demand \(x_I\) and the uninformed demand \(x_U\) (the CARA demands of Remark 6.5 in §2) satisfy \[ \lambda\, x_I + (1 - \lambda)\, x_U = u . \] The residual at \((s, u)\) is the left side minus \(u\). Clearing is required at each pair \((s, u)\). Step 3 of the proof of Proposition 6.7 in §3 evaluates the residual at three of these pairs. With the price rule \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) substituted (\(\theta_s\) and \(\theta_u\) fixed), the residual is a linear function of \((s, u)\) with no constant term. A linear function that is not identically zero vanishes only on a line, and a line has probability zero because \((s, u)\) has a nondegenerate normal law under A1. So clearing with probability one forces the residual to vanish at every pair, and the coefficients are those of Proposition 6.7.
0029| Assumption G (two facts about the normal law)
0030| (i) A linear image of a jointly normal random vector is jointly normal. (ii) If the components of a jointly normal random vector are pairwise uncorrelated, they are mutually independent. Both facts are used here without proof. The moment generating function of a normal variable, which Lemma 6.2 (in §2) needs, is proved in that lemma.
0031| 2Beliefs and demands
0032| Given an investor's information, the conditional distribution of \(v\) is the investor's posterior. Its mean is the posterior mean, and its variance is the posterior variance used below.
0033| Lemma 6.2 (CARA demand under normal beliefs)
0034| Assume A3 (price taking) for an investor who takes \(p\) as given. Suppose that, conditional on the investor's information, \(v\) is normal with mean \(m\) and variance \(V > 0\). Then the investor's optimal holding is the unique maximizer \[ x^\ast = \frac{m - p}{a V}. \]
0035| Proof
0036| Let \(Z\) be normal with mean \(\mu\) and variance \(V\), and let \(t\) be real. Then \(\E e^{tZ} = e^{t\mu + t^2 V / 2}\). To see this, write \[ \E e^{tZ} = \int e^{tz} (2\pi V)^{-1/2} e^{-(z - \mu)^2 / (2V)} \, dz . \] Completing the square gives \[ tz - \frac{(z - \mu)^2}{2V} = -\frac{(z - \mu - tV)^2}{2V} + t\mu + \frac{t^2 V}{2} . \] The integral of the shifted normal density is 1, which gives the formula.
0037| Apply this with \(Z = v - p\), which has mean \(m - p\) and variance \(V\) given the information, and with \(t = -a x\). Expected utility is \[ \E\bigl[-e^{-a w_0 - a x (v - p)}\bigr] = -e^{-a w_0}\, e^{g(x)}, \] with \[ g(x) = -a x (m - p) + \tfrac{a^2 V}{2} x^2 . \] Maximizing expected utility is minimizing \(g\), because \(-e^{-a w_0} < 0\) and \(\exp\) is increasing. The function \(g\) is a quadratic with leading coefficient \(a^2 V / 2 > 0\), so it has a unique minimizer. Setting \(g'(x) = -a(m - p) + a^2 V x\) to zero gives \(x^\ast = (m - p)/(aV)\).
0038| Lemma 6.3 (informed beliefs)
0039| Assuming A1 and G. Let \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) for any real \(\theta_s\) and \(\theta_u\). Then an informed investor, who observes \((s, p)\), has \[ \E[v \mid s, p] = \omega\, s, \qquad \Var(v \mid s, p) = V_I , \] where \[ \omega = \frac{\sigma_v^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2}, \qquad V_I = \frac{\sigma_v^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2} . \] Equivalently, \(1/V_I = \tau_v + \tau_\varepsilon\) and \(\omega/V_I = \tau_\varepsilon\).
0040| Proof
0041| Let \(e = v - \omega s\). Then \(\Cov(e, s) = \sigma_v^2 - \omega(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2) = 0\), by the choice of \(\omega\). Also \(\Cov(e, u) = \Cov(v, u) - \omega \Cov(s, u) = 0\), because \(v\), \(\varepsilon\) and \(u\) are independent by A1. The vector \((e, s, u)\) is jointly normal by A1. Its components are pairwise uncorrelated: \(e\) is uncorrelated with \(s\) and with \(u\) as shown above, and \(\Cov(s, u) = 0\) because \(s = v + \varepsilon\) is independent of \(u\) by A1. By G, \(e\) is independent of \((s, u)\). Since \(p\) is a function of \((s, u)\), \(e\) is independent of \((s, p)\). Therefore \(\E[v \mid s, p] = \omega s + \E[e] = \omega s\), because \(\E e = 0\) by A1. Also \[\Var(v \mid s, p) = \Var(e) = \sigma_v^2 - 2\omega \sigma_v^2 + \omega^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2) = \sigma_v^2(1 - \omega) = V_I\]. The two identities follow from the definitions of \(\omega\) and \(V_I\). Since \(e\) is normal and independent of \((s, p)\), the conditional law of \(v = \omega s + e\) given \((s, p)\) is normal with mean \(\omega s\) and variance \(V_I > 0\), as Lemma 6.2 requires.
0042| Lemma 6.4 (uninformed beliefs are a linear projection)
0043| Assuming A1 and G. Let \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) with \((\theta_s, \theta_u) \neq (0, 0)\), so that \(\Var(p) > 0\). Define \[ b = \frac{\Cov(v, p)}{\Var(p)} , \qquad \text{so that} \quad \Cov(v, p) = \theta_s \sigma_v^2 \quad (\text{because } \Cov(v, u) = 0 \text{ by A1}) . \] Then an uninformed investor, who observes \(p\), has \(\E[v \mid p] = b\, p\) and \(\Var(v \mid p) = \sigma_v^2 - b\, \Cov(v, p) =: V_U\).
0044| Proof
0045| Let \(e = v - bp\). Then \(\Cov(e, p) = \Cov(v, p) - b \Var(p) = 0\). The vector \((e, p)\) is a linear image of \((v, s, u)\), so it is jointly normal by A1, and G makes \(e\) independent of \(p\). Hence \(\E[v \mid p] = bp + \E e = bp\), and \[\Var(v \mid p) = \Var(e) = \sigma_v^2 - 2b\,\Cov(v, p) + b^2 \Var(p) = \sigma_v^2 - b\,\Cov(v, p)\], because \(b^2 \Var(p) = b\,\Cov(v, p)\). Given \(p\), \(v = bp + e\) with \(e\) normal and independent of \(p\), so the conditional law of \(v\) is normal with mean \(bp\) and variance \(V_U\), as Lemma 6.2 requires. Here \(V_U = \sigma_v^2(\theta_s^2 \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \theta_u^2 \sigma_u^2)/\Var(p) > 0\), because \((\theta_s, \theta_u) \neq (0, 0)\) and \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2, \sigma_u^2 > 0\).
0046| Remark 6.5 (the uninformed investor's demand)
0047| Lemmas 6.2 and 6.4 give the uninformed demand \(x_U = (bp - p)/(a V_U)\), with \(V_U = \Var(v \mid p)\). An uninformed investor takes the price as a signal and forms a linear forecast of \(v\) from it. The informed demand is \(x_I = (\omega s - p)/(a V_I)\) by Lemmas 6.2 and 6.3. Both demands are linear in \((s, p)\). That is why the market-clearing condition of A4 becomes a linear condition on the coefficients.
0048| 3The linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium
0049| Definition 6.6 (linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium)
0050| A linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium is a pair of real coefficients \((\theta_s, \theta_u)\) such that the price \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) satisfies A4, where the informed demand is \(x_I = (\omega s - p)/(a V_I)\) (Lemma 6.3) and the uninformed demand is \(x_U = (bp - p)/(a V_U)\), with \(b\) and \(V_U\) given by Lemma 6.4 for this price. For the pair \((0, 0)\) the price is identically zero, so the uninformed belief is the prior, with \(b = 0\) and \(V_U = \sigma_v^2\).
0051| Proposition 6.7 (the coefficients are unique and given in closed form)
0052| Assuming A1 to A4 and G. Define \[ \kappa = \frac{a}{\lambda \tau_\varepsilon} = \frac{a \sigma_\varepsilon^2}{\lambda} , \] \[ \sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2 \sigma_u^2 , \qquad \tau_\eta = \frac{1}{\sigma_\eta^2} , \] \[ \bar\tau = \lambda \tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda) \tau_\eta , \] \[ \theta_s^\ast = \frac{\bar\tau}{\tau_v + \bar\tau} , \qquad \theta_u^\ast = \kappa\, \theta_s^\ast . \] Then (a) \((\theta_s^\ast, \theta_u^\ast)\) is a linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium, and (b) every linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium \((\theta_s, \theta_u)\) equals \((\theta_s^\ast, \theta_u^\ast)\).
0053| Proof of (b), uniqueness
0054| Step 1: \(\theta_s \neq 0\). Suppose \(\theta_s = 0\). Then \(p = -\theta_u u\), so \(\Cov(v, p) = 0\), the uninformed belief is \(\E[v \mid p] = 0\), and their demand is \(x_U = -p/(a\sigma_v^2)\), which does not depend on \(s\). The informed demand is \(x_I = (\tau_\varepsilon s - (\tau_v + \tau_\varepsilon) p)/a\) by Lemma 6.3. Fix \(u = 0\). Then \(p = 0\), so \(x_I = \tau_\varepsilon s/a\) and \(x_U = 0\), and A4 requires \(\lambda \tau_\varepsilon s / a = 0\) for every \(s\). This fails at \(s = 1\), because \(\lambda \tau_\varepsilon / a \neq 0\). So \(\theta_s \neq 0\).
0055| Step 2: the price as a signal. Let \(\kappa' = \theta_u/\theta_s\) and \(Y = p/\theta_s = s - \kappa' u\). Then \(Y - v = \varepsilon - \kappa' u =: \eta\). The noise \(\eta\) is a function of \((\varepsilon, u)\), so it is independent of \(v\) and jointly normal with it, with variance \(\sigma_\eta'^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa'^2 \sigma_u^2\). Write \(\tau_\eta' = 1/\sigma_\eta'^2\) and \(P_U' = \tau_v + \tau_\eta'\). Since \(\sigma(p) = \sigma(Y)\), the argument of Lemma 6.4 applied to the linear combination \(Y\) gives \(\E[v \mid p] = \sigma_v^2 Y/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta'^2) = (\tau_\eta'/P_U') Y\) and \(V_U = 1/P_U'\). Hence \(x_U = (\tau_\eta' Y - P_U' p)/a\).
0056| Step 3: market clearing. With \(P_I = \tau_v + \tau_\varepsilon\), A4 gives \(\lambda(\tau_\varepsilon s - P_I p) + (1 - \lambda)(\tau_\eta' Y - P_U' p) = a u\) for every \((s, u)\). Substitute \(Y = p/\theta_s\) and write \(\bar\tau' = \lambda \tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta'\). Then \[ \lambda \tau_\varepsilon s - Q\, p = a u , \] where \[ Q = \tau_v + \bar\tau' - \frac{(1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta'}{\theta_s} . \] With \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\), this reads \((\lambda \tau_\varepsilon - Q\theta_s)\, s + (Q\theta_u - a)\, u = 0\) for every \((s, u) \in \R^2\). Evaluating at \((0,0)\), \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) gives \(Q\theta_s = \lambda \tau_\varepsilon\) and \(Q\theta_u = a\).
0057| Step 4: solving. Dividing gives \(\kappa' = \theta_u/\theta_s = a/(\lambda\tau_\varepsilon) = \kappa\). So \(\sigma_\eta'^2 = \sigma_\eta^2\), \(\tau_\eta' = \tau_\eta\) and \(\bar\tau' = \bar\tau\). Now \(Q\theta_s = (\tau_v + \bar\tau)\theta_s - (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon\) gives \((\tau_v + \bar\tau)\theta_s = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta = \bar\tau\). Hence \(\theta_s = \theta_s^\ast\), and \(\theta_u = \kappa \theta_s = \theta_u^\ast\).
0058| Proof of (a), existence
0059| Define \(\theta_s^\ast\) and \(\theta_u^\ast\) as in the statement. Since \(\bar\tau > 0\), \(\theta_s^\ast \neq 0\). The price \(p^\ast = \theta_s^\ast s - \theta_u^\ast u\) gives \(Y = p^\ast/\theta_s^\ast = s - \kappa u\), so the noise in \(Y\) is \(\varepsilon - \kappa u\), with variance \(\sigma_\eta^2\). Step 2 of part (b) therefore applies with \(\kappa' = \kappa\). Let \(Q = \tau_v + \bar\tau - (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta/\theta_s^\ast\). The definition of \(\theta_s^\ast\) gives \((\tau_v + \bar\tau)\theta_s^\ast = \bar\tau\), so \(Q\theta_s^\ast = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon\). Also \(Q\theta_u^\ast = Q\kappa\theta_s^\ast = \kappa\lambda\tau_\varepsilon = a\). Then the clearing expression of Step 3 becomes \[\lambda\tau_\varepsilon s - Q(\theta_s^\ast s - \theta_u^\ast u) = (\lambda\tau_\varepsilon - Q\theta_s^\ast)s + Q\theta_u^\ast u = au\] for every \((s, u)\), which is A4.
0060| Corollary 6.8 (the uninformed projection at the equilibrium)
0061| Assuming A1 to A4 and G, and let \((\theta_s^\ast, \theta_u^\ast)\) be the equilibrium of Proposition 6.7. Then \[ \E[v \mid p] = \frac{\tau_\eta}{\theta_s^\ast (\tau_v + \tau_\eta)}\, p , \] \[ \Var(v \mid p) = \frac{1}{\tau_v + \tau_\eta} = \frac{\sigma_v^2 \sigma_\eta^2}{\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2} . \] The slope \(b\) of Lemma 6.4 equals \(\tau_\eta / (\theta_s^\ast (\tau_v + \tau_\eta))\).
0062| Proof
0063| Step 4 of part (b) of Proposition 6.7 gives \(\tau_\eta' = \tau_\eta\), so Step 2 of that proof gives \(\E[v \mid p] = (\tau_\eta/P_U) Y\) and \(\Var(v \mid p) = 1/P_U\), with \(P_U = \tau_v + \tau_\eta\). Substituting \(Y = p/\theta_s^\ast\) gives the first formula. The second equality is the identity of Problem P3 with \(\sigma_\eta^2\) in place of the noise variance. Lemma 6.4 gives \(\E[v \mid p] = b\,p\), and \(\Var(p) > 0\) because \(\theta_s^\ast \ne 0\), so \(p\) is not a constant. Comparing the coefficient of \(p\) with the first formula gives \(b = \tau_\eta/(\theta_s^\ast(\tau_v + \tau_\eta))\).
0064| Example 6.9 (a base instance)
0065| Take \(\sigma_v^2 = 1\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 1\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(\sigma_u^2 = \tfrac14\). Then \(\tau_v = \tau_\varepsilon = 1\), \(\kappa = 2\), \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + 4 \cdot \tfrac14 = 2\), \(\tau_\eta = \tfrac12\), \(\bar\tau = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34\), \(\theta_s^\ast = \tfrac{3/4}{1 + 3/4} = \tfrac37\) and \(\theta_u^\ast = 2 \cdot \tfrac37 = \tfrac67\). So the price is \(p = \tfrac37 s - \tfrac67 u\). The uninformed slope is \(b = \tfrac{\tau_\eta}{\theta_s^\ast(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)} = \tfrac{1/2}{(3/7)(3/2)} = \tfrac79\), and \(V_U = \tfrac23\). At \(s = 1\) and \(u = 0\), the price is \(\tfrac37\), and the demands are \(x_I = \tfrac17\) and \(x_U = -\tfrac17\), which clear the market because \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac17 + \tfrac12 \cdot (-\tfrac17) = 0\). Lab 1 prints \(\kappa\), \(\sigma_\eta^2\), \(\tau_\eta\), \(\bar\tau\), \(\theta_s\), \(\theta_u\), the slope \(7/9\), the posterior variance \(2/3\) and the informed demand \(1/7\) at this instance. It does not print \(\tau_v = \tau_\varepsilon = 1\), the price \(3/7\) at \((s, u) = (1, 0)\), or the uninformed demand \(-1/7\). The clearing check at \((1, 0)\) is one of the 16 grid residuals of Part C.
0066| Remark 6.10 (the price as a noisy signal)
0067| Proposition 6.7 gives \(Y = p/\theta_s^\ast = s - \kappa u\). Uninformed investors therefore observe \(v + \eta\), where \(\eta = \varepsilon - \kappa u\) is noise with variance \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2 \sigma_u^2\). Since \(\sigma_\eta^2 \geq \sigma_\varepsilon^2 > 0\) for every \(\sigma_u^2 \geq 0\), the price reveals the payoff only with noise, and the supply contributes the term \(\kappa^2\sigma_u^2\) of that noise. A larger noise-trader variance \(\sigma_u^2\) raises \(\sigma_\eta^2\) and so lowers \(\tau_\eta\). Lesson L07 turns this into a measure of informativeness.

The closed form gives \(\kappa = 2\), \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 2\), \(\tau_\eta = \tfrac12\), \(\bar\tau = \tfrac34\), \(\theta_s = \tfrac37\) and \(\theta_u = \tfrac67\) (Proposition 6.7). The slope of \(v\) on \(p\) is \(\tfrac79\), the posterior variance of an uninformed investor is \(\tfrac23\), and the residual of A4 is zero at all 16 points of the grid (Part C, with A4 and Proposition 6.7). An alternative price coefficient \(\theta_s = \tfrac12\), with \(\theta_u = \tfrac67\) unchanged, leaves a nonzero residual at \((s, u) = (1, 0)\). Part E is SIMULATED: one seeded draw of 20 000 observations of \((v, \varepsilon, u)\), with seed 6. Each sample slope should lie within three estimated standard errors of its population value. No data are read.

0073| 4Where the canon breaks
0074| Normality. Lemmas 6.3 and 6.4 use G, which applies to jointly normal vectors. With a payoff or a supply that is not normal, the conditional mean of \(v\) given \(p\) need not be linear in \(p\), and the closed form of Proposition 6.7 does not follow from this proof.
0075| Utility. Lemma 6.2 depends on CARA utility. Its proof uses the moment generating function of a normal variable with a constant risk-aversion coefficient. Under other utilities, such as CRRA utility, the demand under normal beliefs need not be the expression of Lemma 6.2, and the linear equilibrium of Proposition 6.7 need not exist.
0076| Exogenous information. The informed fraction \(\lambda\) is an input (A2). Lesson L08 makes it a choice, so the coefficients here are conditional on \(\lambda\).
0077| Independent supply. A1 makes \(u\) independent of \(v\) and \(\varepsilon\). If the supply were correlated with the payoff, the zero covariance in the proof of Lemma 6.3 would fail. Also \(\Cov(v, p) = \theta_s\sigma_v^2 - \theta_u\Cov(v, u)\) would differ from the value \(\theta_s\sigma_v^2\) used in Lemma 6.4, so the uninformed inference would change.
0078| Price taking. A3 says that no investor takes account of the price's response to its own trade. A large informed trader would face a price that depends on its demand, and that case is outside this lesson.
0079| One period and one asset. The model has one risky asset and one trading date. It does not model the dynamics of learning or repeated trade.
0080| 5Misconceptions
0081| “Once the price depends on the signal, the uninformed investors learn the signal exactly.”
0082| The price is \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\), and the uninformed observe \(Y = s - \kappa u = v + \eta\), where \(\eta = \varepsilon - \kappa u\). The variance of \(\eta\) is \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2\), which is at least \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\) (Remark 6.10). At the base instance it is 2 (Lab 1, Part A), so the price is a noisy signal.
0083| “A noise trader must be an irrational investor who trades on mistaken beliefs.”
0084| In Definition 6.1 the noise trader is a random supply \(u\) that is independent of the payoff. A1 and A2 say nothing about its beliefs, and the model contains no beliefs for it. By Definition 6.1 and A2, the informed and uninformed investors are the optimizing agents of the model, each with the CARA utility of Definition 6.2, and the supply \(u\) is a primitive, not a choice.
0085| “The regression slope of \(v\) on \(p\) equals the slope of \(p\) on \(s\).”
0086| The slope of \(p\) on \(s\) is \(\theta_s = 3/7\), and the slope of \(v\) on \(p\) is \(b = 7/9\) at the base instance (Lab 1, Parts A and B). The first describes how the price moves with the signal, and the second describes how the best linear forecast of the payoff moves with the price. They are different objects.
0087| “The price is pure noise to the uninformed, so they learn nothing from it.”
0088| The posterior variance of \(v\) given \(p\) is \(2/3\), which is below the prior variance \(\sigma_v^2 = 1\) (Lab 1, Part B; Corollary 6.8). The price lowers the uninformed investor's uncertainty, but it does not remove all of it.

0092| M1 · Coefficients at a new instance
0093| Take \(\sigma_v^2 = 1\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2 = 1\), \(a = 2\), \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) and \(\sigma_u^2 = \tfrac14\). Compute \(\kappa\), \(\sigma_\eta^2\), \(\tau_\eta\), \(\bar\tau\), \(\theta_s^\ast\) and \(\theta_u^\ast\) from Proposition 6.7.
0094| Solution
0095| \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda = 2 \cdot 1 / \tfrac12 = 4\). Then \(\sigma_\eta^2 = 1 + 16 \cdot \tfrac14 = 5\), so \(\tau_\eta = \tfrac15\). Next \(\bar\tau = \tfrac12 \cdot 1 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac15 = \tfrac35\). Then \(\theta_s^\ast = \tfrac{3/5}{1 + 3/5} = \tfrac38\) and \(\theta_u^\ast = 4 \cdot \tfrac38 = \tfrac32\).
0096| M2 · Projection slope and posterior variance
0097| For the instance of M1, compute the slope \(b\) of \(v\) on \(p\), the posterior variance \(\Var(v \mid p)\), and the squared correlation \(\Corr(v, p)^2\). Compute \(\Var(p)\) from A1 and the price rule of A3.
0098| Solution
0099| \[\Var(p) = (\tfrac38)^2 \cdot 2 + (\tfrac32)^2 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac{9}{32} + \tfrac{9}{16} = \tfrac{27}{32}\], and \(\Cov(v, p) = \tfrac38\). So \(b = (\tfrac38)/(\tfrac{27}{32}) = \tfrac49\). The posterior variance is \(\Var(v \mid p) = 1 - \tfrac49 \cdot \tfrac38 = \tfrac56\), which equals \(1/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta) = 1/(1 + \tfrac15) = \tfrac56\). The squared correlation is \((\tfrac38)^2 / (1 \cdot \tfrac{27}{32}) = \tfrac16\), which equals \(\sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) = 1/6\).
0100| M3 · Market clearing at one point
0101| For the instance of M1, the price is \(p = \theta_s^\ast s - \theta_u^\ast u\). At \((s, u) = (2, 1)\), compute the price, both demands and the value of \(\lambda x_I + (1 - \lambda) x_U\). Check that it equals \(u\).
0102| Solution
0103| The price is \(p = \tfrac38 \cdot 2 - \tfrac32 \cdot 1 = -\tfrac34\). The informed demand is \(x_I = (\tau_\varepsilon s - (\tau_v + \tau_\varepsilon)p)/a = (2 + \tfrac32)/2 = \tfrac74\). For the uninformed investor, \(Y = p/\theta_s^\ast = -2\), so \(x_U = (\tau_\eta Y - (\tau_v + \tau_\eta)p)/a = (-\tfrac25 + \tfrac{9}{10})/2 = \tfrac14\). Then \(\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac74 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac14 = 1 = u\), so the market clears at this point.
0104| Proof
0105| P1 · The informed belief does not depend on the price
0106| Under A1 and G, show that \(\E[v \mid s, p] = \omega s\) for every real \(\theta_s\) and \(\theta_u\), including \(\theta_s = 0\). Identify exactly which step of Lemma 6.3 uses each of A1 and G.
0107| Solution
0108| Lemma 6.3 does not use \(\theta_s\) or \(\theta_u\), because \(e = v - \omega s\) is uncorrelated with \(s\) and with \(u\) whatever the price coefficients are. The independence of \(u\) from \((v, \varepsilon)\), which A1 gives, makes \(\Cov(e, u) = 0\). G turns the zero covariances into independence of \(e\) from \((s, u)\), and \(p\) is a function of \((s, u)\), so \(e\) is independent of \((s, p)\). The mean of \(e\) is zero by A1, so the conditional mean is \(\omega s\).
0109| P2 · No equilibrium has a zero signal coefficient
0110| Show that any linear noisy rational-expectations equilibrium has \(\theta_s \neq 0\), following Step 1 of the proof of Proposition 6.7 in full. State which hypothesis of A4 is used and at which points of \(\R^2\).
0111| Solution
0112| If \(\theta_s = 0\), then \(p = -\theta_u u\) is uncorrelated with \(v\), so the uninformed belief is zero and their demand is \(-p/(a\sigma_v^2)\), which depends only on \(u\). The informed demand still contains \(\tau_\varepsilon s/a\). With \(u = 0\), the clearing condition of A4 at \((s, 0)\) and \((s + 1, 0)\) differ by \(\lambda\tau_\varepsilon/a \neq 0\), but the supply \(u = 0\) is the same at both points, which contradicts A4.
0113| P3 · Variance of the uninformed posterior
0114| Show that \[\sigma_v^2 - \sigma_v^4/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) = \sigma_v^2\sigma_\eta^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)\], and that this equals \(1/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\). State the hypothesis under which each equality holds.
0115| Solution
0116| The first equality is the common-denominator identity \(\sigma_v^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2) - \sigma_v^4 = \sigma_v^2\sigma_\eta^2\). For the second, divide the numerator and denominator by \(\sigma_v^2\sigma_\eta^2\) to get \(1/(\sigma_\eta^{-2} + \sigma_v^{-2}) = 1/(\tau_\eta + \tau_v)\). Both steps need only \(\sigma_v^2 > 0\) and \(\sigma_\eta^2 > 0\), which hold under A1 because \(\sigma_\eta^2 \geq \sigma_\varepsilon^2 > 0\).

0131| R1 · The price as the informed mass vanishes
0132| Hold \(\sigma_v^2\), \(\sigma_\varepsilon^2\), \(a\) and \(\sigma_u^2\) fixed, and let \(\lambda \to 0\). Show that \(\kappa \to \infty\), \(\tau_\eta \to 0\), \(\theta_s^\ast \to 0\) and \(\Corr(v, p)^2 \to 0\). State what the limit means for the price, and say which assumption of the model is outside the range of this limit.
0133| Solution
0134| Since \(\kappa = a\sigma_\varepsilon^2/\lambda\), \(\kappa \to \infty\), so \(\sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2 \to \infty\) and \(\tau_\eta \to 0\). Then \(\bar\tau = \lambda\tau_\varepsilon + (1 - \lambda)\tau_\eta \to 0\), so \(\theta_s^\ast = \bar\tau/(\tau_v + \bar\tau) \to 0\). Since \(\theta_u^\ast = \kappa\theta_s^\ast\), \(\Var(p) = (\theta_s^\ast)^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2) = (\theta_s^\ast)^2(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)\) and \(\Cov(v, p) = \theta_s^\ast\sigma_v^2\), so \(\Corr(v, p)^2 = \sigma_v^2/(\sigma_v^2 + \sigma_\eta^2)\), which tends to \(0\) because \(\sigma_\eta^2 \to \infty\). In the limit the correlation between the payoff and the price tends to zero. The limit is outside A2, which requires \(\lambda \in (0, 1)\), and the proof does not extend to \(\lambda = 0\).
0135| R2 · Uninformed learning and the noise volume
0136| Show that \(\Var(v \mid p)\) is strictly increasing in \(\sigma_u^2\), with the other parameters fixed. Use Corollary 6.8 and state the sign of each step.
0137| Solution
0138| By Corollary 6.8, \(\Var(v \mid p) = 1/(\tau_v + \tau_\eta)\). The map \(\sigma_u^2 \mapsto \sigma_\eta^2 = \sigma_\varepsilon^2 + \kappa^2\sigma_u^2\) is strictly increasing, since \(\kappa > 0\) does not depend on \(\sigma_u\). So \(\tau_\eta = 1/\sigma_\eta^2\) is strictly decreasing, and \(\tau_v + \tau_\eta\) is strictly decreasing. The reciprocal of a strictly decreasing positive function is strictly increasing, which gives the claim.
0139| R3 · Non-normal payoffs
0140| Suppose the payoff \(v\) is not normal, while the signal and the supply are as in A1. Identify the steps of Lemmas 6.3 and 6.4 that use G, and name the conditional facts that Proposition 6.7 needs as explicit hypotheses. Do not claim that the linear formulas hold without those hypotheses.
0141| Solution
0142| Both lemmas use G to pass from zero covariance to independence: Lemma 6.3 for \(e = v - \omega s\) against \((s, u)\), and Lemma 6.4 for \(e = v - bp\) against \(p\). Without joint normality, zero covariance need not give independence, so neither conditional mean need be linear. The proof of Proposition 6.7 needs four facts: \(\E[v \mid s, p] = \omega s\), \(\Var(v \mid s, p) = V_I\), \(\E[v \mid p] = bp\) and \(\Var(v \mid p) = V_U\). The demands also use Lemma 6.2, which needs the conditional law of \(v\) given \((s, p)\) and given \(p\) to be normal, so that is a fifth fact. These can be stated as hypotheses. Under A1 with normal \(v\), all five follow from G. The lesson makes no claim about other payoff laws.

0187| With the price rule \(p = \theta_s s - \theta_u u\) substituted, the clearing residual in A4 is a linear function of \((s, u)\). Why does requiring clearing with probability one give the same coefficients as requiring it at every pair \((s, u)\)?
0188| Options A nonzero linear function of \((s, u)\) vanishes only on a line, which has probability zero under the normal law of \((s, u)\), so clearing with probability one already forces both coefficients of the residual to be zero Because the price must be positive in every state Because the uninformed investors have CARA utility
0189| The residual has no constant term, because the demands and the price are linear in \((s, u)\). A nonzero one vanishes only on a line, and that line has probability zero because \((s, u)\) has a nondegenerate normal law. So a residual that is zero with probability one is zero at every pair. The pointwise form is used in Step 3 of Proposition 6.7, which evaluates the residual at three points.
