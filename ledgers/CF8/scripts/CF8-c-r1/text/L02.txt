      A supplier that lets a customer pay later is lending it money, and the price of that loan is hidden in the terms. An early-payment discount states the price directly, so a firm can compare it with its other borrowing. A credit-term extension asks whether the extra sales repay the cash tied up in receivables. Section 4 defines supply-chain finance, in which a financier pays the supplier early. This lesson computes the implied annual cost of a discount, derives the break-even margin of an extension in closed form, and sets out what supply-chain finance leaves unchanged in the buyer's payable days and what early payment implies as an annual cost to the supplier.
      1Credit terms and the implied cost of a discount
      The terms used here have three numbers. The invoice amount is \(S\). The seller offers a discount \(d\) with \(0
        Definition 1.1 (trade credit and the credit policy)
        Trade credit is the deferral of payment for goods already delivered. A credit policy is the rule a seller applies to that deferral: the credit period \(N\), the discount \(d\) and the early date \(k\).
        The early-payment discount has two payment choices for the buyer. Pay \((1-d)S\) on day \(k\), or pay \(S\) on day \(N\). The difference between them is the subject of Proposition 1.3.
        Convention 1.2 (simple annualization)
        The annual cost of a period of \(N - k\) days is the simple rate that scales the period cost to a 365-day year, with no compounding: the period cost multiplied by \(365/(N-k)\). The lesson reports this figure as the simple annual cost. The compounded figure, used in Section 2, compounds the period cost over the same \(365/(N-k)\) periods: \((1/(1-d))^{365/(N-k)} - 1\).
        Proposition 1.3 (the implied cost of giving up a discount)
        Under Definition 1.1, paying \(S\) on day \(N\) instead of \((1-d)S\) on day \(k\) is the same as borrowing \((1-d)S\) from day \(k\) to day \(N\) at a cost of \(dS\). The cost of the period is \(d/(1-d)\) of the amount borrowed, and its simple annual cost, under Convention 1.2, is
        \[ c(d, k, N) = \frac{d}{1-d}\cdot\frac{365}{N-k}. \]
        Proof
        The buyer who pays on day \(k\) pays \((1-d)S\). The buyer who waits to day \(N\) pays \(S\). Between the two dates the buyer keeps \((1-d)S\) for \(N-k\) days. The extra payment is \(S - (1-d)S = dS\), so the period cost is \(dS\) on an amount of \((1-d)S\). The ratio is \(dS/((1-d)S) = d/(1-d)\), which does not depend on \(S\). Scaling this period cost to a year by Convention 1.2 gives \(c(d,k,N)\). \(\blacksquare\)
        Example 1.4 (terms 2/10 net 30)
        Take \(d = 0.02\), \(k = 10\) and \(N = 30\). The period cost is \(0.02/0.98 = 0.020408\ldots\), over \(N - k = 20\) days. The simple annual cost is \[0.020408\ldots \cdot 365/20 = 0.3724490\ldots\], which is 37.24 percent. The 2 percent discount costs 2.04 percent of the amount kept for those 20 days, not a cost for a year. Lab 2, Part A, prints the same 37.24 percent for these terms, and 44.59 percent compounded.
        Proposition 1.5 (the cost rises with the discount and falls with the gap)
        For fixed \(N - k\), \(c(d,k,N)\) is strictly increasing in \(d\) on \((0,1)\). For fixed \(d\), \(c(d,k,N)\) is strictly decreasing in \(N - k\) on \((0,\infty)\).
        Proof
        The map \(d \mapsto d/(1-d)\) has derivative \(1/(1-d)^2\), which is positive on \((0,1)\), so it is strictly increasing. Multiplying by the positive constant \(365/(N-k)\) keeps that property. For the second claim, \(d/(1-d)\) is fixed and positive, and \(N - k \mapsto 365/(N-k)\) is strictly decreasing on \((0,\infty)\). \(\blacksquare\)
        Remark 1.6 (the same algebra covers financing)
        Proposition 1.3 is a statement about one payment and one later payment. It says nothing about why a seller offers the discount. Section 4 uses the same formula for a financier's discount on an invoice, because the algebra does not change when the party who pays early is a bank and not the buyer.
      2Compounded and simple costs: a basis choice
        Proposition 2.1 (compounding gives the larger cost)
        Let \(N - k \le 365\), and let \(q = 365/(N-k) \ge 1\). The compounded annual cost \((1/(1-d))^{q} - 1\) is at least the simple annual cost \(c(d,k,N)\). The inequality is strict for every \(d \in (0,1)\) when \(N - k
        Proof
        Let \(x = d/(1-d) > 0\) and let \(h(x) = (1+x)^{q} - 1 - qx\) for \(x \ge 0\), with \(q \ge 1\). Then \(h(0) = 0\), and \(h'(x) = q\,[(1+x)^{q-1} - 1]\). For \(q \ge 1\) and \(x \ge 0\) the base \(1 + x\) is at least 1, so \((1+x)^{q-1} \ge 1\) and \(h'(x) \ge 0\). Hence \(h(x) \ge 0\), with \(h(x) > 0\) for \(x > 0\) when \(q > 1\). Since \(c(d,k,N) = qx\) and \((1/(1-d))^q - 1 = (1+x)^q - 1\), the claim follows. \(\blacksquare\)
        Remark 2.2 (which annual figure to quote)
        The simple figure is the one this lesson uses. For a gap of at most 365 days, Proposition 2.1 shows that it is never the larger of the two. A reader who quotes an annual rate must name the convention. Lab 2, Part A, prints both figures for four sets of SIMULATED terms, with gaps of 20, 20, 50 and 30 days, all at most 365, and the compounded figure is larger in each of the four rows.
      3Credit-term extensions and the break-even margin
      A seller that lengthens its credit period from \(N_0\) to \(N_1\) days does two things at once. It ties up more cash in receivables on the sales it already makes, and it may win extra sales. The model below keeps both effects and fixes the rest.
        Assumption 3.0 (the extension model)
        (1) The seller's annual revenue before the change is \(\mathrm{Rev}_0\), collected on terms of \(N_0\) days. (2) The extension adds \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev} > 0\) of revenue each year for years \(1, \dots, n\), and that revenue earns a margin \(m\) per dollar, received at each year end. (3) The new terms apply to all revenue after the change, so the year-end receivable is revenue times the credit period over 365. (4) The extra receivable \(\Delta I\) is paid at period 0, and it is recovered at the end of year \(n\). (5) One rate \(r > 0\) discounts every period. (6) Nothing else changes: costs, bad debts and the pre-change revenue \(\mathrm{Rev}_0\) are as they were. (7) The horizon \(n\) is an integer with \(n \ge 1\).
        Definition 3.1 (the extra investment)
        Under Assumption 3.0, the extra investment in receivables is
        \[ \Delta I = (\mathrm{Rev}_0 + \Delta\mathrm{Rev})\,\frac{N_1}{365} - \mathrm{Rev}_0\,\frac{N_0}{365}. \]
        It is the year-end receivable on the new terms less the receivable on the old terms, on the same convention as Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1.
        Proposition 3.2 (the break-even margin)
        Under Assumption 3.0 with horizon \(n \ge 1\), the extension has a net present value of zero at the margin
        \[ m^\ast = \frac{r\,\Delta I}{\Delta\mathrm{Rev}}, \]
        and this margin does not depend on \(n\). The net present value at a margin \(m\) is
        \[ \mathrm{NPV}(m) = -\Delta I + m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n) + \Delta I\,(1+r)^{-n}, \qquad A(r,n) = \sum_{t=1}^{n}(1+r)^{-t}. \]
        Proof
        The first claim is the expression for \(\mathrm{NPV}(m)\), which follows from Assumption 3.0: the investment is paid at period 0 and returned at the end of year \(n\), and the margin \(m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\) is received at the end of each year \(1, \dots, n\). Discounting each cash flow at \(r\) gives the sum. Summing the geometric series, \(A(r,n) = (1 - (1+r)^{-n})/r\), so \(1 - (1+r)^{-n} = r\,A(r,n)\). Setting \(\mathrm{NPV}(m) = 0\) and using this identity gives \[-\Delta I\,(1 - (1+r)^{-n}) + m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n) = 0\], that is \[-\Delta I\,r\,A(r,n) + m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n) = 0\]. Since \(A(r,n) > 0\), dividing by it gives \(m = r\,\Delta I/\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\). The right side has no \(n\). \(\blacksquare\)
        Corollary 3.3 (the decision rule)
        Under Assumption 3.0 with horizon \(n \ge 1\), \(\mathrm{NPV}(m)\) is strictly increasing in \(m\), because \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n) > 0\). So the extension has a positive net present value exactly when the margin exceeds \(m^\ast\).
        Proof
        The derivative of \(\mathrm{NPV}(m)\) in \(m\) is \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n)\), which is positive because \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev} > 0\) and \(A(r,n) > 0\). Hence \(\mathrm{NPV}\) crosses zero once, at \(m^\ast\). \(\blacksquare\)
        Example 3.4 (a SIMULATED extension)
        Let \(\mathrm{Rev}_0 = 12{,}000{,}000\) dollars, \(N_0 = 30\), \(N_1 = 45\), \(\Delta\mathrm{Rev} = 1{,}500{,}000\) dollars and \(r = 0.10\). Definition 3.1 gives \[\Delta I = (13{,}500{,}000 \cdot 45 - 12{,}000{,}000 \cdot 30)/365 = 678{,}082.19\] dollars. Proposition 3.2 gives \[m^\ast = 0.10 \cdot 678{,}082.19 / 1{,}500{,}000 = 0.0452\], which is 4.52 percent of extra revenue. Lab 2, Part B, prints the same investment and margin, and an NPV at a 5 percent margin of 27,262.51 dollars over five years. A margin of 5 percent is above the break-even, so the NPV is positive.
        Remark 3.5 (where the result comes from)
        Proposition 3.2 is an exact result under Assumption 3.0. Its simple form, \(r\,\Delta I/\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\), says that the break-even margin is the carrying charge on the extra receivable, one year's interest at \(r\), spread over the extra revenue. It does not say that the carrying charge is the right rate; \(r\) is an input that the analyst must justify.

        Supply-chain finance is a payment arrangement in which a financier pays the supplier, before the invoice's due date, a sum \((1-\delta)S\) that is less than the invoice \(S\). The buyer then pays the financier the full \(S\) on the original due date \(N\). The discount \(\delta\), with \(0

        Proof
        The supplier receives \((1-\delta)S\) on day \(k\) in place of \(S\) on day \(N\). This is the early-payment choice of Proposition 1.3 with \(d = \delta\), so the cost is the same expression. For \(0  0\) and \(365/(N-k) > 0\), so the cost is positive. \(\blacksquare\)

        Under Definition 4.1, the buyer's trade payable stays at the invoice amount \(S\) until the due date \(N\), and the program changes neither that balance nor the buyer's purchases.
        Remark 4.3 (the program leaves the buyer's DPO unchanged)
        Under Assumption 4.2 the program leaves the buyer's payable balance and its purchases unchanged, so the buyer's DPO, \(\mathrm{AP}\cdot D/\mathrm{Pur}\) in Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1, is unchanged. A change in the buyer's DPO can come from a change in its payable balance or in its purchases, and a change in the due date \(N\) can change the payable balance at a year end. The supplier receives cash on day \(k\) instead of day \(N\). Whether the supplier's balance sheet still shows the receivable is an accounting question that this lesson does not settle.

SIMULATED: every term, balance, margin and rate below is an invented input for this lab. No company, supplier or
financier is real, and the lab reads no data file and makes no network request. Money is in integer cents: each
revenue and receivable is an int number of cents, and NPVs are exact Fractions of cents. The one rounding step is the
investment in receivables, a day-count ratio that is rounded half up to whole cents in extension_investment. Each
printed dollar amount is a cent count divided by 100 and rounded half up to the cent for display only. The break-even
margin and the NPV checks use fractions.Fraction and are exact. The compounded implied cost in Part A is computed in
floating point and printed to two decimals. Part D draws its cases from a seeded random number generator. Standard
library only.
"""
from __future__ import annotations
import math
import random
from decimal import ROUND_HALF_UP, Decimal
from fractions import Fraction
DAYS_PER_YEAR = 365
SEED = 20261029
RATE = Fraction(1, 10)
def pct_text(value: Fraction) -> str:
    """A rate as a percentage rounded half up to two decimals, for display only."""
    quotient = Decimal(value.numerator) * 100 / Decimal(value.denominator)
    return str(quotient.quantize(Decimal("0.01"), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP)) + "%"
def dollars_text(cents: Fraction) -> str:
    """An amount in cents shown in dollars to two decimals, rounded half up for display only."""
    quotient = Decimal(cents.numerator) / Decimal(cents.denominator) / 100
    return str(quotient.quantize(Decimal("0.01"), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP))
def implied_simple_cost(discount: Fraction, days_early: int, days_late: int) -> Fraction:
    """Annual simple cost of giving up a discount d for paying N - k days later: d/(1-d) times 365/(N-k)."""
    if days_late <= days_early:
        raise ValueError("the full payment must fall after the discount date")
    return discount / (1 - discount) * Fraction(DAYS_PER_YEAR, days_late - days_early)
def implied_compound_cost(discount: Fraction, days_early: int, days_late: int) -> float:
    """Annual compounded cost in floating point: (1/(1-d)) raised to 365/(N-k), minus one."""
    factor = float(1 / (1 - discount))
    power = float(factor ** (DAYS_PER_YEAR / (days_late - days_early)))
    return power - 1.0
def npv_extension(margin: Fraction, extra_margin_base: int, extra_invest: int, years: int) -> Fraction:
    """NPV at RATE of an extension, in cents: pay the investment now, earn margin times the extra revenue each year,
    get the investment back at year n. The extra revenue is extra_margin_base; the investment is extra_invest."""
    annuity = sum((1 / (1 + RATE)) ** t for t in range(1, years + 1))
    return -Fraction(extra_invest) + margin * extra_margin_base * annuity + Fraction(extra_invest) / (1 + RATE) ** years
def break_even_margin(extra_revenue: int, extra_invest: int) -> Fraction:
    """Break-even margin m* = r times the investment over the extra revenue (the closed form of Section 3)."""
    return RATE * extra_invest / extra_revenue
def extension_investment(revenue: int, days_old: int, days_new: int, extra_revenue: int) -> int:
    """Extra receivables from the new terms in whole cents: (revenue + extra revenue) times the new days, less the old
    balance, rounded half up to the cent once."""
    new_balance = Fraction(revenue + extra_revenue) * days_new / DAYS_PER_YEAR
    old_balance = Fraction(revenue) * days_old / DAYS_PER_YEAR
    return math.floor((new_balance - old_balance) + Fraction(1, 2))

      5Where the canon breaks
      The algebra is exact. The model that feeds it is not, and four assumptions and conventions carry the results:
        The investment is recovered at year \(n\). Proposition 3.2 uses this recovery. If the receivable is never recovered, the break-even margin is higher, as Problem R1 shows. The horizon of the extension is a judgment the model does not supply.
        The margin is a stated input. The model takes \(m\) as given, with the costs that vary with extra sales already removed. Estimating \(m\) is a separate task.
        Default risk is outside the model. Assumption 3.0 item (6) keeps bad debts unchanged. A longer credit period could bring more non-payment, and the model does not price it.
        Compounding is a convention. Section 2 shows that, for a gap of at most 365 days, the compounded cost is at least the simple cost, strictly when the gap is below 365 days, and a comparison with a rate of borrowing needs both on one basis.
        6Misconceptions
            “A 2 percent discount for paying early is a 2 percent cost.”
            The 2 percent discount costs 2.04 percent of the amount kept, over the days between the early date and the due date. Under the terms 2/10 net 30 the gap is 20 days, and the simple annual cost is 37.24 percent (Example 1.4 and Lab 2, Part A). The same 2 percent over a 50-day gap, as in 2/10 net 60, costs 14.90 percent a year, so the gap must appear next to the rate.
            “A credit extension pays if the extra sales are large enough to cover the costs.”
            The extra receivable on existing sales is a cost, and the test is the margin on the extra revenue against \(r\,\Delta I/\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\), which includes the carrying charge on the whole receivable increase. Corollary 3.3 says the extension has a positive net present value exactly when the margin exceeds that break-even.
            “A firm that stretches its payables pays nothing for the longer period.”
            If the supplier offers an early-payment discount, the stretch gives up that discount. Proposition 1.3 gives the cost of the period, and for 2/10 net 30 it is 37.24 percent a year on the simple convention. Section 3 does not model a payables stretch, so this lesson makes no claim about its cost beyond the discount forgone.

            M1 · The implied cost of 1/15 net 45
            Terms of 1/15 net 45 give a discount of 1 percent for payment by day 15 and full payment by day 45. Find the simple annual cost of the early payment, to two decimals.
            Solution
              Here \(d = 0.01\), \(k = 15\) and \(N = 45\), so \(N - k = 30\). Proposition 1.3 gives \[c = (0.01/0.99)\cdot 365/30 = 0.010101\ldots \cdot 12.1667 = 0.1228956\ldots\], which is 12.29 percent a year on the simple convention.
            M2 · Break-even margin of an extension
            A seller has revenue of 6,000,000 dollars a year on 30-day terms. It extends terms to 60 days and expects 600,000 dollars of extra revenue a year for four years. The rate is 8 percent. Find the extra receivable in dollars to the cent, and the break-even margin as a share of extra revenue, to two decimals.
            Solution
              By Definition 3.1, \[\Delta I = (6{,}600{,}000 \cdot 60 - 6{,}000{,}000 \cdot 30)/365 = (396{,}000{,}000 - 180{,}000{,}000)/365 = 216{,}000{,}000/365 = 591{,}780.8219\ldots\] dollars, which is 591,780.82 dollars to the cent. By Proposition 3.2, \[m^\ast = 0.08 \cdot 591{,}780.8219\ldots/600{,}000 = 0.078904\ldots\], which is 7.89 percent. The horizon of four years does not enter the answer.
            M3 · NPV at a margin of 10 percent
            For the extension of Problem M2, find the net present value at a margin of 10 percent of extra revenue, in dollars to the cent, using the closed form of Proposition 3.2.
            Solution
              The annuity factor is \[A(0.08, 4) = \sum_{t=1}^{4} 1.08^{-t} = (1 - 1.08^{-4})/0.08 = 3.3121268\ldots\]. Then \[\mathrm{NPV}(0.10) = -591{,}780.8219 + 0.10 \cdot 600{,}000 \cdot 3.3121268\ldots + 591{,}780.8219 \cdot 1.08^{-4}\]. The three terms are \(-591{,}780.82\), \(198{,}727.61\) and \(434{,}976.57\) dollars to the cent. Their sum is \(41{,}923.36\) dollars to the cent, which is the exact sum \(41{,}923.3589\ldots\) dollars rounded half up. The margin of 10 percent is above the break-even of 7.89 percent, so the sign is positive, as Corollary 3.3 requires.
          Proof
            P1 · The period cost is d over one minus d
            Show that paying \((1-d)S\) on day \(k\) rather than \(S\) on day \(N\) earns a period return of \(d/(1-d)\) on the amount paid early, for every \(S > 0\).
            Solution
              Keeping \((1-d)S\) for the period means not paying \(S - (1-d)S = dS\) on day \(N\), which is the gain. The return on the amount paid early is the gain over the amount paid: \(dS/((1-d)S) = d/(1-d)\). The factor \(S\) cancels, so the return is the same for every invoice size. The argument uses only the two payment dates and the two amounts.

            P3 · Compounding gives no less than simple for a 30-day gap
            Prove Proposition 2.1 for \(N - k = 30\) and any \(d \in (0,1)\), by showing \((1+x)^q - 1 \ge qx\) with \(x = d/(1-d)\) and \(q = 365/30\).
            Solution
              Here \(q = 365/30 = 12.1667 \ge 1\) and \(x > 0\). Let \(h(x) = (1+x)^q - 1 - qx\). Then \(h(0) = 0\) and \(h'(x) = q[(1+x)^{q-1} - 1]\). Because \(q - 1 > 0\) and \(1 + x > 1\), the power \((1+x)^{q-1} > 1\), so \(h' > 0\) on \((0, \infty)\). Hence \(h(x) > 0\) for \(x > 0\), which is the claim. The proof uses the exponent \(q \ge 1\), which holds because \(N - k \le 365\).

            A SIMULATED buyer is offered 2/10 net 30, 1/15 net 45 and 2/10 net 60. Its bank line costs 9 percent a year on the simple convention. Find the simple annual cost of each offer, rank them, and state whether paying early beats the bank line for each.
            Solution

            E2 · A SIMULATED extension with a 6 percent margin
            A seller has revenue of 20,000,000 dollars on 30-day terms and considers 45-day terms.           Research
            R1 · An extension that is never recovered
            Suppose the extra receivable is never recovered, and the margin is earned for \(n\) years only. Find the break-even margin \(m_{\mathrm{nr}}\) and show that it exceeds \(m^\ast\) for every integer horizon \(n \ge 1\) and every \(r > 0\). Evaluate it for the extension of Problem M2.
            Solution
              With no recovery, \[\mathrm{NPV}(m) = -\Delta I + m\,\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n)\], so \[m_{\mathrm{nr}} = \Delta I/(\Delta\mathrm{Rev}\,A(r,n))\]. Using \(A(r,n) = (1-(1+r)^{-n})/r\) gives \[m_{\mathrm{nr}} = m^\ast/(1-(1+r)^{-n})\]. Since \(0  m^\ast\). For Problem M2, \(A(0.08,4) = 3.3121268\ldots\), so \[m_{\mathrm{nr}} = 591{,}780.82/(600{,}000 \cdot 3.312126) = 0.297785\ldots\], which is 29.78 percent, against 7.89 percent with recovery. The ratio of the margin without recovery to \(m^\ast\) is \(1/(1-1.08^{-4}) = 3.77\).
            R2 · A concentrated set of customers
            Extra sales come from three large customers, not spread across all customers. Describe which inputs of Assumption 3.0 fail, and what to measure before the break-even margin is used.
            Solution
              Assumption 3.0 item (2) assumes the extra revenue arrives as a stable flow each year. Three customers can leave, delay or renegotiate, so the flow is uncertain and its timing is lumpy. Item (6) assumes bad debts are unchanged, and a large customer's default is a large change. Before the margin is used, the analyst should measure each customer's history of payment against terms, the share of the extra revenue each customer supplies, and the margin on each customer's sales. The model does not give these inputs, and its result depends on them.
            R3 · What data would show a change in a buyer's DPO
            A supplier wants to know whether its large customer lengthened its payable period after joining a supply-chain finance program. State the three quantities needed and the test that uses them, without assuming the answer.
            Solution
              The three quantities are the customer's year-end payables, its purchases in the year, and the due dates on its open invoices before and after joining the program. From the first two, the customer's DPO follows from Definition 1.1 of Lesson 1, before and after. The due dates show whether the contract term changed; this lesson does not model that change, and Remark 4.3 notes that a change in the due date can move the payable balance at a year end. The test compares DPO across the two periods on the same basis and checks whether the due-date change accounts for it. A change in DPO can come from the payables balance, from purchases, or from a change in the due dates. Holding purchases fixed separates the balance effect from the purchase effect. The analyst needs all three quantities before naming a cause.
