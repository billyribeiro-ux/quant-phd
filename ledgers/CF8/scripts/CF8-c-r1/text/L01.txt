      A firm pays suppliers, holds goods, sells on credit and collects later. The cash it must finance in that gap is its working capital, and the gap is measured in days. Three day counts describe it: how many days of sales sit in receivables, how many days of cost of sales sit in inventory, and how many days of purchases are still owed to suppliers. Their combination is the cash conversion cycle, the number of days between paying for inputs and collecting for outputs. This lesson defines the three counts and the cycle, proves the identity that links them, shows what one count does to cash, and sets out the basis choices to check before comparing two firms.
      1Three day counts
      The counts in this lesson use year-end balances and full-year flows. A balance is a stock read from the balance sheet at the fiscal year end. A flow is a total for the year read from the income statement, or, for purchases, from the purchase records the firm reports. The year has \(D = 365\) days. The lesson uses that convention throughout, and Section 4 shows how the count moves on other bases. The filers in this lesson are SIMULATED, and no filer on this page is a real company's.
      Write \(\mathrm{AR}\) for year-end trade receivables, \(\mathrm{Inv}\) for year-end inventory, \(\mathrm{AP}\) for year-end trade payables, \(\mathrm{Rev}\) for revenue, \(\mathrm{COGS}\) for cost of sales and \(\mathrm{Pur}\) for purchases of goods in the year. Each day count below is a balance times 365 over the flow that the balance is held against.
        Definition 1.1 (working-capital days)
        The working-capital days of a year are the three counts
        \[ \mathrm{DSO} = \frac{\mathrm{AR}\cdot D}{\mathrm{Rev}}, \qquad \mathrm{DIO} = \frac{\mathrm{Inv}\cdot D}{\mathrm{COGS}}, \qquad \mathrm{DPO} = \frac{\mathrm{AP}\cdot D}{\mathrm{Pur}}. \]
        \(\mathrm{DSO}\) is the days of sales held as receivables, \(\mathrm{DIO}\) the days of cost of sales held as inventory, and \(\mathrm{DPO}\) the days of purchases still owed to suppliers. Each count is in days, and each is a ratio of a year-end stock to a full-year flow. The denominators are different on purpose: receivables are compared with sales because they arise from sales, inventory with cost of sales because it leaves the firm at cost, and payables with purchases because they arise from purchases.
        Remark 1.2 (the denominators are conventions)
        Definition 1.1 fixes the denominators. A reader who divides receivables by cost of sales, or payables by cost of sales when purchases are not reported, gets a different number, and the number answers a different question. Section 4 states the choice this lesson makes and the effect of the average-balance and 360-day alternatives on the day counts.
      2The cash conversion cycle and its identity
        Definition 2.1 (cash conversion cycle)
        The cash conversion cycle of a year is
        \[ \mathrm{CCC} = \mathrm{DSO} + \mathrm{DIO} - \mathrm{DPO}. \]
        The cycle adds the days the firm holds goods and the days it waits for customers, and subtracts the days its suppliers wait for it. A positive cycle means the firm finances the gap; a negative cycle means suppliers finance it.
        Assumption 2.0 (positive flows)
        Revenue, cost of sales and purchases for the year are positive, \(\mathrm{Rev}, \mathrm{COGS}, \mathrm{Pur} > 0\), so that each count in Definition 1.1 is defined.
        Proposition 2.2 (the cycle is exactly its three parts)
        Under Definitions 1.1 and 2.1 and Assumption 2.0, for any year, the cash conversion cycle equals \(\mathrm{DSO} + \mathrm{DIO} - \mathrm{DPO}\) exactly, with no approximation and no residual term.
        Proof
        Definition 2.1 defines \(\mathrm{CCC}\) as \(\mathrm{DSO} + \mathrm{DIO} - \mathrm{DPO}\), so the claim is an identity between two expressions for one quantity. The three counts are finite because their denominators are positive by Assumption 2.0. No step uses an approximation, so the equality holds as an identity of rational numbers. \(\blacksquare\)
        Remark 2.3 (what the identity does not say)
        Proposition 2.2 is an accounting identity. It does not say that the cycle is short for a good firm or long for a bad one. The cycle is a description. Whether a particular cycle is a problem depends on the firm's sales, its margins and the cost of the cash it ties up, which L02 (Trade Credit, Credit Policy and Supply-Chain Finance) takes up as the implied annual cost of supplier credit.
        Proposition 2.4 (one-for-one with receivables)
        Under Assumption 2.0, let revenue, cost of sales and purchases be held fixed, and let inventory and payables be unchanged. If year-end receivables change by \(\Delta\mathrm{AR}\), then \(\Delta\mathrm{DSO} = \Delta\mathrm{AR}\cdot D/\mathrm{Rev}\) and \(\Delta\mathrm{CCC} = \Delta\mathrm{DSO}\). The other two counts do not change.
        Proof
        Hypotheses used: Assumption 2.0, with \(\mathrm{Rev}\), \(\mathrm{COGS}\) and \(\mathrm{Pur}\) fixed and \(\mathrm{Inv}\) and \(\mathrm{AP}\) unchanged, so only \(\mathrm{AR}\) changes. From Definition 1.1, \(\mathrm{DSO}\) is linear in \(\mathrm{AR}\) with slope \(D/\mathrm{Rev}\), so \(\Delta\mathrm{DSO} = \Delta\mathrm{AR}\cdot D/\mathrm{Rev}\). The counts \(\mathrm{DIO}\) and \(\mathrm{DPO}\) depend on \(\mathrm{Inv}\), \(\mathrm{COGS}\), \(\mathrm{AP}\) and \(\mathrm{Pur}\) only, none of which changed, so they are unchanged. Substituting into Definition 2.1 gives \(\Delta\mathrm{CCC} = \Delta\mathrm{DSO} + 0 - 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
        Remark 2.5 (the fixed-revenue hypothesis carries the result)
        Proposition 2.4 needs revenue held fixed. If a change in receivables comes with a change in revenue, the denominator of \(\mathrm{DSO}\) moves as well, and the change in \(\mathrm{DSO}\) is no longer \(\Delta\mathrm{AR}\cdot D/\mathrm{Rev}\) at the old revenue. Lab 1, Part B, checks the fixed-revenue case only.

        A SIMULATED filer has revenue of 2,400,000 dollars, cost of sales of 1,500,000 dollars, purchases of 1,600,000 dollars, receivables of 300,000 dollars, inventory of 250,000 dollars and payables of 200,000 dollars. Its DSO is \(300{,}000 \cdot 365 / 2{,}400{,}000 = 45.625\) days, its DIO is \(250{,}000 \cdot 365 / 1{,}500{,}000 = 60.8333\ldots\) days and its DPO is \(200{,}000 \cdot 365 / 1{,}600{,}000 = 45.625\) days.         Remark 3.4 (a day is worth revenue over 365)
        Proposition 3.2 gives a rule of thumb that is exact under its hypothesis: one day of DSO is worth \(\mathrm{Rev}/365\) of cash, once. For the filer above, one day is worth 6,575.34 dollars. The release is a one-time change in the balance. It does not repeat each year unless the firm keeps shortening the period. If payables also change in the same period, the net cash effect differs from this release, which is why Assumption 3.0 excludes that case.
      4Basis choices that move a day count
      Two firms can have the same balances and different day counts if they use different bases. The three choices below are conventions, and each one must be stated before two counts are compared.
        Assumption 4.0 (the basis of this lesson)
        The lesson's day counts use year-end balances, a 365-day year and full-year flows (revenue, cost of sales and purchases). Revenue, cost of sales and purchases are positive, so each count is defined. Section 4 gives the effect of an average-balance basis. Proposition 2.2 is an identity in whatever counts are defined, so it holds on any basis. Propositions 2.4 and 3.2 are stated with the year-end form of Definition 1.1 and are not restated for other bases.
        Proposition 4.1 (average against year-end receivables)
        Under Assumption 4.0, let \(\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{prev}}\) be receivables at the prior year end and \(\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{end}}\) at this year end, and let \(\mathrm{DSO}_{\mathrm{avg}}\) use the average of the two in place of \(\mathrm{AR}\). Then
        \[ \mathrm{DSO}_{\mathrm{avg}} - \mathrm{DSO}_{\mathrm{end}} = \frac{(\mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{prev}} - \mathrm{AR}_{\mathrm{end}})\,D}{2\,\mathrm{Rev}}. \]
        The average basis gives the lower count when receivables rose during the year, and the higher count when they fell.

        Remark 4.3 (the other two bases)
        Two more choices move the counts. Dividing payables by a figure other than purchases changes DPO, and Definition 1.1 divides by purchases. A 360-day year rescales all three counts by \(360/365\), so a 360-day DSO is \(360/365\) of the 365-day figure. The lesson does not claim which convention a particular data source uses; a reader must read the source's definition before comparing two numbers.

def to_cents(amount: Fraction) -> int:
    """Return a non-negative exact amount in cents as a whole number of cents, with halves taken upward."""
    return math.floor(amount + Fraction(1, 2))
def days_text(value: Fraction) -> str:
    """A day count rounded half up to two decimals, for display only; the exact value is kept elsewhere."""
    quotient = Decimal(value.numerator) / Decimal(value.denominator)
    return str(quotient.quantize(Decimal("0.01"), rounding=ROUND_HALF_UP))
def dso(receivables_cents: int, revenue_cents: int) -> Fraction:
    """Days sales outstanding: year-end receivables times 365 over revenue for the year."""
    return Fraction(receivables_cents * DAYS_PER_YEAR, revenue_cents)
def dio(inventory_cents: int, cost_of_sales_cents: int) -> Fraction:
    """Days inventory outstanding: year-end inventory times 365 over cost of sales for the year."""
    return Fraction(inventory_cents * DAYS_PER_YEAR, cost_of_sales_cents)
def dpo(payables_cents: int, purchases_cents: int) -> Fraction:
    """Days payables outstanding: year-end payables times 365 over purchases for the year."""
    return Fraction(payables_cents * DAYS_PER_YEAR, purchases_cents)
def cycle(
    receivables_cents: int,
    revenue_cents: int,
    inventory_cents: int,
    cost_of_sales_cents: int,
    payables_cents: int,
    purchases_cents: int,
) -> Fraction:
    """Cash conversion cycle in days: DSO plus DIO minus DPO, computed from the three exact day counts."""
    return (
        dso(receivables_cents, revenue_cents)
        + dio(inventory_cents, cost_of_sales_cents)
        - dpo(payables_cents, purchases_cents)
    )
def generated_filer(rng: random.Random) -> dict[str, int]:
    """One SIMULATED filer with plausible but invented balances; every amount is an integer number of cents."""
    revenue = rng.randint(2_000_000_000, 20_000_000_000)
    cost = revenue * rng.randint(45, 75) // 100
    purchases = cost * rng.randint(90, 115) // 100
    return {
        "revenue_cents": revenue,
        "cost_of_sales_cents": cost,
        "purchases_cents": purchases,
        "receivables_cents": revenue * rng.randint(5, 20) // 100,
        "inventory_cents": cost * rng.randint(5, 25) // 100,
        "payables_cents": purchases * rng.randint(5, 20) // 100,
    }

      5Where the canon breaks
      The identity is exact. The reading of the day counts is not, and four places need the analyst's judgment:
        A day count is an annual average. \(\mathrm{DSO}\) divides a year-end balance by a full-year flow. A year-end receivables balance can differ from the average balance over the year, so a year-end DSO can differ from the average-balance DSO (Section 4). The count describes the year's ratio, not the collection time of any invoice.
        The fixed-revenue hypothesis carries Proposition 2.4. When revenue changes, the denominator of DSO changes, and the cycle moves for two reasons at once. Proposition 2.4 gives the receivables effect alone when the revenue hypothesis holds.
        The basis is a choice. Section 4 shows a difference of 3.04 days for one SIMULATED filer between two bases that are both correct. A comparison of two firms' day counts also compares their bases, unless the bases are the same.
        Purchases are an input. DPO needs purchases in the year. A filer that reports cost of sales but not purchases needs a stated assumption about purchases, and the lesson does not supply one.
        6Misconceptions
            “A shorter cash conversion cycle is always better for the firm.”
            The cycle is a description of how long cash is tied up. A shorter DSO releases cash under Proposition 3.2, which is exact under Assumption 3.0 (revenue fixed, receivables the only balance that moves). A shorter cycle does not show by itself that the firm is better off, because the change may cost sales or raise the price of what it buys. Proposition 3.2 says nothing about what customers do in response. L02 computes the implied annual cost of an early-payment discount, which is the cost of a longer payable period.
            “DSO is the number of days a customer takes to pay.”
            DSO divides year-end receivables by annual revenue, so it mixes the timing of payments with the level and seasonality of sales. Lab 1, Part D, gives two DSO values for the same filer in the same year (48.67 and 45.63 days) that differ in the balance used. Neither is a payment record for a customer.
            “A change in the cycle moves cash one-for-one with the change in days.”
            A change in DSO moves the cycle by the same number of days when DIO and DPO are unchanged, whatever revenue does (Definition 2.1 and Proposition 2.2). Holding revenue fixed is needed for the dollar release of Proposition 3.2 and for the formula \(\Delta\mathrm{DSO} = \Delta\mathrm{AR}\cdot D/\mathrm{Rev}\) of Proposition 2.4. One day of DSO is worth \(\mathrm{Rev}/365\) dollars, so when revenue changes, the dollar effect of the same day change changes too.

            Solution
              DSO is \(300{,}000 \cdot 365 / 2{,}400{,}000 = 45.625\), which is 45.63 days. DIO is \(250{,}000 \cdot 365 / 1{,}500{,}000 = 60.8333\ldots\), which is 60.83 days. Here the cycle equals DIO exactly, because DSO and DPO are equal.
            M2 · Cash released by a shorter DSO
            For the filer of Problem M1, find the cash released by cutting DSO by 10 days with revenue held at 2,400,000 dollars. Give the answer in dollars to the cent.
            Solution
              By Proposition 3.2 under Assumption 3.0, the release is \(\mathrm{Rev}\cdot d/D = 2{,}400{,}000 \cdot 10 / 365 = 65{,}753.42465\ldots\) dollars. To the cent this is 65,753.42 dollars. The exact value is the one to carry, and it rounds to the cent as stated.
            M3 · Stretching payables with purchases fixed
            A filer with purchases of 1,460,000 dollars a year lengthens its payables from 30 days of purchases to 45 days of purchases, holding purchases fixed. Find the increase in payables in dollars, and the change in DPO in days.
            Solution
              DPO is \(\mathrm{AP}\cdot D/\mathrm{Pur}\), so \(\Delta\mathrm{AP} = \Delta\mathrm{DPO}\cdot\mathrm{Pur}/D = 15 \cdot 1{,}460{,}000 / 365 = 60{,}000\) dollars exactly. The change in DPO is 15 days, and the payables increase is 60,000.00 dollars. The increase in payables reduces the cycle by 15 days, by Proposition 2.2.
          Proof
            P1 · Scaling the flows leaves the cycle unchanged
            Let every flow and every balance of a filer be multiplied by the same \(k > 0\). Show that DSO, DIO, DPO and the cycle are unchanged.
            Solution
              Each day count is a balance times 365 over a flow, so after scaling it is \((k\,\mathrm{AR})\cdot D/(k\,\mathrm{Rev}) = \mathrm{AR}\cdot D/\mathrm{Rev}\). The factor \(k\) cancels in each ratio, so DSO, DIO and DPO are unchanged, and so is their combination \(\mathrm{CCC}\). The cycle is a ratio of dollars to dollars, so it does not depend on the unit of money, which is why the lab can be read in cents or in dollars.
            P2 · The cash released is linear in the days
            Prove that the cash released by a fall in DSO from \(\mathrm{DSO}_0\) to \(\mathrm{DSO}_0 - d\), with revenue fixed, is \(\mathrm{Rev}\cdot d/D\), and that the release for \(d_1 + d_2\) days equals the sum of the releases for \(d_1\) and \(d_2\) days.
            Solution
              By Proposition 3.2 under Assumption 3.0, the release is \(\mathrm{Rev}\cdot d/D\), which is a constant times \(d\). For \(d = d_1 + d_2\), the release is \(\mathrm{Rev}(d_1 + d_2)/D = \mathrm{Rev}\,d_1/D + \mathrm{Rev}\,d_2/D\), which is the sum of the two releases. The additivity holds because the release depends on the days only through the product with a constant, with no interaction term.

          Empirical
            E1 · Decompose the change in the cycle
            A SIMULATED filer has two years. In 2025: revenue 5,000,000 dollars, receivables 600,000 dollars, cost of sales 3,000,000 dollars, inventory 480,000 dollars, purchases 3,200,000 dollars and payables 400,000 dollars. In 2026: revenue 6,000,000 dollars, receivables 840,000 dollars, cost of sales 3,900,000 dollars, inventory 585,000 dollars, purchases 4,000,000 dollars and payables 480,000 dollars. Find each day count and the cycle in both years, and split the change in the cycle into its three parts, to two decimals.
            Solution
              For 2025, DSO is \(600{,}000 \cdot 365/5{,}000{,}000 = 43.80\), DIO is \(480{,}000 \cdot 365/3{,}000{,}000 = 58.40\), and DPO is \(400{,}000 \cdot 365/3{,}200{,}000 = 45.625\), which is 45.63. The cycle is \(43.80 + 58.40 - 45.625 = 56.575\), which is 56.58 at half-up rounding of the exact value. For 2026, DSO is \(51.10\), DIO is \(54.75\), DPO is \(43.80\) and the cycle is \(62.05\). The change in the cycle is \(62.05 - 56.575 = 5.475\), which is 5.48. Its parts are \(\Delta\mathrm{DSO} = 7.30\), \(\Delta\mathrm{DIO} = -3.65\) and \(-\Delta\mathrm{DPO} = +1.825\), which is 1.83. Their sum is 5.475, so the split is exact.
            E2 · Which part moved the most
            Using E1, name the part of the change in the cycle with the largest absolute size, and say whether it lengthened or shortened the cycle.
            Solution
              The largest absolute part is \(\Delta\mathrm{DSO} = 7.30\) days, which lengthened the cycle. The inventory part shortened it by 3.65 days, and the payables part lengthened it by 1.825 days, because DPO fell. E1 uses the year-end basis with a 365-day year, the basis of Definition 1.1.
            E3 · Average basis for the E1 filer
            For the filer of E1, compute DSO for 2026 on year-end receivables and on the average of the 2025 and 2026 balances, and check the difference against Proposition 4.1.
            Solution
              On year-end receivables, DSO is 51.10 days. The average balance is \((600{,}000 + 840{,}000)/2 = 720{,}000\), so the average-basis DSO is \(720{,}000 \cdot 365/6{,}000{,}000 = 43.80\) days. The difference is \(-7.30\) days. Proposition 4.1 gives \((600{,}000 - 840{,}000)\cdot 365/(2 \cdot 6{,}000{,}000) = -240{,}000 \cdot 365/12{,}000{,}000 = -7.30\) days, which agrees.
          Research
            R1 · Which basis for a growing firm
            A firm's receivables grow every year. Argue which of the year-end and average bases gives the count a reader should use to judge collection discipline, and state what evidence would decide between them.
            Solution
              Proposition 4.1 shows that when receivables rose during the year, the average-basis DSO is below the year-end DSO, so the average basis gives a lower count than the year-end basis does. For a judgment about the balance on the books at a date, the year-end basis matches the balance. For a judgment about the flow of sales through the year, the average basis matches the balance outstanding over the period. The decision rule is to choose the basis that matches the question, and to report both when the balances move much during the year. The evidence that would decide is the firm's own monthly receivables and monthly sales, which this lesson does not have.
            R2 · A seasonal business
            A business has revenue concentrated in the last quarter, so its year-end receivables are high relative to its annual revenue. Describe how the year-end DSO can mislead a reader, and say what single additional number would show the size of the distortion.
            Solution
              Year-end receivables can be built mostly from the last quarter's sales, while annual revenue covers four quarters. The year-end DSO then compares a balance that may reflect one quarter's sales with a flow that covers four, and it can be high or low depending on the timing of the sales. A count that exposes the distortion is the year-end receivables divided by the last quarter's revenue, multiplied by the number of days in that quarter. Comparing that count with the annual DSO shows how much the timing matters. The count uses the same definition as Definition 1.1, applied to a quarter.
            R3 · Revenue growth and the cycle
            Two firms have the same DSO and DIO but different revenue growth. Explain why a reader cannot tell from the cycle alone whether the firm with faster growth is tying up more cash, and state the extra quantity needed to decide.
            Solution
              The cycle is a ratio of balances to flows. Two firms with the same DSO and DIO have receivables proportional to revenue and inventory proportional to cost of sales, so a larger flow means a larger balance, which can mean more cash tied up in dollars even at the same cycle. The cycle is silent on the level. The extra quantity is the trade working capital in dollars, \(\mathrm{AR} + \mathrm{Inv} - \mathrm{AP}\), or its change over the year. Proposition 3.2 converts a day change into dollars with revenue, which is why the dollar figure, not the cycle, answers the question of cash.
