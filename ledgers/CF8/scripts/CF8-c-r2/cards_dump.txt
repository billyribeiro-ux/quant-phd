CF8-c001 L01 - <NOBASIS> | What are the three working-capital day counts, and what is the denominator of each?
   B: DSO = year-end receivables × 365 / revenue; DIO = year-end inventory × 365 / cost of sales; DPO = year-end payables × 365 / purchases for the year (Definition 1.1). Each is a year-end stock held against a full-year flow, and the denominators differ on purpose.
CF8-c002 L01 - <NOBASIS> | What is the cash conversion cycle?
   B: CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO, the number of days between paying for inputs and collecting for outputs (Definition 2.1). A positive cycle means the firm finances the gap, and a negative cycle means suppliers finance it.
CF8-c003 L01 - <NOBASIS> | What is trade working capital, and what does it exclude?
   B: At a year end, TWC = AR + Inv − AP (Definition 3.1). Cash, other receivables and financing balances are excluded.
CF8-c004 L01 T Proposition 2.2 (Section 2) | Under Definitions 1.1 and 2.1, for any year with revenue, cost of sales and purchases all positive, does CCC equal DSO + DIO − DPO exactly?
   B: Yes. The equality is an identity with no approximation and no residual term (Proposition 2.2). Its proof is the definition of CCC in Section 2.
CF8-c005 L01 T Proposition 2.4 (Section 2); Lab 1, Part B checks the fixed-revenue case | With revenue, cost of sales and purchases held fixed and only year-end receivables changing by ΔAR, how do DSO and CCC change?
   B: ΔDSO = ΔAR × 365 / revenue, and ΔCCC = ΔDSO. DIO and DPO do not change, because they depend only on inventory, cost of sales, payables and purchases (Proposition 2.4).
CF8-c006 L01 T Assumption 3.0 and Proposition 3.2 (Section 3); Problem M2 | With revenue fixed at Rev, receivables the only balance that changes, and DSO falling by d days where 0 < d < DSO, how much cash is released?
   B: Exactly Rev × d / 365. The release is linear in d, so doubling d doubles the cash (Proposition 3.2 under Assumption 3.0).
CF8-c007 L01 T Proposition 4.1 (Section 4); Lab 1, Part D | Under the average-balance basis, how does DSO_avg differ from year-end DSO?
   B: DSO_avg − DSO_end = (AR_prev − AR_end) × 365 / (2 × revenue), where AR_prev and AR_end are the prior and current year-end receivables (Proposition 4.1). The average basis gives the lower count when receivables rose during the year.
CF8-c008 L01 T Problem M1 (exact arithmetic) | For the filer of Problem M1 (revenue 2,400,000 dollars, cost of sales 1,500,000, purchases 1,600,000, receivables 300,000, inventory 250,000, payables 200,000), what are DSO, DIO, DPO and the cycle?
   B: DSO 45.625 days (45.63 to two decimals), DIO 60.8333… days (60.83), DPO 45.625 days (45.63), and CCC 60.8333… days (60.83). The two-decimal values are the exact values rounded half up.
CF8-c009 L01 C Convention stated in Definition 1.1 and Assumption 4.0; no source is cited for it | Which basis do the day counts in this lesson use?
   B: Year-end balances against full-year flows, on a 365-day year (Definition 1.1 and Assumption 4.0). An average-balance basis or a 360-day year gives different counts.
CF8-c010 L01 C Lab 1, Part A (SIMULATED) | In Lab 1, Part A, what are DSO, DIO, DPO and CCC for SIMULATED filer S-1 in 2025?
   B: DSO 48.67, DIO 53.85, DPO 45.63 and CCC 56.89 days, computed from year-end balances and full-year flows.
CF8-c011 L01 C Misconception 2 (Section 6); Lab 1, Part D (SIMULATED) | Is DSO the number of days a customer takes to pay?
   B: No. DSO divides year-end receivables by annual revenue, so it mixes payment timing with the level and seasonality of sales. In Lab 1, Part D, the same 2025 filer has DSO 48.67 days on year-end receivables and 45.63 days on average receivables, and neither is a payment record.
CF8-c012 L01 - Misconception 1 (Section 6); Proposition 3.2 and Assumption 3.0 | Is a shorter cash conversion cycle always better for the firm?
   B: No. A shorter cycle does not by itself show that the firm is better off, because the change may cost sales or raise the price of what it buys. Proposition 3.2 gives the cash released by a shorter DSO exactly under Assumption 3.0, and it says nothing about how customers respond (Section 6).
CF8-c013 L01 - Misconception 3 (Section 6); Definition 2.1; Proposition 2.2; Remark 3.4 | Does every change in the cycle move cash one-for-one with the change in days?
   B: No. In days, a change in DSO moves the cycle by the same number of days when DIO and DPO are unchanged, whatever revenue does (Definition 2.1; Proposition 2.2). In dollars it does not: one day of DSO is worth Rev/365, so the dollar effect of the same day change moves when revenue changes (Remark 3.4).
CF8-c014 L02 - <NOBASIS> | What are trade credit and a credit policy?
   B: Trade credit is the deferral of payment for goods already delivered. A credit policy is the rule a seller applies to that deferral: the credit period N, the discount d and the early date k (Definition 1.1).
CF8-c015 L02 C Convention 1.2; no source is cited for it | What is the simple annual cost convention?
   B: The annual cost of a period of N − k days is the period cost multiplied by 365 / (N − k), with no compounding (Convention 1.2). The lesson reports the compounded figure separately for comparison.
CF8-c016 L02 T Proposition 1.3 (Section 1) | Under the early-payment terms of Definition 1.1, where the buyer pays (1 − d)S by day k or S by day N, what is the simple annual cost of giving up the discount?
   B: c(d, k, N) = d / (1 − d) × 365 / (N − k). The period cost is d/(1 − d) of the amount paid early, and it does not depend on the invoice S (Proposition 1.3).
CF8-c017 L02 T Proposition 1.5 (Section 1) | For the implied annual cost c(d, k, N), how does it change with the discount d and with the gap N − k?
   B: For fixed N − k it is strictly increasing in d on (0, 1). For fixed d it is strictly decreasing in N − k on (0, ∞) (Proposition 1.5).
CF8-c018 L02 T Proposition 2.1 (Section 2); Lab 2, Part A | When N − k is at most 365 days, is the compounded annual cost (1/(1 − d))^n − 1, with n = 365/(N − k), at least the simple annual cost?
   B: Yes. The compounded cost is at least the simple cost. The inequality is strict for every d in (0, 1) when N − k < 365, and the two are equal when N − k = 365 (Proposition 2.1).
CF8-c019 L02 C Lab 2, Part A (SIMULATED terms); Example 1.4 | What simple and compounded annual costs does the lesson give for the terms 2/10 net 30?
   B: A simple annual cost of 37.24 percent and a compounded annual cost of 44.59 percent, for a 2 percent discount over a 20-day gap (Example 1.4; Lab 2, Part A).
CF8-c020 L02 - Definition 3.1 (Section 3); Assumption 3.0 | Under the extension model of Assumption 3.0, with revenue Rev_0 on old terms N_0 and new terms N_1, and extra revenue ΔRev, what is the extra investment in receivables ΔI?
   B: ΔI = (Rev_0 + ΔRev) × N_1 / 365 − Rev_0 × N_0 / 365, the year-end receivable on the new terms less the receivable on the old terms (Definition 3.1).
CF8-c021 L02 T Proposition 3.2 (Section 3); Lab 2, Parts B and C | Under Assumption 3.0 (the extra receivable is paid at period 0 and recovered at the end of year n, the margin is received at each year end, and one rate r discounts every period), what is the break-even margin, and does it depend on the horizon n?
   B: m* = r × ΔI / ΔRev, and it does not depend on n. The net present value at margin m is −ΔI + m × ΔRev × A(r, n) + ΔI × (1 + r)^(−n), where A(r, n) is the annuity factor (Proposition 3.2).
CF8-c022 L02 T Corollary 3.3 (Section 3) | Under Assumption 3.0, when does an extension have a positive net present value?
   B: Exactly when the margin exceeds m* = r × ΔI / ΔRev. The net present value is strictly increasing in the margin, because ΔRev × A(r, n) is positive (Corollary 3.3).
CF8-c023 L02 T Proposition 3.2 (Section 3) | Under Assumption 3.0, at the break-even margin m* = r × ΔI / ΔRev, does the net present value of an extension depend on the horizon n?
   B: No. The net present value at m* is zero for every horizon n. The margin's present value is m* × ΔRev × A(r, n) = ΔI × (1 − (1 + r)^(−n)), which is ΔI less its discounted recovery, so the net present value is zero (Proposition 3.2 and its proof).
CF8-c024 L02 T Proposition 4.2 (Section 4) | Under supply-chain finance (Definition 4.1), where the financier pays (1 − δ)S on day k and the buyer pays S on day N, what is the supplier's implied simple annual cost of receiving the early payment, and is it positive?
   B: It is c(δ, k, N), the expression of Proposition 1.3 with d = δ. It is positive for every δ > 0 (Proposition 4.2).
CF8-c025 L02 T Assumption 4.2 and Remark 4.3 (Section 4) | Does supply-chain finance alone change the buyer's DPO?
   B: No, under Assumption 4.2. The buyer's trade payable stays at the invoice S until the due date N and its purchases are unchanged, so the program alone leaves the buyer's DPO unchanged. A change in DPO can come from the payable balance, from purchases, or from a change in the due date N (Remark 4.3).
CF8-c026 L02 - <NOBASIS> | What is supply-chain finance?
   B: A payment arrangement in which a financier pays the supplier, before the invoice's due date, a sum (1 − δ)S less than the invoice S. The buyer then pays the financier the full S on the original due date N. The discount δ is the financier's price for the early payment (Definition 4.1).
CF8-c027 L02 T Problem R1 (Research tier, worked solution) | If the extra receivable is never recovered and the margin is earned for n years only, is the break-even margin higher than m*?
   B: Yes. The break-even margin without recovery is ΔI / (ΔR × A(r, n)) = m* / (1 − (1 + r)^(−n)), which exceeds m* for every n and every r > 0 (Problem R1).
CF8-c028 L02 - Misconception 1 (Section 6); Example 1.4; Lab 2, Part A | Is a 2 percent early-payment discount a 2 percent cost?
   B: No. The 2 percent is the cost for the gap between the early date and the due date. For 2/10 net 30 the gap is 20 days, and the simple annual cost is 37.24 percent. The same 2 percent over a 50-day gap (2/10 net 60) costs 14.90 percent a year (Section 6).
CF8-c029 L02 - Misconception 2 (Section 6); Corollary 3.3 | Does a credit extension pay if its extra sales are large enough to cover the costs?
   B: Not by sales alone. The extra receivable on existing sales is a cost, so the test is the margin on the extra revenue against r × ΔI / ΔRev, which includes the carrying charge on the whole receivable increase. The extension has a positive net present value exactly when the margin exceeds that break-even (Corollary 3.3; Section 6).
CF8-c030 L02 - Misconception 3 (Section 6); Proposition 1.3 | Does a firm that stretches its payables pay nothing for the longer period?
   B: Not if the supplier offers an early-payment discount, because the stretch gives up that discount. For 2/10 net 30 its cost is 37.24 percent a year on the simple convention (Proposition 1.3). The lesson does not model a payables stretch beyond the discount forgone (Section 6).
CF8-c031 L02 C Lab 2, Part B (SIMULATED); Example 3.4 | For the SIMULATED extension with revenue R0 of 12,000,000 dollars, terms moving from 30 to 45 days, extra revenue of 1,500,000 dollars and rate r = 0.10, what are ΔI and m*?
   B: ΔI = 678,082.19 dollars and m* = 4.52 percent of extra revenue (Example 3.4; Lab 2, Part B).
CF8-c032 L03 - <NOBASIS> | What is a 13-week direct cash-flow forecast?
   B: A schedule, for weeks w = 1 to 13, of cash receipts and cash disbursements in integer cents, together with the opening cash C0. It is direct because it lists cash that moves in the week, and week w ends on day 7w (Definition 1.1).
CF8-c033 L03 - <NOBASIS> | What is cash before credit-line movements in week w?
   B: P_w = C_(w−1) + ν_w, where ν_w = Rec_w − Dis_w is the weekly net flow. It is the cash at week end if the credit line is neither drawn nor repaid that week (Definition 1.2).
CF8-c034 L03 T Theorem 1.3 (Section 1); Lab 1 prints True in every week | Given integer-cent draws and repayments with C_w = P_w + Dr_w − Rp_w, does the weekly cash identity C_w = C_(w−1) + Rec_w − Dis_w + Dr_w − Rp_w hold in every week, for any receipts and disbursements?
   B: Yes. The identity holds for every week from 1 to 13, and C_H = C_0 + the sum of the weekly net flows + the sum of the net draws (Theorem 1.3). It checks arithmetic only and says nothing about whether the receipts are right (Remark 1.4).
CF8-c035 L03 - <NOBASIS> | What is the credit line's rule with commitment K, opening drawn balance 0 ≤ B0 ≤ K, and minimum cash m?
   B: If P_w < m, the draw is min(m − P_w, K − B_(w−1)) and there is no repayment. If P_w > m and B_(w−1) > 0, the repayment is min(P_w − m, B_(w−1)) and there is no draw. Otherwise neither occurs. The drawn balance is B_w = B_(w−1) + Dr_w − Rp_w (Definition 2.1).
CF8-c036 L03 - <NOBASIS> | What is a capped week, and what is the shortfall in a week?
   B: A capped week is one in which the draw rule applies and m − P_w > K − B_(w−1), so the draw is limited by the commitment. The shortfall is Sh_w = max(0, m − C_w) (Definition 2.1).
CF8-c037 L03 T Theorem 2.2(a) (Section 2) | Under the credit-line rule with 0 ≤ B0 ≤ K, does the drawn balance stay between zero and the commitment in every week?
   B: Yes. In every week 0 ≤ B_w ≤ K, and the draw and repayment are nonnegative, by induction on the week (Theorem 2.2(a)).
CF8-c038 L03 T Theorem 2.2(b) (Section 2); Lab 1 shows a zero shortfall in every week | Under the credit-line rule, if week w is not capped, is ending cash at least the minimum, and when is it exactly the minimum?
   B: Yes, it is at least the minimum. If P_w is at or above the minimum, the repayment leaves C_w at or above it. If P_w is below the minimum and the week is not capped, the draw restores C_w to exactly the minimum (Theorem 2.2(b)).
CF8-c039 L03 T Theorem 2.2(c) (Section 2); Problem M3 | In a capped week, what is the availability after the draw, and what is the shortfall?
   B: Availability is zero, A_w = 0, and the shortfall is Sh_w = m − P_w − (K − B_(w−1)), which is positive (Theorem 2.2(c)).
CF8-c040 L03 C Lab 1 (SIMULATED, seed 7) | In Lab 1 (seed 7, commitment 4,000,000 cents), which weeks draw, which weeks repay, and what is the peak drawn balance?
   B: Draws in weeks 6, 7, 8, 10 and 11; repayments in weeks 9, 12 and 13; a peak drawn balance of 1,288,640 cents in week 11 (Lab 1 output).
CF8-c041 L03 - Misconception 1 (Section 4); Definition 1.1 | Is forecast cash the same as forecast profit?
   B: No. A direct forecast lists the cash that moves in each week, so an uncollected sale and an unpaid expense are excluded. A profitable period can therefore show a cash shortfall (Definition 1.1; Section 4).
CF8-c042 L03 C Misconception 2 (Section 4); Remark 2.4; Example 2.3 | Is a draw on the credit line income that raises the week's operating flow?
   B: No. A draw is not part of the weekly net flow ν_w. It raises ending cash and the drawn balance by the same amount (Remark 2.4). In Lab 1 the week 6 draw of 539,905 cents is printed in the draw column, not in receipts.
CF8-c043 L03 - Misconception 3 (Section 4); Theorem 2.2(b) and (c) | Can minimum cash be breached whenever the credit line has availability left?
   B: No. A week that is not capped holds minimum cash (Theorem 2.2(b)), and a shortfall needs a capped week, which leaves zero availability (Theorem 2.2(c)). A breach therefore needs a fully drawn line.
CF8-c044 L03 - Misconception 4 (Section 4); Remark 1.4 | Does a passing cash identity check prove that the forecast is right?
   B: No. Theorem 1.3 holds for any receipts, so the check confirms that the stated flows add up and is silent on whether the flows are correct (Remark 1.4).
CF8-c045 L04 - <NOBASIS> | What is a revolving credit facility?
   B: A committed line with commitment K in integer cents. The borrower may draw up to K in total, repay, and draw again up to the commitment. Its drawn balance is a liability, and its undrawn availability is A_w = K − B_w (Definition 1.1).
CF8-c046 L04 - <NOBASIS> | How are liquidity, headroom and the pinch week defined?
   B: Liquidity at week end is L_w = C_w + A_w, the ending cash plus the undrawn availability. Headroom is U_w = L_w − θ for a threshold θ in cents. The pinch week is the week of least headroom, and the earlier week on a tie (Definition 1.2).
CF8-c047 L04 - <NOBASIS> | What financial covenant does this lesson use?
   B: Minimum liquidity with threshold θ. The covenant is met at a week end when U_w ≥ 0, and a week with U_w < 0 is a breach. Other covenant types are not covered (Definition 1.3).
CF8-c048 L04 - <NOBASIS> | On what grid are flows and balances measured in this lesson?
   B: At week end, as in L03. A breach that begins and ends inside a week is therefore not seen, and the pinch week is the least headroom on that grid (Definition 2.6).
CF8-c049 L04 T Theorem 2.1 (Section 2) | Under the L03 flows and credit-line rule, with 0 ≤ B0 ≤ K and no interest, fee or other cash cost on the facility, does a draw or repayment change liquidity?
   B: No. Liquidity equals C_0 − B_0 + K + Cum_w, where Cum_w is the cumulative net flow through week w, so a draw or repayment changes L_w by nothing (Theorem 2.1).
CF8-c050 L04 T Corollary 2.2 (Section 2); Lab 1 prints the same week | Under the assumptions of Theorem 2.1, how does headroom depend on the cumulative net flow, and which week is the pinch week?
   B: U_w = (C_0 − B_0 + K − θ) + Cum_w, so headroom differs across weeks only through the cumulative net flow. The pinch week is the week of least cumulative net flow, and the earlier week on a tie (Corollary 2.2).
CF8-c051 L04 T Theorem 2.3 (Section 2) | Under the L03 flows and credit-line rule with 0 ≤ B0 ≤ K, what is the smallest commitment with no shortfall in any week?
   B: K* = max(B0, the maximum over weeks of B∞_w), where B∞_w is the drawn balance when the draw is not capped. The shortfall is zero in every week if and only if K ≥ K* (Theorem 2.3).
CF8-c052 L04 T Theorem 2.4 (Section 2); Example 2.5 | If weekly sales are S cents, DSO rises by d days from week 1, every receipt is a collection, and the collections given up stay uncollected through the horizon, by how much do cumulative receipts and liquidity fall, and does the pinch week change?
   B: Cumulative receipts through each week fall by exactly dS/7 cents, and liquidity falls by the same amount in every week, so the pinch week does not change (Theorem 2.4). The lab prints the amount to the nearest cent.
CF8-c053 L04 C Misconception 1 (Section 4); Theorem 2.1; Lab 1 | Does drawing on the facility improve liquidity?
   B: No. A draw raises cash and the drawn balance by the same amount, so availability falls by that amount and liquidity is unchanged (Theorem 2.1). In Lab 1 the week 6 liquidity is 1,660,095 cents even though that week's draw is 539,905 cents (Section 4).
CF8-c054 L04 C Misconception 2 (Section 4); Corollary 2.2; Example 2.3 | Is the pinch week the week with the largest outflow?
   B: No. The pinch week is the week of least cumulative net flow (Corollary 2.2). In Lab 1 the largest single-week net outflow is week 6, at −608,065 cents, but the cumulative flow is least in week 11, at −1,488,640 cents.
CF8-c055 L04 - Misconception 3 (Section 4); Theorem 2.4; Section 3 | Does a one-time DSO slip that stays uncollected move the pinch week?
   B: No. Such a slip lowers every week's liquidity by the same amount (Theorem 2.4), so the least-headroom week does not change. A phased or partly recovered slip can move the pinch week (Section 3).
CF8-c056 L04 C Misconception 4 (Section 4); Theorem 2.3; Lab 1 | Is a commitment that covers the opening drawn balance enough to avoid a shortfall?
   B: No. The smallest commitment with no shortfall is the peak of the unconstrained drawn balance, which can exceed the opening balance (Theorem 2.3). In Lab 1 a commitment of 1,200,000 cents leaves a shortfall of 88,640 cents in week 11, because the peak is 1,288,640 cents.
CF8-c057 L04 C Lab 1 (SIMULATED, seed 7) | In Lab 1 (SIMULATED, seed 7, commitment 1,200,000 cents), which week is the pinch week, what is its headroom, and what commitment avoids any shortfall?
   B: Week 11, with headroom −888,640 cents. The commitment with no shortfall is 1,288,640 cents (Lab 1 output).
CF8-c058 L05 - <NOBASIS> | In a year with monthly net flows X_1 to X_N and a cash buffer B, when does a shortfall occur?
   B: When B + S_t < 0 for some month t from 1 to N, where S_t is the cumulative net flow. This is the same event as the lowest cumulative flow M being below −B (Definition 1.1).
CF8-c059 L05 - <NOBASIS> | What is a cash buffer, and how is the cumulative net flow defined?
   B: A cash buffer B ≥ 0, in cents, is the cash the firm holds at the start of the year. The cumulative net flow is S_t = X_1 + … + X_t, with S_0 = 0, where a positive X_t is a surplus of receipts over disbursements in month t (Definition 1.1).
CF8-c060 L05 - <NOBASIS> | What is commercial paper in this lesson?
   B: A short-term debt security that a firm issues to investors for a stated maturity and repays at maturity. It is unsecured, so no specific asset is pledged. It is not a bank line such as the revolving credit facility of L04 (Definition 1.2).
CF8-c061 L05 - <NOBASIS> | What are precautionary cash holdings?
   B: Cash a firm keeps so that it can cover a net outflow whose size or timing it does not know in advance. It is a reason for holding cash, not a quantity. The lesson measures the shortfall frequency at a buffer instead (Definition 1.3).
CF8-c062 L05 - <NOBASIS> | What is the nearest-rank quantile used in this lesson?
   B: For N sorted values, the q-quantile is the value at position ceil(qN), counted from 1. For N_Y yearly minima and k = ceil(0.05 N_Y), the 5 percent quantile is the k-th smallest minimum (Remark 2.4).
CF8-c063 L05 T Proposition 2.1 (Section 2) | On a fixed set of years, with the same years used at every buffer and 0 ≤ B ≤ B′ in cents, can a larger buffer B′ add shortfall years?
   B: No. The set of years that run short at B′ is contained in the set that runs short at B, so the count of shortfall years, and therefore the shortfall frequency, cannot rise with the buffer (Proposition 2.1).
CF8-c064 L05 T Proposition 2.2 (Section 2); Problem P3 | If every monthly net flow is at least −L cents over a horizon of N months, and the buffer satisfies B ≥ NL, does any year run short?
   B: No. Each cumulative flow satisfies S_t ≥ −NL, so B + S_t ≥ 0 in every month, for any flows that meet the bound (Proposition 2.2). A buffer of NL − 1 cents does not guarantee this for the whole class (Problem P3).
CF8-c065 L05 T Remark 2.4 (Section 2), derived from the nearest-rank definition | With N_Y yearly minima, k = ceil(0.05 N_Y), and a nearest-rank 5 percent quantile q that is zero or negative, at most how many years run short at the buffer B = −q, and what share is that?
   B: At most k − 1 years, a share of at most (k − 1)/N_Y. For N_Y = 2000 and k = 100 that is 99 of 2000 years, or 4.95 percent (Remark 2.4).
CF8-c066 L05 C Misconception 1 (Section 4); Proposition 2.1; Lab 1 output | Does a bigger cash buffer always lower the shortfall frequency?
   B: No. The frequency is non-increasing in the buffer, which allows it to stay flat. In Lab 1 the shortfall count is 0 at 750000, 1000000 and 1200000 cents (Section 4).
CF8-c067 L05 - Misconception 2 (Section 4); Definition 1.2 | Is commercial paper a bank loan that the firm can draw at will?
   B: No. Commercial paper is a note that the firm sells to investors, with a stated maturity, and it is unsecured. A bank line such as the revolving credit facility of L04 is a different instrument (Definition 1.2; Section 4).

   B: No. The frequency comes from one seed and one generator. At a frequency of 0.1780 the standard error is 0.0086 under the independence formula, so the frequency describes the generator and not the firm (Section 4; Lab 1 output).
