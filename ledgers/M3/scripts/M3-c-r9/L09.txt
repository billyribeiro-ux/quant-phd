63: A matrix of pairwise correlations can fail to be a correlation matrix (Definition 1.1 of this lesson) because it can fail to be positive semidefinite (PSD, defined in Section 1), so some quadratic form is negative. The lesson defines the nearest correlation matrix in Frobenius distance (Definitions 1.2 and 2.1 of this lesson) and two repairs, eigenvalue clipping and rescaling (Section 3). Clip-and-rescale is proved to give a correlation matrix when every diagonal entry of the clipped matrix is positive. Of 40 SIMULATED seeded inputs, 23 are not PSD; in all 23, Lab 1 finds a correlation matrix within tolerance that is closer to the input than the clip-and-rescale repair.
66: 1Correlation matrices and the Frobenius norm
69: Throughout, matrices are real and square. A matrix \(A\) is symmetric when \(A^{\top} = A\), and a symmetric \(C\) is positive semidefinite, written PSD, when \(x^{\top} C x \ge 0\) for every vector \(x\) (Lesson 5). The transpose \(M^{\top}\) swaps rows and columns, and the trace \(\tr M\) is the sum of the diagonal entries of \(M\).
73: Definition 1.1 (correlation matrix)
74: A real \(n \times n\) matrix \(C\) is a correlation matrix when \(C\) is symmetric, \(C\) is positive semidefinite, and every diagonal entry satisfies \(C_{ii} = 1\).
80: Definition 1.2 (Frobenius norm)
81: For a real \(n \times n\) matrix \(M\), the Frobenius norm is \(\lVert M \rVert_F = \bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} M_{ij}^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\). The squared norm is \(\lVert M \rVert_F^{2} = \sum_{i,j} M_{ij}^{2}\). The distance between two matrices of the same size is \(\lVert A - C \rVert_F\).
87: Definition 1.2a (orthogonal matrix)
88: A real \(n \times n\) matrix \(Q\) is orthogonal when \(Q^{\top} Q = Q Q^{\top} = I\), the identity matrix.
94: Lemma 1.3 (the Frobenius norm is unchanged by an orthogonal change of basis)
95: Let \(Q\) be an orthogonal real \(n \times n\) matrix and \(M\) a real \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(\lVert Q M Q^{\top} \rVert_F = \lVert M \rVert_F\).
101: Proof
102: First, \(\lVert M \rVert_F^{2} = \tr(M^{\top} M)\). The diagonal entry \((M^{\top} M)_{ii}\) equals \(\sum_j M_{ji}^{2}\), and summing over \(i\) gives \(\sum_{i,j} M_{ji}^{2} = \lVert M \rVert_F^{2}\).
104: Second, \(\tr(XY) = \tr(YX)\) for any real \(n \times n\) matrices \(X\) and \(Y\). Indeed \(\tr(XY) = \sum_i \sum_j X_{ij} Y_{ji} = \sum_j \sum_i Y_{ji} X_{ij} = \tr(YX)\).
106: Now let \(N = Q M Q^{\top}\). Then \(N^{\top} = Q M^{\top} Q^{\top}\), and using \(Q^{\top} Q = I\),
107: \[ \lVert N \rVert_F^{2} = \tr(N^{\top} N) = \tr(Q M^{\top} Q^{\top} Q M Q^{\top}) = \tr(Q M^{\top} M Q^{\top}). \]
108: By the second fact with \(X = Q\) and \(Y = M^{\top} M Q^{\top}\), the trace equals \(\tr(M^{\top} M Q^{\top} Q) = \tr(M^{\top} M)\). So \(\lVert N \rVert_F^{2} = \lVert M \rVert_F^{2}\), and taking square roots gives the claim.
113: 2The nearest correlation matrix
116: The repair problem needs a target. Given a real symmetric matrix \(A\), the natural target is the correlation matrix closest to \(A\) in Frobenius distance.
120: Definition 2.1 (nearest correlation matrix)
121: Let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix. A nearest correlation matrix to \(A\) is a correlation matrix \(C^{\ast}\) such that \(\lVert A - C^{\ast} \rVert_F \le \lVert A - C \rVert_F\) for every correlation matrix \(C\) of the same size.
127: Remark 2.2 (what the definition does not assert)
128: Definition 2.1 says what a nearest correlation matrix is. It does not assert that one exists for every \(A\), and it does not assert that it is unique. This lesson proves neither. Method 1 in Section 4 produces a correlation matrix when every diagonal entry of the clipped matrix is positive (Propositions 3.2(a) and 3.4); the lesson does not prove that Method 2 does. The lab compares distances. If one correlation matrix is closer to \(A\) than another correlation matrix, the farther one is not nearest.
134: Example 2.3 (a symmetric unit-diagonal matrix that is not PSD)
135: Let
136: \[ C_0 = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.9 & 0.9 \\ 0.9 & 1 & -0.9 \\ 0.9 & -0.9 & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]
137: \(C_0\) is symmetric with unit diagonal. Take \(x = (1, -1, -1)^{\top}\). Then
138: \[ x^{\top} C_0 x = \sum_i C_{ii} x_i^{2} + 2 \sum_{i \lt j} C_{ij} x_i x_j = 3 + 2\bigl(0.9 \cdot (1)(-1) + 0.9 \cdot (1)(-1) + (-0.9)(-1)(-1)\bigr) = 3 + 2(-2.7) = -2.4. \]
139: Since \(x^{\top} C_0 x \lt 0\), \(C_0\) is not PSD, so it is not a correlation matrix, and it cannot be its own nearest correlation matrix in the sense of Definition 2.1.
144: 3Eigenvalue clipping and rescaling
147: Two operations repair a symmetric matrix. The first fixes the eigenvalues. The second fixes the diagonal. Both use the spectral decomposition of a symmetric matrix \(A\), which is \(A = Q \Lambda Q^{\top}\) with \(Q\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)\) real (Lesson 4). The numbers \(\lambda_i\) are the eigenvalues of \(A\) and the columns of \(Q\) are its eigenvectors (Lesson 3).
151: Definition 3.1 (eigenvalue clipping at a floor)
152: Let \(A = Q \Lambda Q^{\top}\) be a spectral decomposition of a real symmetric matrix, and let \(\varepsilon \ge 0\). The eigenvalue clipping of \(A\) at the floor \(\varepsilon\) is
153: \[ A_{\varepsilon} = Q \, \diag\bigl(\max(\lambda_1, \varepsilon), \ldots, \max(\lambda_n, \varepsilon)\bigr) \, Q^{\top}. \]
154: Clipping at \(\varepsilon = 0\) replaces each negative eigenvalue by zero and keeps the others. Fix one spectral decomposition; the results below hold for any choice.
160: Proposition 3.2 (what clipping produces)
161: Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix with spectral decomposition \(A = Q \Lambda Q^{\top}\), and let \(\varepsilon \ge 0\). Then:
163: (a) \(A_{\varepsilon}\) is symmetric and positive semidefinite, and its eigenvalues are \(\max(\lambda_i, \varepsilon)\).
165: (b) If \(\varepsilon \gt 0\), then \(x^{\top} A_{\varepsilon} x \ge \varepsilon \lVert x \rVert^{2}\) for every real vector \(x\). So \(A_{\varepsilon}\) is positive definite and therefore invertible.
171: Proof
172: Write \(\Lambda_{\varepsilon} = \diag(\max(\lambda_i, \varepsilon))\), a diagonal matrix, so \(A_{\varepsilon} = Q \Lambda_{\varepsilon} Q^{\top}\).
174: (a) Since \(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}\) is diagonal, \(\Lambda_{\varepsilon}^{\top} = \Lambda_{\varepsilon}\), and so \(A_{\varepsilon}^{\top} = Q \Lambda_{\varepsilon}^{\top} Q^{\top} = A_{\varepsilon}\). For a vector \(x\), put \(y = Q^{\top} x\). Then
175: \[ x^{\top} A_{\varepsilon} x = (Q^{\top} x)^{\top} \Lambda_{\varepsilon} (Q^{\top} x) = \sum_{i} \max(\lambda_i, \varepsilon)\, y_i^{2}. \]
176: Each \(\max(\lambda_i, \varepsilon) \ge \varepsilon \ge 0\), so the sum is nonnegative and \(A_{\varepsilon}\) is PSD. For eigenvalues, \(A_{\varepsilon} Q = Q \Lambda_{\varepsilon}\), so the \(i\)th column of \(Q\) is an eigenvector of \(A_{\varepsilon}\) with eigenvalue \(\max(\lambda_i, \varepsilon)\).
178: (b) Since \(\varepsilon \gt 0\) and \(\max(\lambda_i, \varepsilon) \ge \varepsilon\), the displayed sum is at least \(\varepsilon \sum_i y_i^{2}\). Because \(Q\) is orthogonal, \(\lVert y \rVert^{2} = x^{\top} Q Q^{\top} x = \lVert x \rVert^{2}\). So \(x^{\top} A_{\varepsilon} x \ge \varepsilon \lVert x \rVert^{2}\). If \(A_{\varepsilon} x = 0\), then \(x^{\top} A_{\varepsilon} x = 0\), which forces \(x = 0\); so the kernel is trivial and \(A_{\varepsilon}\) is invertible.
184: Proposition 3.3 (the distance that clipping moves a matrix)
185: Let \(A\), \(Q\), \(\Lambda\) and \(\varepsilon \ge 0\) be as in Proposition 3.2. Then:
187: (a) \(\lVert A - A_{\varepsilon} \rVert_F^{2} = \sum_{i \,:\, \lambda_i \lt \varepsilon} (\varepsilon - \lambda_i)^{2}\).
189: (b) \(A_{\varepsilon} = A\) if and only if \(\lambda_i \ge \varepsilon\) for every \(i\).
195: Proof
196: (a) We have \(A - A_{\varepsilon} = Q \bigl(\Lambda - \diag(\max(\lambda_i, \varepsilon))\bigr) Q^{\top}\), a conjugation of a diagonal matrix by \(Q\). The \(i\)th diagonal entry of the inner matrix is \(\lambda_i - \max(\lambda_i, \varepsilon)\). If \(\lambda_i \ge \varepsilon\), this is \(0\). If \(\lambda_i \lt \varepsilon\), it is \(\lambda_i - \varepsilon = -(\varepsilon - \lambda_i)\). Lemma 1.3 removes \(Q\) and \(Q^{\top}\), and the Frobenius norm of a diagonal matrix is the square root of the sum of its squared diagonal entries. Squaring gives (a).
198: (b) Matrices are equal exactly when their difference has norm zero. By (a), that norm is zero if and only if every term \((\varepsilon - \lambda_i)^{2}\) with \(\lambda_i \lt \varepsilon\) is zero. Each such term is strictly positive, so the norm is zero if and only if no \(\lambda_i\) lies below \(\varepsilon\).
204: Proposition 3.4 (rescaling to unit diagonal)
205: Let \(B\) be a real symmetric positive semidefinite \(n \times n\) matrix whose diagonal entries \(b_{11}, \ldots, b_{nn}\) are all positive. Let \(D = \diag(b_{11}, \ldots, b_{nn})\). Then \(C = D^{-1/2} B D^{-1/2}\) is a correlation matrix, and its entries are \(c_{ij} = b_{ij} / (\sqrt{b_{ii}}\sqrt{b_{jj}})\).
211: Proof
212: \(D^{-1/2}\) is the diagonal matrix with entries \(1/\sqrt{b_{ii}}\), which exist because the diagonal entries are positive. It is symmetric, so \(C^{\top} = D^{-1/2} B^{\top} D^{-1/2} = C\), and \(C\) is symmetric.
214: For positive semidefiniteness, let \(x\) be any vector and put \(y = D^{-1/2} x\). Then \(x^{\top} C x = (D^{-1/2} x)^{\top} B (D^{-1/2} x) = y^{\top} B y \ge 0\), because \(B\) is PSD.
216: For the diagonal, \(c_{ii} = b_{ii} / (\sqrt{b_{ii}} \sqrt{b_{ii}}) = b_{ii} / b_{ii} = 1\). So \(C\) satisfies every condition of Definition 1.1.
222: Proposition 3.5 (a zero diagonal entry forces a zero row)
223: Let \(B\) be a real symmetric positive semidefinite \(n \times n\) matrix. If \(b_{ii} = 0\) for some \(i\), then \(b_{ij} = 0\) for every \(j\).
229: Proof
230: Fix \(j \ne i\), and suppose \(b_{ij} \ne 0\). Let \(x = e_i + t e_j\) for a real \(t\), where \(e_k\) is the \(k\)th unit vector. Then
231: \[ x^{\top} B x = b_{ii} + 2 t\, b_{ij} + t^{2} b_{jj} = 2 t\, b_{ij} + t^{2} b_{jj}, \]
232: using \(b_{ii} = 0\). Put \(\delta = |b_{ij}| / (1 + b_{jj}) \gt 0\), which is defined because \(b_{jj} \ge 0\) (PSD), and let \(t = -\delta\, \mathrm{sign}(b_{ij})\). Then \(2 t\, b_{ij} = -2\delta |b_{ij}|\) and \(t^{2} b_{jj} = \delta^{2} b_{jj}\), so
233: \[ x^{\top} B x = \delta \bigl(-2|b_{ij}| + \delta\, b_{jj}\bigr). \]
234: Since \(\delta b_{jj} = |b_{ij}| \cdot b_{jj} / (1 + b_{jj}) \lt |b_{ij}|\), the bracket is less than \(-|b_{ij}| \lt 0\). So \(x^{\top} B x \lt 0\), contradicting positive semidefiniteness. Hence \(b_{ij} = 0\). For \(j = i\) the claim is \(b_{ii} = 0\), which holds by hypothesis.
240: Example 3.6 (clip and rescale a 2 by 2 matrix by hand)
241: Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Its eigenvalues are \(1 \pm 1.2\), namely \(2.2\) and \(-0.2\). Eigenvectors are \((1, 1)^{\top}\) for \(2.2\) and \((1, -1)^{\top}\) for \(-0.2\), since \(A (1, 1)^{\top} = (2.2, 2.2)^{\top}\) and \(A (1, -1)^{\top} = (-0.2, 0.2)^{\top}\). With the orthogonal matrix \(Q\) whose columns are \((1, 1)^{\top}/\sqrt{2}\) and \((1, -1)^{\top}/\sqrt{2}\), the spectral decomposition is \(A = Q\,\diag(2.2, -0.2)\,Q^{\top}\).
243: Clipping at \(\varepsilon = 0\) gives \(A_0 = Q\,\diag(2.2, 0)\,Q^{\top} = 2.2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.1 & 1.1 \\ 1.1 & 1.1 \end{pmatrix}\). Its diagonal is \(1.1\), not \(1\). Rescaling by Proposition 3.4 gives \(c_{12} = 1.1 / (\sqrt{1.1}\sqrt{1.1}) = 1\), so the repaired matrix is \(R = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\).
245: The distance check of Proposition 3.3 agrees. \(A - A_0 = \begin{pmatrix} -0.1 & 0.1 \\ 0.1 & -0.1 \end{pmatrix}\), so \(\lVert A - A_0 \rVert_F^{2} = 4 \cdot 0.01 = 0.04\). The formula gives \((0 - (-0.2))^{2} = 0.04\). The rescaled output differs from \(A\) only in its off-diagonal entries, where \(A - R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0.2 \\ 0.2 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), so \(\lVert A - R \rVert_F^{2} = 2 \cdot 0.04 = 0.08\) for this matrix.
250: 4Two repairs, and what they guarantee
253: Method 1, clip and rescale. Given a real symmetric \(A\), compute \(B = A_0\) by Definition 3.1, then \(R = D^{-1/2} B D^{-1/2}\) by Proposition 3.4, when every diagonal entry of \(B\) is positive. Under that positive-diagonal condition, Propositions 3.2(a) and 3.4 prove that \(R\) is a correlation matrix. Proposition 3.3 gives the exact squared distance \(\lVert A - B \rVert_F^{2}\) of the first step. It does not give \(\lVert A - R \rVert_F\), because the rescaling moves the matrix again, and this lesson gives no general formula for that distance. Example 3.6 computes it for this one matrix only.
256: Method 2, alternating projections. Start from \(X = A\). Repeat: eigenvalue-clip \(X\) at zero (Definition 3.1 with \(\varepsilon = 0\)) to get \(Y\), set every diagonal entry of \(Y\) to \(1\), and stop when the largest entrywise change from \(X\) to \(Y\) is below \(10^{-12}\) or after 500 steps. The output \(W\) has unit diagonal by construction. The construction does not guarantee that \(W\) is PSD, and on the seeded inputs the lab checks it only to within \(10^{-8}\). The lesson does not prove that the procedure converges, or that it reaches a nearest correlation matrix.
259: Neither method is claimed to be the nearest correlation matrix. The comparison in the next lab uses only distances, so it can show that one candidate is nearer than another.
263: Lab 1 · Clip and rescale against alternating projections
265: Stated before running: the 3x3 matrix \(C_0\) of Example 2.3 is not PSD, and the witness \(x = (1, -1, -1)\) gives \(x^{\top} C_0 x = -2.4\). Clipping \(C_0\) at zero gives a PSD matrix whose squared distance from \(C_0\) equals the sum of its squared negative eigenvalues, and rescaling that matrix gives a correlation matrix \(R\). For 40 SIMULATED seeded \(5 \times 5\) matrices, the lab counts how many alternating-projection outputs \(W\) pass its correlation check (symmetric and unit diagonal to \(10^{-12}\), smallest eigenvalue at least \(-10^{-8}\)), and how many of the non-PSD matrices have \(\lVert A - W \rVert_F\) strictly below \(\lVert A - R \rVert_F\).

308: def clip_eigenvalues(a: FloatMatrix, floor: float = 0.0) -> FloatMatrix:
309: """Return Q max(Lambda, floor) Q^T for the spectral decomposition a = Q Lambda Q^T of a symmetric matrix a."""

The clipped matrix \(B\) is PSD, and the squared distance from \(C_0\) to \(B\), \(0.6400000000\), agrees with the sum of the squared negative eigenvalues, \(0.6400000000\): this is Proposition 3.3(a) on this input. The printed \(R\) has unit diagonal and entries \(\pm 0.5\), and the check prints True. Of the 40 SIMULATED seeded matrices, 23 are not PSD. For all 23, the alternating-projection output passes the correlation check of the lab (symmetric and unit diagonal to \(10^{-12}\), smallest eigenvalue at least \(-10^{-8}\)), and it is strictly closer to the input than \(R\). The lab says nothing about the 17 PSD matrices among the 40, nothing about other seeds, and nothing about how close any repair comes to the true nearest correlation matrix, which the lab does not compute.
421: 5Where the canon breaks
424: Clipping fixes the eigenvalues, not the diagonal. Example 3.6 shows \(A_0\) with diagonal \(1.1\). Rescaling moves the eigenvalues again (Problem R2). The order also matters. For \(A\) of Example 3.6, rescaling first changes nothing, because its diagonal is already one, and clipping afterwards leaves the diagonal at \(1.1\). Clipping first and rescaling second gives \(R\), with unit diagonal.
426: The repair is not always the nearest matrix. Lab 1 gives 23 SIMULATED instances in which \(W\) passes the lab's correlation check and is strictly closer to the input than \(R\). The lab uses 40 SIMULATED seeded matrices, so it supports only that count and gives no rate for other inputs.
428: A repair can be singular. The matrix \(R\) of Example 3.6 has eigenvector \((1, -1)^{\top}\) with eigenvalue \(0\), since \(R(1,-1)^{\top} = (0,0)^{\top}\). A matrix with a nonzero vector sent to zero has no inverse, because \(M v = 0\) with \(M^{-1}\) existing would give \(v = M^{-1} 0 = 0\). A formula that needs the inverse of such a repair fails, because no inverse exists. Clipping at a positive floor avoids this (Proposition 3.2(b)), but its distance from \(A\) is not smaller than at floor zero, because each term of the sum in Proposition 3.3(a) grows with \(\varepsilon\) and more terms appear.
430: Rescaling needs a positive diagonal. Proposition 3.5 shows that a PSD matrix with a zero diagonal entry has a zero row. Then \(D^{-1/2}\) does not exist and Proposition 3.4 does not apply.
437: 6Misconceptions
441: “Setting the negative eigenvalues to zero gives a correlation matrix.”
443: The result is PSD (Proposition 3.2(a)) but its diagonal need not be one. Example 3.6 gives diagonal entries \(1.1\), so the output is not a correlation matrix until it is rescaled.
448: “Rescaling a PSD matrix to unit diagonal can make it fail to be PSD.”
450: Rescaling is a congruence \(D^{-1/2} B D^{-1/2}\) with an invertible diagonal matrix, and it preserves positive semidefiniteness (Proposition 3.4). So if \(B\) is PSD and every diagonal entry of \(B\) is positive, which is the case where rescaling is defined, the rescaled matrix is PSD.
455: “The clip-and-rescale matrix is the nearest correlation matrix to the input.”
457: Definition 2.1 asks for the minimum over all correlation matrices, which the clip-and-rescale construction does not compute. Lab 1 gives 23 non-PSD SIMULATED instances in which a matrix \(W\), which passes the lab's correlation check (symmetric, unit diagonal to \(10^{-12}\), and smallest eigenvalue at least \(-10^{-8}\)), is strictly closer to the input than \(R\). Up to that tolerance, \(R\) is not the nearest correlation matrix in those instances.

472: Mechanics
475: M1 · Is a 2 by 2 matrix a correlation matrix?
476: Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.6 \\ 0.6 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Decide whether \(A\) is a correlation matrix in the sense of Definition 1.1.
478: Solution
479: \(A\) is symmetric with unit diagonal. Its eigenvectors are \((1, 1)^{\top}\) and \((1, -1)^{\top}\), with eigenvalues \(1 + 0.6 = 1.6\) and \(1 - 0.6 = 0.4\). Both are positive, so \(A\) is PSD (Lesson 5: a symmetric matrix with nonnegative eigenvalues is PSD). Therefore \(A\) is a correlation matrix.
485: M2 · The squared Frobenius norm of a 2 by 2 matrix
486: For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1.2 \\ 1.2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) of Example 3.6, compute \(\lVert A \rVert_F^{2}\) directly from the entries.
488: Solution
489: The squared entries are \(1\), \(1.44\), \(1.44\) and \(1\). Their sum is \(4.88\), so \(\lVert A \rVert_F^{2} = 4.88\). As a check, the eigenvalues \(2.2\) and \(-0.2\) satisfy \(2.2^{2} + (-0.2)^{2} = 4.84 + 0.04 = 4.88\), as Lemma 1.3 requires.
495: M3 · Rescale a PSD matrix
496: Let \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 9 \end{pmatrix}\). Check that \(B\) is PSD and compute \(C = D^{-1/2} B D^{-1/2}\) with \(D = \diag(4, 9)\).
498: Solution
499: \(B\) is symmetric. Its eigenvalues are the roots of \(\lambda^{2} - 13\lambda + 32 = 0\) (trace \(13\), determinant \(36 - 4 = 32\)). The discriminant is \(169 - 128 = 41\), so the roots are \((13 \pm \sqrt{41})/2\). Since \(\sqrt{41} \lt 13\), both roots are positive, and \(B\) is PSD. The entries of \(C\) are \(c_{11} = 4/(\sqrt{4}\sqrt{4}) = 1\), \(c_{22} = 9/(\sqrt{9}\sqrt{9}) = 1\), and \(c_{12} = 2/(\sqrt{4}\sqrt{9}) = 2/6 = 1/3\). So \(C = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\).
508: Proof
511: P1 · Off-diagonal entries of a correlation matrix lie in [-1, 1]
512: Let \(C\) be a correlation matrix. Prove that \(|C_{ij}| \le 1\) for all \(i\) and \(j\).
514: Solution
515: The diagonal case is \(C_{ii} = 1\). For \(i \ne j\), let \(x = e_i + t e_j\) with \(t\) real. Since \(C_{ii} = C_{jj} = 1\), \(x^{\top} C x = 1 + 2t\, C_{ij} + t^{2}\), and PSD gives \(1 + 2t C_{ij} + t^{2} \ge 0\) for every \(t\). Taking \(t = -1\) gives \(2 - 2C_{ij} \ge 0\), so \(C_{ij} \le 1\). Taking \(t = 1\) gives \(2 + 2C_{ij} \ge 0\), so \(C_{ij} \ge -1\).
521: P2 · Clipping above zero gives an invertible matrix
522: Let \(A\) be real symmetric with spectral decomposition \(A = Q \Lambda Q^{\top}\), and let \(\varepsilon \gt 0\). Prove that \(A_{\varepsilon}\) is invertible, using Proposition 3.2(b) and the fact that a square matrix with trivial kernel is invertible.
524: Solution
525: Proposition 3.2(b) gives \(x^{\top} A_{\varepsilon} x \ge \varepsilon \lVert x \rVert^{2}\). Suppose \(A_{\varepsilon} x = 0\) for some \(x\). Then \(x^{\top} A_{\varepsilon} x = 0\), so \(\varepsilon \lVert x \rVert^{2} \le 0\), which forces \(x = 0\). A square matrix whose kernel is trivial is invertible.
531: P3 · The set of correlation matrices is convex
532: Let \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) be correlation matrices of the same size, and let \(s \in [0, 1]\). Prove that \(sC_1 + (1 - s) C_2\) is a correlation matrix.
534: Solution
535: The matrix is symmetric, because each term is. Its diagonal entries are \(s \cdot 1 + (1 - s) \cdot 1 = 1\). For any vector \(x\), \(x^{\top}(sC_1 + (1-s)C_2)x = s\, x^{\top} C_1 x + (1-s)\, x^{\top} C_2 x\). Both terms are nonnegative because \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) are PSD and \(s\) and \(1 - s\) are nonnegative, so the matrix is PSD.
544: Empirical
547: E1 · Check the witness in Lab 1 by hand
548: Lab 1 reports \(x^{\top} C_0 x = -2.4\) for \(x = (1, -1, -1)\). Recompute this value term by term, and state what it shows about \(C_0\).
550: Solution
551: The diagonal terms contribute \(1 + 1 + 1 = 3\). The off-diagonal pairs \((1,2)\), \((1,3)\) and \((2,3)\) contribute \(2 \cdot 0.9 \cdot (1)(-1) = -1.8\), \(2 \cdot 0.9 \cdot (1)(-1) = -1.8\) and \(2 \cdot (-0.9)(-1)(-1) = -1.8\). The total is \(3 - 5.4 = -2.4\), which matches the lab. A negative value of the quadratic form means \(C_0\) is not PSD.
557: E2 · The distance from C0 to the clip-and-rescale matrix
558: The lab's rescaled matrix \(R\) for \(C_0\) has off-diagonal entries \(0.5, 0.5, -0.5\) and unit diagonal. Compute \(\lVert C_0 - R \rVert_F\) exactly from the entries, and give it to four decimals.
560: Solution
561: \(C_0 - R\) has entries \(0.4\), \(0.4\) and \(-0.4\) in the three off-diagonal pairs, and zeros on the diagonal. Each pair appears twice in the matrix, so the squared norm is \(6 \cdot 0.16 = 0.96\). Then \(\lVert C_0 - R \rVert_F = \sqrt{0.96} = 0.9798\) to four decimals.
567: E3 · Distance from clipping a known spectrum
568: A real symmetric \(3 \times 3\) matrix \(A\) has eigenvalues \(2\), \(0.5\) and \(-0.5\). Without computing \(A\) itself, find \(\lVert A - A_0 \rVert_F\) and the eigenvalues of \(A_0\).
570: Solution
571: By Proposition 3.2(a), the eigenvalues of \(A_0\) are \(\max(2, 0) = 2\), \(\max(0.5, 0) = 0.5\) and \(\max(-0.5, 0) = 0\). By Proposition 3.3(a), \(\lVert A - A_0 \rVert_F^{2} = (0 - (-0.5))^{2} = 0.25\), so \(\lVert A - A_0 \rVert_F = 0.5\).
580: Research
583: R1 · Split of the squared norm at zero
584: Let \(A = Q \Lambda Q^{\top}\) be real symmetric, and write \(\Lambda_{+} = \diag(\max(\lambda_i, 0))\) and \(\Lambda_{-} = \diag(\min(\lambda_i, 0))\). Put \(A_{+} = Q \Lambda_{+} Q^{\top}\) and \(A_{-} = Q \Lambda_{-} Q^{\top}\). Prove that \(A = A_{+} + A_{-}\), that \(A - A_0 = A_{-}\), and that \(\lVert A \rVert_F^{2} = \lVert A_{+} \rVert_F^{2} + \lVert A_{-} \rVert_F^{2}\).
586: Solution
587: For each \(i\), \(\max(\lambda_i, 0) + \min(\lambda_i, 0) = \lambda_i\), so \(\Lambda_{+} + \Lambda_{-} = \Lambda\) and \(A_{+} + A_{-} = A\). Since \(A_0 = A_{+}\) (Definition 3.1 with \(\varepsilon = 0\)), \(A - A_0 = A_{-}\). For the norms, Lemma 1.3 gives \(\lVert A_{\pm} \rVert_F^{2} = \sum_i \max(\lambda_i, 0)^{2}\) and \(\sum_i \min(\lambda_i, 0)^{2}\) respectively, and \(\lambda_i^{2} = \max(\lambda_i, 0)^{2} + \min(\lambda_i, 0)^{2}\) holds because one of the two terms is \(0\). Summing over \(i\) gives \(\lVert A \rVert_F^{2} = \sum_i \lambda_i^{2}\), which equals the sum of the two norms. The identity \(\lVert A - A_0 \rVert_F = \lVert A_{-} \rVert_F\) reproduces Proposition 3.3(a) at \(\varepsilon = 0\).
593: R2 · Rescaling changes the eigenvalues
594: Let \(B = \diag(1, 4)\). Show that \(B\) is PSD with positive diagonal, compute its rescaling \(C\) of Proposition 3.4, and compare the eigenvalues of \(B\) and \(C\).
596: Solution
597: \(B\) is diagonal with nonnegative entries, so it is PSD, and its diagonal entries \(1\) and \(4\) are positive. Here \(D = B\), so \(D^{-1/2} = \diag(1, 1/2)\) and \(C = D^{-1/2} B D^{-1/2} = \diag(1, 1)\), the identity. The eigenvalues of \(B\) are \(\{1, 4\}\) and those of \(C\) are \(\{1, 1\}\). The eigenvalues differ, so rescaling can change the eigenvalues.
603: R3 · The norm of a correlation matrix
604: Let \(C\) be an \(n \times n\) correlation matrix. Show that \(\lVert C \rVert_F^{2} = n + \sum_{i \ne j} C_{ij}^{2}\), and conclude that \(\lVert C \rVert_F^{2} \ge n\), with equality only when \(C = I\).
606: Solution
607: Split the sum in Definition 1.2 into diagonal entries and off-diagonal entries. The diagonal entries are all \(1\), contributing \(n\), and the off-diagonal entries contribute \(\sum_{i \ne j} C_{ij}^{2}\). This is a sum of nonnegative terms, so the norm is at least \(n\). Equality holds exactly when every off-diagonal entry is \(0\), that is, when \(C = I\).
