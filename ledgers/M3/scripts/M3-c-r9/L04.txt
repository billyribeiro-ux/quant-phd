62: For every real \(m \times n\) matrix \(B\), the matrix \(B^{\top}B\) is symmetric (Definition 1.1 below), so the spectral theorem (Theorem 2.3, stated below) applies to it. Under Assumptions F1 to F4 (§1), for a symmetric matrix the eigenvectors can be chosen perpendicular. Lesson 8 applies it to a symmetric matrix of centred data, and under Assumptions F1 to F4 the eigenvectors it uses are perpendicular; Lesson 5 links the sign of \(x^{\top}Ax\) to the eigenvalues. Its hypothesis is symmetry, and Remark 2.7 gives a nonsymmetric matrix whose eigenvectors are not orthogonal. This lesson proves the theorem from four named facts, shows the orthogonality of eigenvectors for different eigenvalues, and checks the consequences on seeded random matrices in Lab 1.
65: 1Symmetric matrices, orthogonality and orthogonal matrices
69: Assumptions F1 to F4 (assumed here, not proved)
70: The unit takes the four facts below as given. They are standard results of linear algebra and complex arithmetic, this lesson does not prove them, and no passage locator is cited for them. A result that uses one of them says so in its statement, as "Assuming F1" or "Assuming F1 to F4".
73: F1 (invertibility). For a square matrix \(M\), real or complex, \(M\) is invertible if and only if \(\det M \ne 0\), and if and only if \(Mx = 0\) has only the solution \(x = 0\). If \(MN = I\) or \(NM = I\) for square matrices \(M\) and \(N\) of the same size, then \(M\) is invertible and \(M^{-1} = N\). Lesson 3 states this fact for real matrices; here it is also assumed for complex matrices.
75: F2 (fundamental theorem of algebra). Every polynomial of degree at least 1 with complex coefficients has a complex root. For a real \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) with \(n \ge 1\), the function \(\lambda \mapsto \det(A - \lambda I)\) is a polynomial in \(\lambda\) of degree \(n\), so it has a complex zero.
77: F3 (orthonormal bases). Every subspace of \(\R^n\) has an orthonormal basis (the term is defined in Definition 1.2 below), by the Gram–Schmidt procedure.
79: F4 (rank–nullity). For a real \(m \times n\) matrix \(M\), the null space \(\{x : Mx = 0\}\) has dimension \(n - \operatorname{rank} M\), where the rank is the dimension of the span of the columns. A nonzero \(1 \times n\) matrix has rank 1.
82: Complex numbers are used with the usual conjugation \(\bar z\), and for \(z \in \mathbb{C}^n\) the quantity \(z^{*}z = \sum_i |z_i|^2\) is a positive real number when \(z \ne 0\).
88: Definition 1.1 (transpose and symmetry)
89: For a real matrix \(M\), the transpose \(M^{\top}\) has \((i,j)\) entry equal to the \((j,i)\) entry of \(M\). A real square matrix \(A\) is symmetric when \(A^{\top} = A\).
95: Definition 1.2 (inner product, norm, orthogonality)
96: For \(x, y \in \R^n\), the inner product is \(\langle x, y\rangle = x^{\top}y = \sum_{i} x_i y_i\), and the norm is \(\|x\| = \sqrt{\langle x, x\rangle}\). Vectors \(x\) and \(y\) are orthogonal when \(\langle x, y \rangle = 0\). A set of vectors is orthonormal when each has norm 1 and any two distinct members are orthogonal. A real square matrix \(Q\) is orthogonal when its columns are orthonormal. The \((i,j)\) entry of \(Q^{\top}Q\) is the inner product of columns \(i\) and \(j\) of \(Q\), so \(Q\) is orthogonal exactly when \(Q^{\top}Q = I\).
102: Lemma 1.3 (orthonormal vectors are independent)
103: If \(u_1, \dots, u_k \in \R^n\) are orthonormal, then they are linearly independent. The hypothesis is orthonormality alone.
109: Proof
110: Suppose \(\sum_j c_j u_j = 0\). Taking the inner product with \(u_i\) gives \(0 = \sum_j c_j \langle u_i, u_j\rangle = c_i\), because \(\langle u_i, u_j\rangle\) is 1 when \(j = i\) and 0 otherwise. So every \(c_i = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
116: Lemma 1.4 (orthogonal matrices preserve length)
117: Assuming F1, let \(Q\) be a real orthogonal \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(Q\) is invertible with \(Q^{-1} = Q^{\top}\), and \(\|Qx\| = \|x\|\) for every \(x \in \R^n\). The hypotheses are: \(Q\) real, square and orthogonal, and Assumption F1.
123: Proof
124: Since \(Q^{\top}Q = I\), the matrix \(Q^{\top}\) is a one-sided inverse of \(Q\), so by F1 \(Q\) is invertible with \(Q^{-1} = Q^{\top}\). Also \(\|Qx\|^2 = x^{\top}Q^{\top}Qx = x^{\top}x = \|x\|^2\), and both norms are nonnegative, so \(\|Qx\| = \|x\|\). \(\blacksquare\)
130: Remark 1.5 (a symmetric matrix and a nonsymmetric one)
131: The matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) of Lesson 3, Example 1.6, is symmetric, because its \((1,2)\) and \((2,1)\) entries are both 1. The matrix \(N = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) is not symmetric, because its \((1,2)\) entry is 2 and its \((2,1)\) entry is 0. The rest of this lesson turns on that difference.
137: Remark 1.6 (a rotation has no real eigenvalue)
138: The rotation \(R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\) is not symmetric, because its \((1,2)\) entry is \(-1\) and its \((2,1)\) entry is \(1\). Its characteristic polynomial is \(\det(R - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 1\), which has no real zero. So \(R\) has no real eigenvalue. Its eigenvalues \(\pm i\) both have imaginary part of size 1, as Lab 1 prints.
143: 2Real eigenvalues and the orthonormal eigenbasis
147: Lemma 2.1 (eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are real)
148: Let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix. If \(\lambda \in \mathbb{C}\) and \(z \in \mathbb{C}^n\), \(z \ne 0\), satisfy \(Az = \lambda z\), then \(\lambda\) is real. The hypotheses are: \(A\) real and symmetric, the complex number \(\lambda\), the nonzero complex vector \(z\), and the complex arithmetic stated in §1.
154: Proof
155: Let \(z^{*} = \bar z^{\top}\). Then \(z^{*}Az = \lambda z^{*}z\), and \(z^{*}z\) is a positive real number. Let \(s = z^{*}Az = \sum_{i,j} \bar z_i A_{ij} z_j\). Its complex conjugate is \(\bar s = \sum_{i,j} z_i A_{ij} \bar z_j\), because \(A\) is real. Exchanging the names of the indices \(i\) and \(j\) gives \(\bar s = \sum_{i,j} z_j A_{ji} \bar z_i\), and \(A_{ji} = A_{ij}\) because \(A\) is symmetric. So \(\bar s = s\), and \(s\) is real. Then \(\lambda = s / (z^{*}z)\) is a quotient of real numbers, hence real. \(\blacksquare\)
161: Lemma 2.2 (a real unit eigenvector exists)
162: Assuming F1 and F2, let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix with \(n \ge 1\). Then there are a real number \(\lambda\) and a real vector \(q\) with \(\|q\| = 1\) and \(Aq = \lambda q\). The hypotheses are: \(A\) real and symmetric, and F1 and F2.
168: Proof
169: By F2, \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\) for some complex \(\lambda\). By F1 for complex matrices, \(A - \lambda I\) has a nonzero complex null vector \(z\), so \(Az = \lambda z\). By Lemma 2.1, \(\lambda\) is real. Write \(z = x + iy\) with \(x, y\) real. Since \(A\) and \(\lambda\) are real, comparing real and imaginary parts of \(Az = \lambda z\) gives \(Ax = \lambda x\) and \(Ay = \lambda y\). As \(z \ne 0\), at least one of \(x\) and \(y\) is nonzero, and that vector is a real eigenvector. Dividing it by its norm gives the unit vector \(q\). \(\blacksquare\)
175: Theorem 2.3 (the spectral theorem for symmetric matrices)
176: Assuming F1 to F4, let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(\R^n\) has an orthonormal basis \(q_1, \dots, q_n\) with \(Aq_j = \lambda_j q_j\) for real numbers \(\lambda_j\). Consequently, if \(Q = [q_1 \; \cdots \; q_n]\) and \(\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)\), then \(Q\) is orthogonal and \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\). The hypotheses are: \(A\) real and symmetric, F1 to F4, and the inner product of Definition 1.2.
182: Proof
183: We argue by induction on \(n\). If \(n = 1\), take \(q_1 = 1\), which is a unit eigenvector of the \(1 \times 1\) matrix \(A\).
185: Let \(n \ge 2\), and assume the statement for every real symmetric \((n-1) \times (n-1)\) matrix. Lemma 2.2 gives a unit vector \(q\) and a real \(\lambda\) with \(Aq = \lambda q\). Let \(W = \{w \in \R^n : \langle q, w\rangle = 0\}\). This is the null space of the nonzero \(1 \times n\) matrix \(q^{\top}\), so by F4 it has dimension \(n - 1\).
187: \(W\) is carried into itself by \(A\). Indeed, for \(w \in W\), \[\langle q, Aw\rangle = q^{\top}Aw = (A^{\top}q)^{\top}w = (Aq)^{\top}w = \lambda \langle q, w\rangle = 0\], so \(Aw \in W\). By F3 choose an orthonormal basis \(b_1, \dots, b_{n-1}\) of \(W\), and let \(S\) be the \((n-1) \times (n-1)\) matrix with \(S_{ij} = \langle b_i, Ab_j \rangle\). Then \(S\) is symmetric: \[S_{ji} = \langle b_j, Ab_i\rangle = \langle Ab_j, b_i\rangle = \langle b_i, Ab_j\rangle = S_{ij}\], using \(A^{\top} = A\). The induction hypothesis gives orthonormal eigenvectors \(s_1, \dots, s_{n-1}\) of \(S\), with \(Ss_j = \mu_j s_j\).
189: Put \(u_j = \sum_i (s_j)_i b_i \in W\). Because the \(b_i\) are orthonormal, \(\langle u_j, u_k\rangle = s_j^{\top}s_k\), so the \(u_j\) are orthonormal. Since \(Ab_i \in W\), it equals \(\sum_k S_{ki} b_k\), and therefore \(Au_j = \sum_k (Ss_j)_k b_k = \mu_j u_j\). So each \(u_j\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) with a real eigenvalue \(\mu_j\), and \(u_j\) lies in \(W\), so \(\langle q, u_j\rangle = 0\).
191: The \(n\) vectors \(q, u_1, \dots, u_{n-1}\) are orthonormal. By Lemma 1.3 they are independent, and \(n\) independent vectors in the \(n\)-dimensional space \(\R^n\) form a basis. This is the required orthonormal eigenbasis.
193: For the consequence, the matrix \(Q\) has orthonormal columns, so it is orthogonal. The equations \(Aq_j = \lambda_j q_j\) say \(AQ = Q\Lambda\), and by Lemma 1.4 \(Q^{-1} = Q^{\top}\), so \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\). \(\blacksquare\)
199: Proposition 2.4 (eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal)
200: Let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix, and let \(v\) and \(w\) be eigenvectors of \(A\) for real eigenvalues \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) with \(\lambda \ne \mu\). Then \(\langle v, w\rangle = 0\). The hypotheses are: \(A\) real and symmetric, and \(\lambda \ne \mu\).
206: Proof
207: Symmetry gives \(\langle Av, w\rangle = v^{\top}A^{\top}w = v^{\top}Aw = \langle v, Aw\rangle\). The left side is \(\lambda \langle v, w\rangle\) and the right side is \(\mu \langle v, w\rangle\). Hence \((\lambda - \mu)\langle v, w\rangle = 0\), and since \(\lambda \ne \mu\), \(\langle v, w\rangle = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
213: Corollary 2.5 (\(x^{\top}Ax\) in eigen-coordinates)
214: Assuming F1 to F4, let \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) be as in Theorem 2.3, and for \(x \in \R^n\) let \(y = Q^{\top}x\). Then \(x^{\top}Ax = \sum_j \lambda_j y_j^2\). The hypotheses are those of Theorem 2.3.
220: Proof
221: \[x^{\top}Ax = x^{\top}Q\Lambda Q^{\top}x = (Q^{\top}x)^{\top}\Lambda (Q^{\top}x) = y^{\top}\Lambda y = \sum_j \lambda_j y_j^2\]. \(\blacksquare\)
227: Example 2.6 (a worked decomposition)
228: Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). The vector \((1,1)\) is an eigenvector for 3, and \((1,-1)\) is an eigenvector for 1, since \(A(1,1) = (3,3)\) and \(A(1,-1) = (1,-1)\). Normalising gives \(Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\), which is orthogonal, and \(\Lambda = \diag(3, 1)\). Then \[Q\Lambda Q^{\top} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 3 + 1 & 3 - 1 \\ 3 - 1 & 3 + 1 \end{pmatrix} = A\]. For \(x = (1, 0)\), \(y = Q^{\top}x = (1/\sqrt{2}, 1/\sqrt{2})\), and under Assumptions F1 to F4, Corollary 2.5 gives \(3 \cdot \tfrac12 + 1 \cdot \tfrac12 = 2\), which is \(x^{\top}Ax\).
234: Remark 2.7 (symmetry is needed)
235: For \(N = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\det(N - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda)\), so the eigenvalues are 1 and 3. The eigenspace for 1 is the line through \((1,0)\), and the eigenspace for 3 is the line through \((1,1)\), since \(N(1,1) = (3,3)\). These are the whole eigenspaces: \(N - I\) has both rows equal to \((0, 2)\), so its null space is the line through \((1,0)\), and \(N - 3I\) has rows \((-2, 2)\) and \((0, 0)\), so its null space is the line through \((1,1)\). An orthonormal eigenbasis of \(\R^2\) would consist of two orthonormal eigenvectors, each on one of these lines. Two orthonormal vectors cannot lie on the same line, because two unit vectors on one line are \(\pm\) each other and have inner product \(\pm 1 \ne 0\). So the pair would need one unit vector on each line. Those lines are not perpendicular: \(\langle (1,0), (1,1)\rangle = 1 \ne 0\), and the unit vectors on them have inner product \(\pm 1/\sqrt{2} \ne 0\), as computed next. So \(N\) has no orthonormal eigenbasis. Normalising the two eigenvectors gives inner product \(1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.7071\) in absolute value, because their norms are 1 and \(\sqrt{2}\). Lab 1 prints this value.

339: 3Where the canon breaks
341: Four limits apply to Theorem 2.3.
344: Symmetry is the hypothesis. Remark 2.7 gives a real matrix whose eigenvectors are not orthogonal, and Remark 1.6 gives a real matrix with no real eigenvalue. Neither is symmetric, and the theorem says nothing about either.
346: The eigenbasis is not unique. Each unit eigenvector can be replaced by its negative. For a repeated eigenvalue, any orthonormal basis of the eigenspace serves. For the identity \(I_2\), the vectors \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)/\sqrt{2}\) are not orthogonal, but \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\) are, and both pairs are eigenvectors.
348: The facts F1 to F4 are assumed. The proof is complete from them, but it does not prove the fundamental theorem of algebra, Gram–Schmidt or rank–nullity. A reader who wants those proofs must go to a source outside this lesson.
350: Floating-point solvers approximate the theorem. A solver returns a \(Q\) whose orthogonality holds up to a rounding error, so Lab 1 tests it with the tolerance \(10^{-10}\).
357: 4Misconceptions
361: “Every real matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis.”
363: The matrix \(N\) of Remark 2.7 has eigenspaces that are the lines through \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)\). Those lines are not perpendicular, so \(N\) has no orthonormal eigenbasis, although it has an eigenbasis. The rotation of Remark 1.6 has no real eigenbasis at all. Theorem 2.3 requires a symmetric matrix.
368: “A symmetric matrix is diagonal.”
370: The matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\) is symmetric and has nonzero off-diagonal entries. Theorem 2.3 does not make it diagonal. Under Assumptions F1 to F4, it writes \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\), with \(\Lambda\) diagonal and \(Q\) orthogonal, so the diagonal form is reached by a change of orthonormal coordinates.
375: “Any two eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are orthogonal.”
377: Proposition 2.4 applies to eigenvectors for different eigenvalues. For the identity \(I_2\), the vectors \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)\) are both eigenvectors for 1, and \(\langle (1,0), (1,1)\rangle = 1 \ne 0\). Within one eigenspace the choice of eigenvectors is free, and under Assumptions F1 to F4 the theorem chooses an orthonormal one.

395: M1 · Orthogonality of the two eigenvectors
396: For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\), the vectors \(v = (1, (-1 + \sqrt{5})/2)\) and \(w = (1, (-1 - \sqrt{5})/2)\) are eigenvectors for two different eigenvalues. Show that \(\langle v, w\rangle = 0\).
398: Solution
399: \[\langle v, w\rangle = 1 + \frac{(-1 + \sqrt{5})(-1 - \sqrt{5})}{4} = 1 + \frac{1 - 5}{4} = 1 - 1 = 0\]. Proposition 2.4 predicts this, because \(A\) is symmetric and the two eigenvalues \((7 \pm \sqrt{5})/2\) differ. Normalising \(v\) and \(w\) therefore gives the columns of an orthogonal matrix.
405: M2 · Factoring with an orthogonal matrix
406: Let \(Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\Lambda = \diag(5, -1)\). Show that \(Q\) is orthogonal, and compute \(Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\).
408: Solution
409: The columns \((1,1)/\sqrt{2}\) and \((1,-1)/\sqrt{2}\) have norm 1 and inner product \((1 - 1)/2 = 0\), so \(Q^{\top}Q = I\) and \(Q\) is orthogonal. Then \[Q\Lambda Q^{\top} = 5 \cdot \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} + (-1) \cdot \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \tfrac12 \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 6 \\ 6 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\].
415: M3 · \(x^{\top}Ax\) in eigen-coordinates
416: For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(x = (1, -2)\), compute \(x^{\top}Ax\) directly, and then compute it with Corollary 2.5 using the factorisation of Example 2.6.
418: Solution
419: Directly, \(x^{\top}Ax = 2 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 1 \cdot (-2) + 2 \cdot 4 = 2 - 4 + 8 = 6\). For the factorisation, \(y = Q^{\top}x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1 - 2, 1 + 2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-1, 3)\), so \(y_1^2 = 1/2\) and \(y_2^2 = 9/2\). Under Assumptions F1 to F4, Corollary 2.5 gives \(3 \cdot \tfrac12 + 1 \cdot \tfrac92 = 6\), which agrees.

431: P1 · Products of orthogonal matrices
432: Let \(Q_1\) and \(Q_2\) be orthogonal \(n \times n\) matrices. Prove that \(Q_1Q_2\) is orthogonal.
434: Solution
435: \((Q_1Q_2)^{\top}(Q_1Q_2) = Q_2^{\top}Q_1^{\top}Q_1Q_2\). Since \(Q_1^{\top}Q_1 = I\), this equals \(Q_2^{\top}Q_2\), which is \(I\) because \(Q_2\) is orthogonal. So \(Q_1Q_2\) is orthogonal by Definition 1.2.
441: P2 · The inverse of a symmetric matrix
442: Let \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) be invertible, as in Theorem 2.3. Prove that every \(\lambda_j \ne 0\) and that \(A^{-1} = Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^{\top}\), where \(\Lambda^{-1} = \diag(1/\lambda_1, \dots, 1/\lambda_n)\).
444: Solution
445: Under Assumptions F1 to F4, if \(\lambda_j = 0\), then \(Aq_j = 0\) with \(q_j \ne 0\), which contradicts the invertibility of \(A\) by F1. So every \(\lambda_j \ne 0\), and \(\Lambda^{-1}\) exists. Then \[(Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^{\top})(Q\Lambda Q^{\top}) = Q\Lambda^{-1}\Lambda Q^{\top} = QQ^{\top} = I\], using Lemma 1.4 for \(QQ^{\top} = I\). By F1 the product \(Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^{\top}\) is \(A^{-1}\).
451: P3 · Eigenvalues of a symmetric idempotent
452: Let \(A\) be real and symmetric with \(A^2 = A\). Prove that every real eigenvalue of \(A\) is 0 or 1.
454: Solution
455: Let \(Av = \lambda v\) with \(v \ne 0\). Then \(A^2v = A(\lambda v) = \lambda^2 v\). But \(A^2 = A\) gives \(A^2v = Av = \lambda v\). So \((\lambda^2 - \lambda)v = 0\), and since \(v \ne 0\), \(\lambda^2 = \lambda\). Hence \(\lambda(\lambda - 1) = 0\), and \(\lambda\) is 0 or 1. Symmetry was not needed for this conclusion.
464: Empirical
467: E1 · What the 200 of 200 counts establish (formula input)
468: Lab 1 prints "Q^T Q equals I to 1e-10 (orthogonal eigenvector matrix): 200 of 200". State exactly what this line establishes and what it does not.
470: Solution
471: The line says that for each of the 200 seeded matrices, the matrix returned by the solver satisfies \(Q^{\top}Q = I\) to the tolerance \(10^{-10}\). It establishes that on this sample the solver's output is orthogonal to that tolerance. It does not establish Theorem 2.3, which asserts orthogonality for every real symmetric matrix, and it says nothing about matrices outside the 200 seeded samples.
477: E2 · Counting the pairs (formula input)
478: Lab 1 prints 1200 pairs. Explain this number from the design of the lab, and say what it means that the orthogonality count for pairs is 1200 of 1200.
480: Solution
481: Each sample is \(4 \times 4\), so it has \(\binom{4}{2} = 6\) pairs of eigenvalue indices. Over 200 matrices that gives \(200 \times 6 = 1200\) pairs. The lab counts a pair only when its eigenvalues are more than \(10^{-6}\) apart, so the printed 1200 means that no pair was skipped. The count 1200 of 1200 says that all of those pairs are orthogonal to the tolerance \(10^{-10}\).
487: E3 · The shear's inner product by hand (formula input)
488: Lab 1 prints 0.7071 for the absolute inner product of the normalised eigenvectors of \(N = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Compute this value from Remark 2.7.
490: Solution
491: The normalised eigenvectors are \((1,0)\) and \((1,1)/\sqrt{2}\). Their inner product is \(1/\sqrt{2}\), which is 0.70710678 to eight decimals and rounds to 0.7071 at four decimals, matching the printed value. The value is not zero, so the eigenvectors are not orthogonal.
500: Research
503: R1 · Where symmetry is used in Lemma 2.1
504: Find the step in the proof of Lemma 2.1 where \(A^{\top} = A\) is used, and show that the conclusion fails for \(R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\).
506: Solution
507: Symmetry is used in the line \(A_{ji} = A_{ij}\), which makes \(\bar s = s\). For \(R\), the vector \(z = (1, -i)\) satisfies \(Rz = (i, 1) = i(1, -i) = iz\), so \(\lambda = i\) is not real. The computation \(z^{*}Rz = i \, z^{*}z = 2i\) is not real, which matches the failure of the symmetry step: \(R_{12} = -1 \ne 1 = R_{21}\).
513: R2 · The largest eigenvalue as a maximum
514: Let \(A\) be real symmetric with eigenvalues \(\lambda_j\) listed so that \(\lambda_1\) is the largest. Prove that \(\max\{x^{\top}Ax : \|x\| = 1\} = \lambda_1\), and that the maximum is attained at the eigenvector \(q_1\).
516: Solution
517: Under Assumptions F1 to F4, let \(\|x\| = 1\) and \(y = Q^{\top}x\). Then \(\|y\|^2 = x^{\top}QQ^{\top}x = x^{\top}x = 1\), using \(QQ^{\top} = I\) from Lemma 1.4, so \(\sum_j y_j^2 = 1\). Under Assumptions F1 to F4, Corollary 2.5 gives \(x^{\top}Ax = \sum_j \lambda_j y_j^2 \le \lambda_1 \sum_j y_j^2 = \lambda_1\). For \(x = q_1\), \(y\) is the first standard basis vector \(e_1\), and the value is \(\lambda_1\). So the maximum is \(\lambda_1\), attained at \(q_1\).
523: R3 · Shifting the spectrum
524: Assuming F1 of Lesson 4, for a real symmetric \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) and a real number \(\mu\), show that \(A - \mu I\) has the same eigenvectors as \(A\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_j - \mu\).
526: Solution
527: Since \(QQ^{\top} = I\) (Lemma 1.4, under Assumption F1), \(A - \mu I = Q\Lambda Q^{\top} - \mu QQ^{\top} = Q(\Lambda - \mu I)Q^{\top}\). Hence \((A - \mu I)q_j = Q(\Lambda - \mu I)e_j = (\lambda_j - \mu)q_j\). The columns \(q_j\) are the same eigenvectors, and the eigenvalues are shifted by \(\mu\).
