62: A matrix sends each vector to another vector. Some vectors are sent to a multiple of themselves. This lesson defines those vectors and their multiples, and under Assumption F1 proves the test that finds them and shows when a basis of them gives a factorisation of the matrix into three steps: change coordinates, scale each axis, change back. Lesson 4 uses the eigendecomposition defined here. Lab 1 checks the identities on the fixed matrices in its code.
65: 1Eigenvalues and eigenvectors
69: Assumption F1 (invertibility; assumed here, not proved)
70: For a real square matrix \(M\), let \(\det M\) be its determinant, whose definition the unit takes as given. Then \(M\) is invertible if and only if \(\det M \ne 0\), and \(M\) is invertible if and only if \(Mx = 0\) has only the solution \(x = 0\). If \(MN = I\) or \(NM = I\) for square matrices \(M\) and \(N\), then \(M\) is invertible and \(M^{-1} = N\). The unit takes F1 as given: this lesson does not prove it, and no passage locator is cited for it. A result that uses F1 says so in its statement, as "Assuming F1", and its grade holds only under that assumption.
76: Definition 1.1 (eigenvalue and eigenvector)
77: Let \(A\) be a real \(n \times n\) matrix. A real number \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) when some nonzero vector \(v \in \R^n\) satisfies \(Av = \lambda v\). Such a \(v\) is an eigenvector of \(A\) for \(\lambda\). The eigenvector is nonzero by definition, and the zero vector is never an eigenvector.
83: Definition 1.2 (eigenspace)
84: For a real \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) and a real number \(\lambda\), the eigenspace of \(A\) for \(\lambda\) is \(E_\lambda = \{v \in \R^n : Av = \lambda v\}\). It contains the zero vector, and its nonzero elements are the eigenvectors for \(\lambda\).
90: Proposition 1.3 (eigenspaces are subspaces)
91: Let \(A\) be a real \(n \times n\) matrix and \(\lambda\) a real number. Then \(E_\lambda\) is a subspace of \(\R^n\).
97: Proof
98: \(A0 = 0 = \lambda 0\), so \(0 \in E_\lambda\). Let \(v, w \in E_\lambda\) and let \(c\) be real. Matrix multiplication satisfies \(A(x + y) = Ax + Ay\) and \(A(cx) = c\,Ax\) for all vectors \(x, y\) in \(\R^n\) and real \(c\), so \(A(v + cw) = Av + cAw = \lambda v + c\lambda w = \lambda (v + cw)\). Hence \(v + cw \in E_\lambda\), and \(E_\lambda\) is a subspace. \(\blacksquare\)
104: Proposition 1.4 (the characteristic-polynomial test)
105: Assuming F1, let \(A\) be a real \(n \times n\) matrix and \(\lambda\) a real number. Then \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\) if and only if \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\). The hypotheses are: \(A\) real and square, \(\lambda\) real, and F1.
111: Proof
112: By Definition 1.1, \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue exactly when some \(v \ne 0\) satisfies \(Av = \lambda v\). That equation is \((A - \lambda I)v = 0\). So \(\lambda\) is an eigenvalue exactly when \((A - \lambda I)x = 0\) has a nonzero solution. By F1 this holds exactly when \(A - \lambda I\) is not invertible, and by F1 again exactly when \(\det(A - \lambda I) = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
118: Definition 1.5 (characteristic polynomial)
119: The characteristic polynomial of a real square matrix \(A\) is the function \(p_A(\lambda) = \det(A - \lambda I)\) of the real variable \(\lambda\). Assuming F1, by Proposition 1.4, the real eigenvalues of \(A\) are exactly the real zeros of \(p_A\).
125: Example 1.6 (a symmetric \(2 \times 2\) matrix)
126: Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(p_A(\lambda) = (4 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 1 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 11\). The discriminant is \(49 - 44 = 5 > 0\), so the zeros are \(\lambda_{\pm} = (7 \pm \sqrt{5})/2\), and, assuming F1, these are the eigenvalues by Proposition 1.4. For \(\lambda_\pm\), the vector \(v = (1, \lambda_\pm - 4)\) is an eigenvector: the first coordinate of \((A - \lambda_\pm I)v\) is \((4 - \lambda_\pm) + (\lambda_\pm - 4) = 0\), and the second is \(1 + (3 - \lambda_\pm)(\lambda_\pm - 4) = -(\lambda_\pm^2 - 7\lambda_\pm + 11) = 0\). Since \(\lambda_\pm - 4 = (-1 \pm \sqrt{5})/2\), the eigenvectors are \((1, (-1 + \sqrt{5})/2)\) for \(\lambda_+\) and \((1, (-1 - \sqrt{5})/2)\) for \(\lambda_-\). Rounded to four decimals, \(\lambda_+ = 4.6180\) and \(\lambda_- = 2.3820\).
132: Remark 1.7 (a matrix with no real eigenvalue)
133: Let \(R = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(\det(R - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 1\), which is at least 1 for every real \(\lambda\). Assuming F1, Proposition 1.4 gives that \(R\) has no real eigenvalue. So a real matrix may have none, and the eigenvalue claims in this lesson concern real eigenvalues only. Problem R2 treats \(R\) under F1.
138: 2Eigendecomposition
142: Definition 2.1 (eigendecomposition)
143: An eigendecomposition of a real \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) is a factorisation \(A = PDP^{-1}\), where \(P\) is an invertible real \(n \times n\) matrix and \(D\) is a real diagonal matrix. A matrix with an eigendecomposition is diagonalisable.
149: Proposition 2.2 (independent eigenvectors give a factorisation)
150: Assuming F1, let \(A\) be a real \(n \times n\) matrix with eigenvectors \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) for real eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n\), where \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) are linearly independent. Let \(P\) be the matrix with columns \(v_1, \dots, v_n\) and \(D = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)\). Then \(P\) is invertible and \(A = PDP^{-1}\). The hypotheses are: \(A\) real and square, the \(\lambda_j\) real, linear independence, and F1.
156: Proof
157: The \(j\)-th column of \(AP\) is \(Av_j = \lambda_j v_j\). The \(j\)-th column of \(PD\) is \(\lambda_j\) times the \(j\)-th column of \(P\), which is \(\lambda_j v_j\) as well. So \(AP = PD\). If \(Px = 0\), then \(\sum_j x_j v_j = 0\), and linear independence gives \(x = 0\). By F1, \(P\) is invertible. Multiplying \(AP = PD\) on the right by \(P^{-1}\) gives \(A = PDP^{-1}\). \(\blacksquare\)
163: Proposition 2.3 (eigendecomposition and eigenbases)
164: Assuming F1, let \(A\) be a real \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A\) has an eigendecomposition if and only if \(\R^n\) has a basis made of eigenvectors of \(A\). The hypotheses are: \(A\) real and square, and F1.
170: Proof
171: If \(\R^n\) has a basis of \(n\) eigenvectors, Proposition 2.2 gives an eigendecomposition. Conversely, let \(A = PDP^{-1}\) with \(D = \diag(d_1, \dots, d_n)\), and let \(p_j\) be the \(j\)-th column of \(P\). Then \(AP = PD\) gives \(Ap_j = d_j p_j\). Since \(P\) is invertible, \(Pc = 0\) forces \(c = 0\) by F1, so the columns \(p_1, \dots, p_n\) are linearly independent. They also span \(\R^n\): for every \(b \in \R^n\), \(P(P^{-1}b) = b\) writes \(b\) as a combination of the columns, with coefficients \(P^{-1}b\). So they form a basis of \(\R^n\). Each \(p_j\) is nonzero, so it is an eigenvector of \(A\) for the real number \(d_j\). \(\blacksquare\)
177: Proposition 2.4 (powers of a diagonalisable matrix)
178: If \(A = PDP^{-1}\) with \(P\) invertible and \(D\) diagonal, then \(A^k = PD^kP^{-1}\) for every integer \(k \ge 1\). The hypotheses are: the factorisation, and invertibility of \(P\).
184: Proof
185: By induction on \(k\). The case \(k = 1\) is the hypothesis. If \(A^k = PD^kP^{-1}\), then \(A^{k+1} = A^k A = PD^kP^{-1}PDP^{-1} = PD^{k+1}P^{-1}\), because \(P^{-1}P = I\). \(\blacksquare\)
191: Example 2.5 (an upper triangular matrix)
192: Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\). The vectors \((1,0,0)\), \((1,1,0)\) and \((1,3,6)\) are eigenvectors for \(2\), \(3\) and \(5\). Direct multiplication gives \(A(1,3,6) = (2 + 3, 9 + 6, 30) = (5, 15, 30) = 5(1,3,6)\), and the other two checks are equally short. With \(P\) the matrix whose columns are these three vectors and \(D = \diag(2,3,5)\), Proposition 2.2 applies once the columns are known to be independent. The determinant of \(P\) is 6, which is nonzero, so, assuming F1, \(P\) is invertible; so, assuming F1, \(Px = 0\) forces \(x = 0\), which is independence of the columns. Lab 1 checks \(AP = PD\) in exact integer arithmetic.
198: Proposition 2.6 (a matrix with no basis of eigenvectors)
199: Assuming F1, let \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(\R^2\) has no basis of eigenvectors of \(B\), so by Proposition 2.3 \(B\) has no eigendecomposition. The hypotheses are: \(B\) as stated, Proposition 2.3 and F1.
205: Proof
206: Here \(\det(B - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)^2\), which is zero only for \(\lambda = 1\), so \(1\) is the only eigenvalue of \(B\) by Proposition 1.4. For \(v = (x, y)\) we have \((B - I)v = (y, 0)\), so \(Bv = v\) exactly when \(y = 0\). The eigenspace \(E_1\) is therefore \(\{(x, 0) : x \in \R\}\), a one-dimensional subspace. A basis of \(\R^2\) made of eigenvectors would consist of two linearly independent vectors, both in \(E_1\). Two vectors in a one-dimensional space are linearly dependent (Theorem 3.7 of Lesson 1, since \(E_1\) is spanned by \((1, 0)\)), so no such basis exists. \(\blacksquare\)

314: 3Where the canon breaks
316: Four limits apply to what this lesson establishes.
319: Eigenvalues are real here. Under F1, Remark 1.7 shows that a real matrix can have no real eigenvalue, and Definition 2.1 needs real \(D\). The unit makes no claim about complex eigenvalues.
321: Not every matrix is diagonalisable. Under F1, Proposition 2.6 gives a \(2 \times 2\) matrix with no eigendecomposition. Proposition 2.4 applies only to matrices that have one.
323: Eigenvectors and the order of eigenvalues are conventions. A nonzero multiple of an eigenvector is again an eigenvector (Proposition 1.3 gives the whole eigenspace). The order in which a numerical solver lists eigenvalues is arbitrary, and Lab 1 sorts them before printing.
325: Numerical solvers return approximations. Lab 1 checks the residuals of numpy's eigenpairs for one matrix below \(10^{-12}\). That check is evidence about that matrix and that solver's output. It does not prove the factorisation for other matrices, and, under F1, Proposition 2.2 is the statement that does.
332: 4Misconceptions
336: “The diagonal entries of a matrix are its eigenvalues.”
338: For the matrix of Example 1.6 the diagonal entries are 4 and 3, and the eigenvalues are \((7 \pm \sqrt{5})/2\). Direct computation gives \(\det(A - 4I) = (0)(-1) - 1 = -1\) and \(\det(A - 3I) = (1)(0) - 1 = -1\), so, assuming F1, neither 4 nor 3 is an eigenvalue, by Proposition 1.4. The diagonal entries of a general matrix are not its eigenvalues. For the upper triangular matrix of Example 2.5 they are 2, 3 and 5, and Example 2.5 gives an eigenvector for each. For a diagonal matrix \(D\), \(De_j = d_j e_j\), where \(e_j\) is the \(j\)-th standard basis vector (Lesson 1), so each diagonal entry \(d_j\) is an eigenvalue with eigenvector \(e_j\).
343: “A repeated eigenvalue means the matrix cannot be diagonalised.”
345: The identity matrix \(I_2\) has the eigenvalue 1 twice, and \(E_1 = \R^2\). The standard basis \(e_1, e_2\) is a basis of eigenvectors, so, assuming F1, \(I_2\) has an eigendecomposition with \(P = I_2\) and \(D = I_2\) by Proposition 2.3. The matrix \(B\) of Proposition 2.6 fails for another reason: its eigenspace has dimension 1, which is less than 2, the power of \((1 - \lambda)\) in \(\det(B - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)^2\).
350: “If all eigenvalues are real, the matrix is diagonalisable.”
352: The matrix \(B\) of Proposition 2.6 has the single real eigenvalue 1. Assuming F1, Proposition 2.6 shows that \(B\) has no basis of eigenvectors, and so no eigendecomposition. A real eigenvalue is therefore not enough: Proposition 2.3 requires a full basis of eigenvectors, not just eigenvalues.

367: Mechanics
370: M1 · Eigenpairs of an upper triangular matrix
371: Find the eigenvalues of \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and an eigenvector for each.
373: Solution
374: The matrix \(A - \lambda I = \begin{pmatrix} 3 - \lambda & 2 \\ 0 & 1 - \lambda \end{pmatrix}\) has determinant \((3 - \lambda)(1 - \lambda)\). Assuming F1, by Proposition 1.4 the eigenvalues are \(\lambda = 3\) and \(\lambda = 1\). For \(\lambda = 3\), \(A - 3I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 2 \\ 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}\), and \((1, 0)\) is an eigenvector. For \(\lambda = 1\), \(A - I = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}\), and \((1, -1)\) is an eigenvector, which the check \(A(1,-1) = (3 - 2, -1) = (1, -1)\) confirms.
380: M2 · Eigenvalues of a singular matrix
381: Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\).
383: Solution
384: The determinant is \(\det(A - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)(4 - \lambda) - 4 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda = \lambda(\lambda - 5)\). Assuming F1, the eigenvalues are \(0\) and \(5\). For \(0\), the vector \((2, -1)\) is an eigenvector, since \(A(2, -1) = (0, 0)\). For \(5\), the vector \((1, 2)\) is an eigenvector, since \(A(1,2) = (5, 10) = 5(1,2)\). Assuming F1, the zero eigenvalue shows that \(A\) is not invertible.
390: M3 · Factoring the matrix of M2
391: For \(A\) of Problem M2, let \(P = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(D = \diag(0, 5)\). Show that \(A = PDP^{-1}\).
393: Solution
394: The columns of \(P\) are the eigenvectors \((2,-1)\) and \((1,2)\) from Problem M2, with eigenvalues \(0\) and \(5\), in the order of \(D\). The determinant of \(P\) is \(2 \cdot 2 - 1 \cdot (-1) = 5\), which is nonzero, so \(P^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(PD = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 5 \\ 0 & 10 \end{pmatrix}\), and \(PDP^{-1} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 10 & 20 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = A\). This is an instance of Proposition 2.2 with explicit numbers. The inverse was computed directly, so this check does not use F1.
403: Proof
406: P1 · Eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are independent
407: Let \(\lambda \ne \mu\) be eigenvalues of a real matrix \(A\), with eigenvectors \(v\) and \(w\). Prove that \(v\) and \(w\) are linearly independent.
409: Solution
410: Suppose \(av + bw = 0\) for real \(a\) and \(b\). Applying \(A\) gives \(a\lambda v + b\mu w = 0\). Subtracting \(\mu\) times the first equation gives \(a(\lambda - \mu)v = 0\). Since \(\lambda \ne \mu\) and \(v \ne 0\), \(a = 0\). Then \(bw = 0\), and since \(w \ne 0\), \(b = 0\). So the only relation between \(v\) and \(w\) is the trivial one.
416: P2 · Inverse of an invertible diagonalisable matrix
417: Assuming F1, let \(A = PDP^{-1}\) be invertible, with \(P\) invertible and \(D = \diag(d_1, \dots, d_n)\). Prove that every \(d_j \ne 0\) and that \(A^{-1} = PD^{-1}P^{-1}\), where \(D^{-1} = \diag(1/d_1, \dots, 1/d_n)\).
419: Solution
420: Suppose \(d_j = 0\) for some \(j\). The column \(p_j\) of \(P\) is nonzero and satisfies \(Ap_j = d_j p_j = 0\), so \(A\) has a nonzero null vector. That contradicts F1, because \(A\) is invertible. So every \(d_j \ne 0\), and \(D^{-1}\) exists. Then \((PD^{-1}P^{-1})(PDP^{-1}) = PD^{-1}DP^{-1} = PP^{-1} = I\), and by F1 the product \(PD^{-1}P^{-1}\) is \(A^{-1}\).
426: P3 · Eigenvalues of the inverse
427: Assuming F1, let \(A\) be invertible and let \(Av = \lambda v\) with \(v \ne 0\). Prove that \(\lambda \ne 0\) and that \(A^{-1}v = (1/\lambda)v\).
429: Solution
430: If \(\lambda = 0\), then \(Av = 0\) with \(v \ne 0\), which contradicts the invertibility of \(A\) by F1. So \(\lambda \ne 0\). Multiplying \(Av = \lambda v\) on the left by \(A^{-1}\) gives \(v = \lambda A^{-1}v\), so \(A^{-1}v = (1/\lambda)v\). Hence \(1/\lambda\) is an eigenvalue of \(A^{-1}\), with the same eigenvector.

442: E1 · What the exact count establishes (formula input)
443: Lab 1 prints "3 of 3" for the exact eigenpairs. Explain why this line is an exact statement about the three vectors, while the numerical lines are not.
445: Solution
446: The exact line computes \(Av\) and \(\lambda v\) with Python integers, so the test \(Av = \lambda v\) compares two integer vectors and no rounding enters. The numerical lines call a floating-point solver and compare its output with a tolerance of \(10^{-12}\), so they establish only that the solver's output satisfies the relation to that tolerance.
452: E2 · Recomputing the determinant (formula input)
453: Lab 1 prints "det P = 6". Compute \(\det P\) by hand for \(P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\), and say what the value allows you to conclude.
455: Solution
456: Expanding along the first column, the only nonzero entry is the \((1,1)\) entry 1, and its minor is \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\) with determinant 6. So \(\det P = 6\), which matches the printed value. The determinant is nonzero, so, assuming F1, \(P\) is invertible. Lab 1 also prints "A P equals P D exactly (integer arithmetic): True", and with \(P\) invertible (assuming F1), Proposition 2.2 gives \(A = PDP^{-1}\).
462: E3 · The eigenspace dimension for B (formula input)
463: Lab 1 prints a rank of 1 for \(B - I\) and states that the eigenspace for 1 has dimension 1. Confirm the dimension from the definition of \(E_1\), without using the lab.
465: Solution
466: For \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), \((B - I)(x, y) = (y, 0)\). So \(E_1\) is the set of vectors \((x, 0)\), which is a one-dimensional subspace, spanned by \((1, 0)\). The dimension 1 agrees with the printed rank: by Lesson 1, Proposition 3.12, the dimension of \(E_1\) is 2 minus the rank of \(B - I\). The solution uses Definition 1.2 and the rank-dimension result of Lesson 1, Proposition 3.12.

478: R1 · Why a residual check is not a proof
479: Explain why the residual check in Lab 1 is evidence about one solver's output, while Proposition 2.2 is a statement about every matrix that satisfies its hypotheses.
481: Solution
482: A residual is the size of \(Av - \lambda v\) for a vector that the solver returned. The solver works in floating-point arithmetic, so the residual is small (below the lab's tolerance of \(10^{-12}\) on the listed matrix) and need not be zero for other inputs; it is computed for the listed matrix only. Proposition 2.2 instead proves, assuming F1 and the hypotheses, that the factorisation holds whenever the eigenvectors are independent. The check and the proof answer different questions, and the lesson uses the proof for the general claim.
488: R2 · The rotation is not diagonalisable over the reals
489: Assuming F1, show that the matrix \(R\) of Remark 1.7 has no eigendecomposition with a real diagonal matrix \(D\).
491: Solution
492: Suppose \(R = PDP^{-1}\) with \(D\) real diagonal. The argument of Proposition 2.3 shows that each diagonal entry \(d_j\) of \(D\) is a real eigenvalue of \(R\), because \(Rp_j = d_j p_j\) with \(p_j \ne 0\). By Remark 1.7, \(R\) has no real eigenvalue, so \(D\) cannot have a diagonal entry. But a \(2 \times 2\) diagonal matrix has two diagonal entries. The supposed factorisation cannot exist.
498: R3 · A power by the factorisation
499: For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) of Problem M1, use \(P = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(D = \diag(3, 1)\) to compute \(A^3\), and check the answer by multiplying out \(A^3\) directly.
501: Solution
502: The columns of \(P\) are the eigenvectors \((1, 0)\) for 3 and \((1, -1)\) for 1, so \(A = PDP^{-1}\) by Proposition 2.2 (assuming F1). Here \(P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\), because that matrix is its own inverse, which can be checked directly. By Proposition 2.4, \(A^3 = PD^3P^{-1}\). Computing \(PD^3 = \begin{pmatrix} 27 & 1 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\) and multiplying by \(P^{-1}\) gives \(A^3 = \begin{pmatrix} 27 & 26 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Directly, \(A^2 = \begin{pmatrix} 9 & 8 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(A^3 = A^2 A = \begin{pmatrix} 27 & 26 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), which agrees.
