62: A quadratic form \(x^{\top}Ax\) (Definition 1.1 below) is a number that depends on the direction \(x\). A symmetric matrix whose form is nonnegative for every \(x\) is positive semidefinite (Definition 1.4). Under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, the eigenvalue test of Theorem 2.1 turns that property into a computable check. The test needs care: a computed eigenvalue of a singular matrix is a floating-point number near zero, whose sign is unreliable, so Lab 1 shows how a tolerance decides the verdict. The matrices \(B^{\top}B\) are the standard examples, because their form is the squared length \(\|Bx\|^2\), and Proposition 2.4, under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, shows that every positive semidefinite matrix has the form \(B^{\top}B\).
65: 1Quadratic forms and the definition
69: Definition 1.1 (quadratic form)
70: For a real \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) and \(x \in \R^n\), the quadratic form of \(A\) at \(x\) is \(x^{\top}Ax = \sum_{i,j} A_{ij}x_i x_j\). It is a real number.
76: Remark 1.2 (only the symmetric part matters)
77: For every real square \(A\) and every \(x\), \(x^{\top}Ax = x^{\top}Sx\), where \(S = (A + A^{\top})/2\) is symmetric. The reason is that \(x^{\top}A^{\top}x\) is a \(1 \times 1\) matrix, equal to its own transpose \(x^{\top}Ax\), so \(x^{\top}Ax = \tfrac12(x^{\top}Ax + x^{\top}A^{\top}x) = x^{\top}Sx\). The definition below therefore asks for symmetry explicitly.
83: Definition 1.3 (Gram matrix)
84: For a real \(m \times n\) matrix \(B\), the \(n \times n\) matrix \(B^{\top}B\) is the Gram matrix of \(B\).
90: Definition 1.4 (positive semidefinite and positive definite)
91: A real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix \(A\) is positive semidefinite when \(x^{\top}Ax \ge 0\) for every \(x \in \R^n\). It is positive definite when \(x^{\top}Ax > 0\) for every nonzero \(x \in \R^n\). Symmetry is part of both definitions.
97: Proposition 1.5 (Gram matrices are positive semidefinite)
98: Let \(B\) be a real \(m \times n\) matrix. Then \(B^{\top}B\) is a symmetric \(n \times n\) positive semidefinite matrix. The hypotheses are: \(B\) real, with any \(m \ge 1\) and \(n \ge 1\).
104: Proof
105: \((B^{\top}B)^{\top} = B^{\top}(B^{\top})^{\top} = B^{\top}B\), so the matrix is symmetric. For \(x \in \R^n\), \(x^{\top}B^{\top}Bx = (Bx)^{\top}(Bx) = \|Bx\|^2 \ge 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
111: Proposition 1.6 (diagonal entries and sums)
112: Let \(A\) and \(C\) be real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrices, and \(c \ge 0\). If \(A\) is positive semidefinite, then every diagonal entry \(A_{ii}\) is nonnegative. If \(A\) and \(C\) are positive semidefinite, so are \(A + C\) and \(cA\). The hypotheses are that the matrices are real and symmetric, and that \(c \ge 0\).
118: Proof
119: Taking \(x = e_i\), the \(i\)-th standard basis vector (Lesson 1), gives \(e_i^{\top}Ae_i = A_{ii} \ge 0\). Also \(x^{\top}(A + C)x = x^{\top}Ax + x^{\top}Cx \ge 0\) and \(x^{\top}(cA)x = c\,x^{\top}Ax \ge 0\). The matrices \(A + C\) and \(cA\) are symmetric, so they satisfy Definition 1.4. \(\blacksquare\)
125: Example 1.7 (positive entries are not enough)
126: Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Every entry is positive, and so is every diagonal entry. For \(x = (1, -1)\), \(x^{\top}Ax = 1 \cdot 1 + 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 1 = 1 - 4 + 1 = -2\). The quadratic form is negative at this vector, so \(A\) is not positive semidefinite. Example 2.6 reaches the same conclusion from the eigenvalues.
132: Remark 1.8 (a nonsymmetric matrix with a positive form)
133: Let \(M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\). Then \(Mx = (x_1 + x_2, -x_1 + x_2)\), so \(x^{\top}Mx = x_1^2 + x_2^2 > 0\) for every nonzero \(x\). Yet \(M\) is not symmetric, so Definition 1.4 does not apply to it. Its characteristic polynomial is \(\lambda^2 - 2\lambda + 2\), with zeros \(1 \pm i\), which are not real. Remark 1.8 is the case that the eigenvalue test of §2 cannot handle, and §4 returns to it.
138: 2The eigenvalue test
142: Theorem 2.1 (eigenvalue test for positive semidefiniteness)
143: Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A\) is positive semidefinite if and only if every eigenvalue of \(A\) is nonnegative. The hypotheses are: \(A\) real and symmetric. Assumptions F1 to F4 enter only through the spectral theorem of Lesson 4, used in the (\(\Leftarrow\)) direction.
149: Proof
150: (\(\Rightarrow\)) Let \(\lambda\) be an eigenvalue of \(A\), with eigenvector \(v = x + iy\), where \(x\) and \(y\) are real and \(v \ne 0\). By Lesson 4, Lemma 2.1, \(\lambda\) is real. Comparing real and imaginary parts of \(Av = \lambda v\), using that \(A\) and \(\lambda\) are real, gives \(Ax = \lambda x\) and \(Ay = \lambda y\). As \(v \ne 0\), one of \(x\) and \(y\) is a nonzero real eigenvector \(w\) for \(\lambda\). Then \(0 \le w^{\top}Aw = \lambda w^{\top}w = \lambda \|w\|^2\), and \(\|w\|^2 > 0\), so \(\lambda \ge 0\).
152: (\(\Leftarrow\)) By Theorem 2.3 of Lesson 4, \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) with \(Q\) orthogonal and \(\Lambda = \diag(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)\). Each \(\lambda_j\) is an eigenvalue of \(A\), since \(Aq_j = \lambda_j q_j\) with \(q_j \ne 0\), so each \(\lambda_j \ge 0\) by hypothesis. For \(x \in \R^n\), Corollary 2.5 of Lesson 4 gives \(x^{\top}Ax = \sum_j \lambda_j y_j^2 \ge 0\), where \(y = Q^{\top}x\). \(\blacksquare\)
158: Theorem 2.2 (eigenvalue test for positive definiteness)
159: Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A\) be a real symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A\) is positive definite if and only if every eigenvalue of \(A\) is strictly positive. The hypotheses are those of Theorem 2.1.
165: Proof
166: (\(\Rightarrow\)) Let \(\lambda\) be an eigenvalue of \(A\). As in the proof of Theorem 2.1, \(\lambda\) is real and \(A\) has a nonzero real eigenvector \(w\) for \(\lambda\). Then \(0  0\). (\(\Leftarrow\)) Let \(x \ne 0\) and \(y = Q^{\top}x\). Since \(x = Qy\) and \(Q\) is invertible, \(y \ne 0\), so some \(y_j \ne 0\). Then \(x^{\top}Ax = \sum_j \lambda_j y_j^2\) is a sum of nonnegative terms, one of which is positive, so it is positive. \(\blacksquare\)
172: Proposition 2.3 (a zero form forces a null vector)
173: Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A\) be a real symmetric positive semidefinite matrix. If \(x^{\top}Ax = 0\), then \(Ax = 0\). The hypotheses are: \(A\) real, symmetric and positive semidefinite, and the vector \(x\).
179: Proof
180: Let \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) and \(y = Q^{\top}x\). By Corollary 2.5 of Lesson 4 and Theorem 2.1, \(0 = x^{\top}Ax = \sum_j \lambda_j y_j^2\) is a sum of nonnegative terms, so each term is zero: \(\lambda_j y_j^2 = 0\). Then \(\lambda_j y_j = 0\) for every \(j\), because \(\lambda_j y_j\) is zero whether \(y_j = 0\) or \(\lambda_j = 0\). So \(\Lambda y = 0\), and \(Ax = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}x = Q\Lambda y = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
186: Proposition 2.4 (every positive semidefinite matrix is a Gram matrix)
187: Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A\) be real, symmetric and positive semidefinite. Then \(A = B^{\top}B\) for \(B = \Lambda^{1/2}Q^{\top}\), where \(A = Q\Lambda Q^{\top}\) is as in Theorem 2.3 and \(\Lambda^{1/2} = \diag(\sqrt{\lambda_1}, \dots, \sqrt{\lambda_n})\). The hypotheses are: \(A\) real, symmetric and positive semidefinite, so every \(\lambda_j \ge 0\) by Theorem 2.1, and the existence of square roots of nonnegative real numbers.
193: Proof
194: Since \(\Lambda^{1/2}\) is diagonal, \(\Lambda^{1/2}\Lambda^{1/2} = \Lambda\), so \(B^{\top}B = Q(\Lambda^{1/2})^{\top}\Lambda^{1/2}Q^{\top} = Q\Lambda Q^{\top} = A\). Here \((\Lambda^{1/2})^{\top} = \Lambda^{1/2}\) because it is diagonal, and \(B^{\top} = Q(\Lambda^{1/2})^{\top}\). \(\blacksquare\)
200: Example 2.5 (a factor of a positive definite matrix)
201: Let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Example 2.6 of Lesson 4 gives \(Q = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(\Lambda = \diag(3, 1)\). Then \(B = \Lambda^{1/2}Q^{\top} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix} \sqrt3 & \sqrt3 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\). The entries of \(B^{\top}B\) are \((3 + 1)/2 = 2\), \((\sqrt3 \cdot \sqrt3 - 1)/2 = 1\) and \((3 + 1)/2 = 2\), which is \(A\).
207: Example 2.6 (the eigenvalue test on Example 1.7)
208: For \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), \(\det(A - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)^2 - 4 = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = (\lambda - 3)(\lambda + 1)\), so the eigenvalues are 3 and \(-1\). The vector \((1,-1)\) is an eigenvector for \(-1\), since \(A(1,-1) = (-1, 1)\). Under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Theorem 2.1 says \(A\) is not positive semidefinite, which agrees with the value \(-2\) of Example 1.7.
214: Remark 2.7 (the factor is not unique)
215: If \(B^{\top}B = A\) and \(O\) is an orthogonal matrix, then \((OB)^{\top}(OB) = B^{\top}O^{\top}OB = B^{\top}B = A\). Taking \(O = -I\) shows that \(-B\) is another factor whenever \(B \ne 0\). So a nonzero Gram matrix has at least two factors, and, under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Proposition 2.4 gives one of them.
220: 3Testing numerically
224: Definition 3.1 (tolerance test)
225: Let \(\tau \ge 0\). A real symmetric matrix passes the tolerance test at \(\tau\) when every computed eigenvalue is at least \(-\tau\). This is a decision rule chosen in advance. Theorem 2.1 is a statement about exact eigenvalues, and the tolerance test is a numerical decision that uses it.
231: Remark 3.2 (a rank-deficient Gram matrix)
232: Let \(B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) and \(G = B^{\top}B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\). Expanding gives \(\det(G - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)\lambda(\lambda - 3)\), so the eigenvalues of \(G\) are exactly 0, 1 and 3. The vector \((1, 1, -1)\) is an eigenvector for 0, since \(B(1,1,-1) = (0,0)\). By Proposition 1.5, \(G\) is positive semidefinite. Under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Theorem 2.1 makes its eigenvalues nonnegative, and since its smallest eigenvalue is exactly zero, Theorem 2.2 says \(G\) is not positive definite.

373: 4Where the canon breaks
375: Four limits apply to this lesson.
378: Symmetry is part of the definition. Remark 1.8 gives a matrix whose quadratic form is positive for every nonzero vector, but whose eigenvalues \(1 \pm i\) are not real. Theorem 2.1 does not apply to it, and Definition 1.4 excludes it.
380: The eigenvalue test is exact, and the numerical test is a decision. Theorem 2.1 holds for exact eigenvalues. Definition 3.1 chooses a tolerance, and the result can depend on that choice when an eigenvalue lies near zero.
382: A sample refutes but does not prove. Part C of Lab 1 tests 100000 quadratic forms, and that is evidence only for the seeded matrices and vectors in the code. A single negative value would refute positive semidefiniteness. For a Gram matrix, Proposition 1.5 proves it for every vector. In general, the eigenvalue test (Theorem 2.1, under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4) does.
384: The factor in Proposition 2.4 is not unique. Remark 2.7 shows that every orthogonal change of \(B\) gives the same \(A\). Any statement about \(B\) must be made for the chosen factor.
391: 5Misconceptions
395: “A matrix with all positive entries is positive semidefinite.”
397: Example 1.7 gives \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), with every entry positive, and the quadratic form at \((1,-1)\) is \(-2\). Its eigenvalues are \(-1\) and \(3\), so, under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Theorem 2.1 gives the same conclusion. Positive entries do not fix the sign of \(x^{\top}Ax\): at \((1,-1)\) the form is \(-2\).
402: “A positive semidefinite matrix has all eigenvalues positive.”
404: The Gram matrix \(G\) of Remark 3.2 is positive semidefinite, and its eigenvalue 0 is attained by \((1,1,-1)\), which gives the quadratic form \(0\). Positive definiteness requires strict positivity, and Theorem 2.2 is the statement for it. Under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, for a positive semidefinite matrix the two notions differ exactly when a zero eigenvalue is present.
409: “If a computed eigenvalue is \(-10^{-16}\), the matrix is not positive semidefinite.”
411: The exact eigenvalue of \(G\) in Remark 3.2 is 0. A floating-point solver may return a value near zero with either sign, so a strict test on the sign can reject a positive semidefinite matrix. Definition 3.1 uses a tolerance for that reason, and Part D of Lab 1 shows the tolerance test accepting the matrix.

449: M3 · A quadratic form and an eigenvalue test
450: Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\). Compute its eigenvalues and decide whether it is positive definite. Then compute \(x^{\top}Ax\) at \(x = (1,1)\).
452: Solution
453: \(\det(A - \lambda I) = (3 - \lambda)^2 - 1 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 4)\), so the eigenvalues are 2 and 4. Both are positive, so \(A\) is positive definite by Theorem 2.2, under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4. At \(x = (1,1)\), \(x^{\top}Ax = 3 - 1 - 1 + 3 = 4\).
462: Proof
465: P1 · The symmetric part
466: Prove Remark 1.2: for every real square \(A\) and every \(x\), \(x^{\top}Ax = x^{\top}Sx\) with \(S = (A + A^{\top})/2\).
468: Solution
469: The number \(x^{\top}Ax\) is a \(1 \times 1\) matrix, so it equals its transpose. The transpose of \(x^{\top}Ax\) is \(x^{\top}A^{\top}x\). Hence \(x^{\top}Ax = x^{\top}A^{\top}x\), and averaging the two expressions gives \(x^{\top}Ax = \tfrac12(x^{\top}Ax + x^{\top}A^{\top}x) = x^{\top}\tfrac{A + A^{\top}}{2}x\). The matrix \(S\) is symmetric, since \(S^{\top} = (A^{\top} + A)/2 = S\).
475: P2 · Positive definite Gram matrices
476: Let \(B\) be a real \(m \times n\) matrix whose columns are linearly independent. Prove that \(B^{\top}B\) is positive definite.
478: Solution
479: By Proposition 1.5, \(B^{\top}B\) is positive semidefinite, and \(x^{\top}B^{\top}Bx = \|Bx\|^2\). Let \(x \ne 0\). Since the columns of \(B\) are linearly independent, \(Bx = \sum_j x_j b_j \ne 0\), where \(b_j\) are the columns. So \(\|Bx\|^2 > 0\), and \(B^{\top}B\) is positive definite.
485: P3 · Diagonal entries and null vectors
486: Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, let \(A\) be real symmetric and positive semidefinite, and let \(A_{ii} = 0\) for some \(i\). Prove that row \(i\) and column \(i\) of \(A\) are zero.
488: Solution
489: Take \(x = e_i\). Then \(e_i^{\top}Ae_i = A_{ii} = 0\), so, under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Proposition 2.3 gives \(Ae_i = 0\), which says column \(i\) is zero. Because \(A\) is symmetric, row \(i\) equals column \(i\) transposed, so row \(i\) is zero too.
498: Empirical
501: E1 · The printed form and eigenvalues (formula input)
502: Lab 1 Part A prints the quadratic form \(-2\) and the eigenvalues \(-1.0000\) and \(3.0000\) of \([[1,2],[2,1]]\). Show that the two printed facts agree with each other.
504: Solution
505: The eigenvector for \(-1\) is \((1,-1)\), since \(A(1,-1) = (-1, 1)\). For any eigenvector \(v\) with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), \(v^{\top}Av = \lambda\|v\|^2\). Here \(\|(1,-1)\|^2 = 2\), so the form at this eigenvector is \((-1)(2) = -2\), which is the printed value. Under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Theorem 2.1 then says that a negative eigenvalue gives a negative quadratic form at some vector, and the printed value shows it.
511: E2 · Why 118 of 360 is not one third (formula input)
512: Lab 1 Part B prints 118 of 360 grid angles with a negative form and 4 with a zero form. The negative directions form one third of the circle. Explain the difference between 118 and 120.
514: Solution
515: The form on the unit circle is \(1 + 2\sin 2t\), which is negative for \(t\) in \((105^{\circ}, 165^{\circ}) \cup (285^{\circ}, 345^{\circ})\), a total of \(120^{\circ}\), or one third of the circle. The grid has 360 angles, one for each degree. The four endpoints 105, 165, 285 and 345 are on the boundary, where the form is zero, so the strict count excludes them. The angles strictly inside are 106 to 164 and 286 to 344, which is \(59 + 59 = 118\). The grid count is \(118/360 = 0.3278\), and the one third is the measure of the set, not the count.
521: E3 · The rank 2 Gram matrix (formula input)
522: Lab 1 Part D prints the eigenvalues \(0.0000\), \(1.0000\) and \(3.0000\), and a count of 1 near-zero eigenvalue. Check the three eigenvalues from the characteristic polynomial, and say what the count of 1 shows.
524: Solution
525: Remark 3.2 expands \(\det(G - \lambda I) = (1 - \lambda)\lambda(\lambda - 3)\), so the eigenvalues are exactly 0, 1 and 3, matching the printed values. The count 1 means exactly one eigenvalue has absolute value at most \(10^{-10}\), which is the zero eigenvalue. The eigenvalues 1 and 3 are not near zero. The tolerance test accepts \(G\) because its smallest computed eigenvalue is at least \(-10^{-10}\).

537: R1 · Choosing the Gram factor
538: Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, show that for the matrix \(A\) of Example 2.5 there are infinitely many real \(2 \times 2\) matrices \(B\) with \(B^{\top}B = A\). Name one factor that is not the one in Proposition 2.4.
540: Solution
541: Remark 2.7 shows that \(OB\) is a Gram factor for every orthogonal \(O\). Rotations \(O_t = \begin{pmatrix} \cos t & -\sin t \\ \sin t & \cos t \end{pmatrix}\) are orthogonal for every real \(t\). The factor \(B = \Lambda^{1/2}Q^{\top}\) is invertible, because \(\Lambda^{1/2} = \diag(\sqrt3, 1)\) and \(Q^{\top}\) are invertible, so \(O_tB = O_sB\) forces \(O_t = O_s\). The first column of \(O_t\) is \((\cos t, \sin t)\), which determines \(t\) in \([0, 2\pi)\), so the factors \(O_tB\) for \(t \in [0, 2\pi)\) are infinitely many and pairwise distinct. For instance, \(O_\pi B = -B\) is a different factor with the same Gram matrix.
547: R2 · Sampling cannot prove positivity
548: Assuming F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, explain, with the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\), why a random sample of quadratic forms can refute positive semidefiniteness but never prove it.
550: Solution
551: One vector with a negative form is enough to show that the matrix is not positive semidefinite, and \((1,-1)\) is such a vector with value \(-2\). The converse is not available from a sample. Even if every sampled vector gave a nonnegative form, the vectors not sampled could still give a negative value. A proof must cover all vectors, and, under Assumptions F1 to F4 of Lesson 4, Theorem 2.1 does that through the eigenvalues.
557: R3 · Closure under sums
558: Let \(A\) and \(C\) be positive semidefinite \(n \times n\) matrices. Show that \(A + C\) is positive semidefinite using only Definition 1.4, without the eigenvalue test.
560: Solution
561: \(A + C\) is symmetric, since both summands are. For each \(x\), \(x^{\top}(A + C)x = x^{\top}Ax + x^{\top}Cx\), and each term is nonnegative by Definition 1.4. So the sum is nonnegative, and \(A + C\) satisfies Definition 1.4 directly. This is the content of Proposition 1.6.
