32: Quant & Finance Interviews
33: Quantitative finance questions ask for an estimate, a probability, a fair price or a decision, with the reasoning stated. This module sets out the tools for those questions and tests each of them in a model. You estimate with stated ranges and bounds, compute conditional probabilities by enumeration, and analyse expected-value games with a stopping result that holds for fair games over a finite horizon. You then play a market-making model in code, write a timed coding answer with its cost stated, and answer a technical finance question with its assumptions named. The project is a market-making game: you write a quoting policy and a replay of its log, and you analyse the edge and inventory risk of the log that results.
35: Prerequisites: M4 Probability, CS2 Algorithms & Data Structures and FC2 Derivatives Fundamentals. Their prerequisite closure also includes M1, M2, AC1, CF1, CS1, E1, FC1 and P0.1. P0.1 is not yet released in this program, and its entries in the concept index read "not yet released". No link on this module points to a P0.1 concept.
36: Project: Market-making game, graded by an autograder and scored against a rubric
37: Data: none. The games of Lesson 4 and the project are generated by a seeded engine and labelled SIMULATED. The games of Lesson 3 are fixed exact games with no randomness. The drill items of Lesson 2 are generated from a seed and labelled as generated. No lab or project reads a data file. The game is a model, and no result in this module is a claim about real market makers.

46: Objectives
47: By the end of PC3 you can:
49: Bound a quantity by writing it as a product of stated factor ranges, and state the product bounds and the log-error width of the result (Lesson 1).
50: Compute a conditional probability on a finite space, and apply Bayes' rule and the law of total probability to a puzzle whose sampling rule is stated (Lesson 2).
51: Compute the expected payoff of a game in integer cents, decide whether it is fair, and prove that a stopping rule that sees only the history cannot change the expected total of a finite fair game (Lesson 3).
52: Derive the inventory and cash identities of a market-making game, and check a P&L decomposition against the cash flows and the settlement value (Lesson 4).
53: Implement a sliding-window maximum with a monotonic deque, and state its operation count against a naive scan (Lesson 5).
54: Compute a forward price and a net present value in cents, and state the assumptions and the sensitivity of a case answer (Lesson 6).
55: Write a quoting policy and a P&L replay that pass the autograder on seeds 0 to 49 and on your own workspace seed, and analyse the game log with the decomposition the rubric requires (project).

73: Placement diagnostic
74: Ten questions: eight on the prerequisites (M4, CS2, FC2, M1, M2, CF1 and AC1), and two skill checks, marked as such, on what rungs 1 and 2 of the project teach. The mastery bar is 80 percent. With ten items that bar needs eight correct, since seven of ten is 70 percent and eight of ten is 80 percent. To skip rungs 1 and 2, you must reach the bar and answer both skill checks. P0.1 is not yet released, and none of the ten items tests it, so a placement result says nothing about P0.1.
77: A fair six-sided die is rolled once. What is its expected face?
78: Answer
79: The expected face is \((1 + 2 + \cdots + 6)/6 = 21/6 = 3.5\) (M4 expectation; PC3 Lesson 3, Example 3.3).
82: Two fair coins are flipped independently. What is the probability that both show heads?
83: Answer
84: By independence the probability is \(1/2 \cdot 1/2 = 1/4 = 0.25\) (M4 independence).
87: What is \(\ln(e^2)\)?
88: Answer
89: The logarithm is the inverse of the exponential, so \(\ln(e^2) = 2\) (M1).
92: What is the derivative of \(x^3\) at \(x = 1\)?
93: Answer
94: The derivative of \(x^3\) is \(3x^2\), which equals 3 at \(x = 1\) (M2 derivative).
97: In the worst case, how many comparisons does a binary search of a sorted array of \(n\) items need, up to a constant?
98: Options
99: \(O(1)\)
100: \(O(\log n)\)
101: \(O(n)\)
103: Each comparison halves the remaining range, so about \(\log_2 n\) comparisons suffice in the worst case (CS2 asymptotic complexity).
106: For a spot price \(S\), a continuously compounded rate \(r\) and an expiry \(\tau\), with no income, which expression is the cost-of-carry forward price?
107: Options
108: \(S + r\tau\)
109: \(S e^{r\tau}\)
110: \(S / (1 + r\tau)\)
112: The cost-of-carry forward price is \(F^\ast = S e^{r\tau}\) (FC2, Lesson 2).
115: A cash flow of 100 cents is received in one year. The discount rate is 10 percent a year, compounded annually. What is its present value in cents, to six decimal places?
116: Answer
117: The present value is \(100/1.1 = 90.909091\) cents to six decimal places (CF1 net present value).
120: In a balance sheet, which identity holds?
121: Options
122: Assets = liabilities minus equity
123: Assets = liabilities + equity
124: Equity = assets + liabilities
126: The balance sheet identity is assets equal liabilities plus equity (AC1 articulation).
129: Skill check (rung 1): a market maker buys one unit at a bid of 10 005 cents. In integer cents, what is the cash flow of this fill, with a negative value for cash paid out?
130: Answer
131: A fill in which the maker buys pays its bid, so the cash flow is \(-10\,005\) cents. The worked example of the project (rung 1) computes this cash flow line by line.
134: Skill check (rung 2): after that fill the inventory is +1 unit, and the settlement value is 10 040 cents. In integer cents, what is the inventory mark?
135: Answer
136: The inventory mark is the ending inventory times the settlement value, \(1 \cdot 10\,040 = 10\,040\) cents. The completion problem of the project (rung 2) asks for this line.
142: Lessons
143: Six lessons of six study hours each, which sum to 36 hours. Each page declares its own hours in its data-hours attribute. Lessons 1 to 3 cover estimation, probability puzzles and expected-value games. Lessons 4 and 5 cover the market-making model and timed coding. Lesson 6 covers technical finance questions and case interviews. The lesson pages share one structure: graded claims, runnable labs, a problem set with worked solutions, and a retrieval quiz that feeds your review deck.
145: L01 · 6 h Fermi Estimation and Mental Math Products of stated factor ranges, their exact bounds and log-error widths, and a second-order bound for the mental estimate of \((1 + x)^n\).
146: L02 · 6 h Probability Puzzles and Conditional Reasoning Conditional probability on a finite space, Bayes' rule, the law of total probability, and the coin, urn and Monty Hall puzzles checked by enumeration.
147: L03 · 6 h Expected-Value Games Expected payoffs and linearity, fair games, backward induction in a reroll game, and the finite-horizon theorem on stopping rules.
148: L04 · 6 h Market-Making Games and Inventory Risk The rules of the market-making game, the cash, inventory and P&L identities, and adverse selection in plain words, checked by the seeded engine.
149: L05 · 6 h Timed Coding Interviews Complexity budgets, choosing a structure, stating the edge cases, and a sliding-window maximum by a monotonic deque against a naive scan.
150: L06 · 6 h Technical Finance Questions and Case Interviews Forward prices, put–call parity, net present value and the accounting identity in the closure, and the case framework with its assumptions and sensitivity.

158: Worked example The reference replay applied to the seed-0 log of a fixed symmetric policy, annotated line by line, with the reason for each cash flow, inventory update and mark.
159: Completion problem The same replay with the cash-flow line and the mark line left blank (each of the two lines replaced by ___). Graded instantly in your browser.

The autograder runs them on seeds 0 to 49, on your own workspace seed, and on that seed plus 7919, and analysis.md is scored against the rubric. Project page .
161: Stretch An open-ended extension with no ceiling, for example the distribution of the P&L over 200 seeds with exact quantiles, suitable for your portfolio.
166: Hours
167: The module is 50 hours: 36 hours of lessons and 14 hours of project, which is 28 percent of the module. Each lesson is 6 hours. The project's 14 hours split into seven lines. Rungs 1 and 2 take 1 hour each, one annotated replay and one completion. The quoting rule, quote in market_maker.py , takes 3 hours, and the replay, replay in the same file, takes 2 hours. The autograder runs on the fixed seeds, on your seed and in a local run take 2 hours. The analysis takes 4 hours; the rubric asks for three log turns, one counterfactual rerun and one self-check. The self-assessment takes 1 hour. The lines sum to \(1 + 1 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 4 + 1 = 14\) hours, which is 28 percent of the module.
