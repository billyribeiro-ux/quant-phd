51: An estimate built from a few factors, each stated as a range, answers a quantity question when no table is at hand, and the product of the ranges gives bounds that can be defended one factor at a time. This lesson sets out that method, proves the bounds of a product of positive ranges, proves that the logarithm of the ratio of a product's ends is the sum of the logarithms of the ratios for its factors, and derives a bound on the error of the mental estimate \(1 + nx\) for \((1 + x)^n\). Every range in this lesson is assumed for the example. None is a data value, and the lesson makes no claim about any market.
53: 1 Fermi estimates and product bounds
55: Real numbers are written \(\R\). A quantity that is hard to know is often easy to bound. Write it as a product of factors, state each factor as a range, and multiply the ends of the ranges. Apart from the logarithm (module M1), which Proposition 1.3 and Example 1.4 use, the derivatives and the Taylor theorem (module M2), which Section 3 uses, the binomial theorem, which Remark 1.6 uses, and the square root in the geometric midpoint of Example 1.4, the lesson uses only the ordinary order and arithmetic of \(\R\).
58: Definition 1.1 (Fermi estimate)
59: A Fermi estimate of a quantity \(Q\) is an identity \(Q = x_1 x_2 \cdots x_k\) together with a factor range \([l_i, u_i]\) for each factor \(x_i\). The estimate is the set of products that the ranges allow. Its ends are the bounds of Theorem 1.2.
63: Assumption A1 (positive ranges)
64: Each factor satisfies \(0 < l_i \le x_i \le u_i\) for stated numbers \(l_i\) and \(u_i\). The estimator chooses the ranges, and the theorems below use them as given. No theorem tests whether a range is correct.
68: Theorem 1.2 (product bounds)
70: (H1) \(k \ge 1\) is an integer.
71: (H2) Assuming A1, \(x_1, \dots, x_k\) are real numbers with \(0 < l_i \le x_i \le u_i\) for each \(i\).
73: Conclusion: \(\prod_{i=1}^{k} l_i \le \prod_{i=1}^{k} x_i \le \prod_{i=1}^{k} u_i\). Both bounds are attained. The lower bound is the product when every \(x_i = l_i\), and the upper bound is the product when every \(x_i = u_i\). The interval \([\prod_i l_i, \prod_i u_i]\) is the product interval of the ranges.
77: Proof
78: First, a lemma. Let \(a, a', b, b'\) be real numbers with \(0 < a' \le a\) and \(0 < b' \le b\). Then \(a'b' \le ab\), because \(ab - a'b' = a(b - b') + b'(a - a')\), and both terms are nonnegative: \(a > 0\) with \(b \ge b'\), and \(b' > 0\) with \(a \ge a'\). Likewise, if \(0 < a \le A\) and \(0 < b \le B\), then \(ab \le AB\), because \(AB - ab = A(B - b) + b(A - a)\), and both terms are nonnegative.
79: Now induct on \(k\). For \(k = 1\) the claim is (H2). Assume the claim for \(k - 1\) factors and write \(P = \prod_{i < k} x_i\), \(L = \prod_{i < k} l_i\) and \(U = \prod_{i < k} u_i\). The induction hypothesis gives \(0 < L \le P \le U\). The lemma with \(a = P\), \(a' = L\), \(b = x_k\) and \(b' = l_k\) gives \(P x_k \ge L l_k\). The lemma with \(A = U\), \(B = u_k\), \(a = P\) and \(b = x_k\) gives \(P x_k \le U u_k\). This is the claim for \(k\) factors. Attainment: the point \(x_i = l_i\) for every \(i\) lies in the box of (H2), and its product is \(\prod_i l_i\). The same holds for \(u_i\). \(\blacksquare\)
82: Theorem 1.2 is a worst-case statement. It covers every combination of factor values that the ranges allow, and it assigns no probability to any of them. Its proof uses only the ranges, so it holds whatever the factors are assumed to depend on. Section 4 returns to this point.
85: Proposition 1.3 (log-error widths add)
87: (H1) and (H2) of Theorem 1.2, so that A1 holds.
89: Conclusion: \(\ln\!\big(\prod_i u_i \big/ \prod_i l_i\big) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \ln(u_i / l_i)\) . The left side is the log-error width of the product interval, and each term on the right is the log-error width of a factor range.
93: Proof
94: All ends are positive. Finite products of positive numbers rearrange freely, so \(\prod_i u_i / \prod_i l_i = \prod_i (u_i / l_i)\), and every factor \(u_i / l_i\) is positive. The logarithm turns products into sums: \(\ln(ab) = \ln a + \ln b\) for positive \(a\) and \(b\), which is the defining property of the logarithm (module M1, exponential and logarithm functions ). Induction on \(k\) then gives the sum. \(\blacksquare\)
98: Example 1.4 (quote updates in one day)
99: Take three assumed ranges. Symbols quoted lie in \([20, 60]\). Quote updates per symbol per minute lie in \([2, 10]\). Minutes of quoting per day lie in \([300, 390]\). These are assumptions of the example. By Theorem 1.2 the product, which is the number of quote updates in a day under these assumptions, lies in \([20 \cdot 2 \cdot 300,\ 60 \cdot 10 \cdot 390] = [12\,000,\ 234\,000]\). The ratio of the ends is \(234\,000 / 12\,000 = 19.5\), so the log-error width is \(\ln 19.5\). The factor widths are \(\ln 3\), \(\ln 5\) and \(\ln 1.3\), and by Proposition 1.3 they sum to \(\ln 19.5\).
100: On the log scale the midpoint of \([\ln 12\,000, \ln 234\,000]\) maps back to the geometric midpoint of the product interval, \(\sqrt{12\,000 \cdot 234\,000}\), which is 52 990.57 when displayed to two decimals. Lab 1, Part 1 prints the bounds, the width and the factor widths, and checks the sum.
103: 2 Which factor to refine
105: Proposition 1.3 turns the question of refinement into arithmetic. Narrowing one factor's range by a smaller subrange lowers the total log-error width by exactly the drop in that factor's own width. The total does not depend on the order in which factors are refined. A factor whose own width is already small can lower the total by at most that small width, whatever the other factors are.
107: Lab 1, Part 2 narrows one factor at a time in Example 1.4. Symbols quoted from \([20, 60]\) to \([20, 40]\) lowers the total width from \(\ln 19.5 = 2.9704\) to \(2.5649\), a reduction of \(0.4055\). Quote updates from \([2, 10]\) to \([4, 8]\) lowers it to \(2.0541\), a reduction of \(0.9163\), which is 30.8 percent of the baseline total. Minutes from \([300, 390]\) to \([330, 360]\) lowers it to \(2.7951\), a reduction of \(0.1754\), which is 5.9 percent of the baseline. In this finite comparison the largest reduction comes from the factor with the largest log-error width. That is a statement about these three narrowings. It is not a general rule, and Research problem R3 gives a case where the factor with the larger log-error width does not give the larger reduction.
109: 3 Mental bounds for powers
111: A mental estimate of \((1 + x)^n\) for small \(x\) keeps the first-order part of the expansion, \(1 + nx\). The bound below says how large the error of that estimate can be, for \(x \ge 0\) and integer \(n \ge 2\).
114: Assumption A2 (Lagrange form of Taylor's theorem)
115: For a given \(x \ne 0\) and \(f\) twice differentiable on an interval containing \(0\) and \(x\), there is \(\xi\) strictly between \(0\) and \(x\) with \(f(x) = f(0) + f'(0)\,x + \tfrac12 f''(\xi)\,x^2\). This is the Taylor theorem with Lagrange remainder of module M2 ( Taylor's theorem with remainder ). It is used here as a known result, and the lesson does not reprove it.
119: Theorem 1.5 (second-order remainder of \((1+x)^n\))
121: (H1) \(n \ge 2\) is an integer.
122: (H2) \(x \ge 0\) is real.
123: (H3) Assuming A2 for \(x > 0\), applied to \(f(t) = (1 + t)^n\) on \([0, x]\). The case \(x = 0\) needs no A2, and the proof treats it directly.
125: Conclusion: \(0 \le (1 + x)^n - 1 - nx \le \dfrac{n(n-1)}{2}\,(1 + x)^{n-2}\,x^2\).
129: Proof
130: If \(x = 0\), both sides of the first inequality are 0 and the second is 0. Let \(x > 0\). The function \(f(t) = (1 + t)^n\) is defined and twice differentiable for \(t > -1\), with \(f'(t) = n(1 + t)^{n-1}\) and \(f''(t) = n(n-1)(1 + t)^{n-2}\). Assuming A2, there is \(\xi\) with \(0 < \xi < x\) such that \((1 + x)^n = 1 + nx + \tfrac12 n(n-1)(1 + \xi)^{n-2} x^2\), because \(f(0) = 1\) and \(f'(0) = n\). Hence \((1 + x)^n - 1 - nx = \tfrac12 n(n-1)(1 + \xi)^{n-2} x^2\). Since \(\xi > 0\) and \(n - 2 \ge 0\), the power satisfies \(0 < 1 \le (1 + \xi)^{n-2} \le (1 + x)^{n-2}\), which gives both inequalities. \(\blacksquare\)
134: Remark 1.6
135: For \(n = 2\) the exponent \(n - 2\) is 0. The bound equals \(x^2\), and the remainder is exactly \((1 + x)^2 - 1 - 2x = x^2\), so the upper inequality holds with equality for every \(x \ge 0\). For \(n \ge 3\) and \(x > 0\) the proof gives a strict upper inequality, because \(\xi < x\) and \((1 + \xi)^{n-2} < (1 + x)^{n-2}\); Lab 1, Part 3 prints the remainder and the bound for \(n = 5\) and \(n = 10\), and the printed remainder is below the printed bound in each of those cases. The identity \((1 + x)^n - 1 - nx = \sum_{j=2}^{n} \binom{n}{j} x^j\), which follows from the binomial theorem, gives the same remainder and is checked exactly in the lab.
139: Example 1.7 (ten compounding steps)
140: Take \(n = 10\) and \(x = 1/100\). The first-order estimate is \(1 + nx = 1.1\). Lab 1, Part 3 prints \((1.01)^{10}\) to ten places as \(1.1046221254\) (the exact value is \(1.10462212541\dots\)), the remainder \(0.0046221254\) and the bound \(0.0048728552\). The remainder is below the bound, as Theorem 1.5 requires. The ratio of the remainder to the bound is \(0.0046221254 / 0.0048728552 = 0.9485\) to four places. Measured against the remainder, the bound exceeds it by \(0.0048728552 / 0.0046221254 - 1 = 0.054\), that is 5.4 percent of the remainder, in this case.
141: At \(x = 1/20\) the same comparison is less favourable. The first-order estimate is \(1.5\), and the exact value of \((1.05)^{10}\) is \(1.6289\) to four places. The remainder \(0.1289\) is still below the bound \(0.1662\), but the estimate misses by \(0.1289 / 1.6289 = 0.079\), that is 7.9 percent, of the exact value. The bound grows with \(x\), and the lesson does not claim the estimate for large \(x\).
145: Lab 1 · Product bounds, narrowing and the Taylor bound
146: The lab computes Example 1.4 with fractions and prints the product bounds, 12000 and 234000, the ratio 19.5 and the log-error width 2.9704144656, which equals the sum of the three factor widths. Part 1 also checks 125 products on a grid of factor values against the bounds, which is a finite check of Theorem 1.2. Part 2 prints the total width after narrowing each factor alone. Part 3 prints, for n = 2, 5 and 10 and x = 0.01 and 0.05, the exact value, the first-order estimate, the remainder and the bound, and checks 0 ≤ remainder ≤ bound and the binomial-tail identity with exact fractions. Decimal values print to ten places. The printed block is compared at a tolerance of 1e-9, and the one float check in the code uses 1e-12.
147: """PC3 lab L01: bounds on a Fermi product, its log-error width, and a second-order bound for (1 + x)^n.
149: Standard library only. No randomness and no network. Every factor range below is an assumed range chosen for
150: this example. It is not an estimate of any market, and the lab reads no data file. Bounds and products are
151: fractions.Fraction, so every printed bound, every grid check and every Part 3 inequality is exact. The log-error
152: widths are floats. The one float check, that the factor widths sum to the product width, uses a tolerance of 1e-12.
153: Decimal values print to ten places: the ratio and the geometric midpoint in Part 1, the log-error widths, and the
154: Taylor values in Part 3.
156: The product bounds rest on the monotonicity argument of Theorem 1.2 on the page. The lab checks that every
157: product over a regular grid of factor values lies inside them. A finite grid is a check, not a proof.
158: """
160: from __future__ import annotations
162: import itertools
163: import math
164: from fractions import Fraction
166: Factor = tuple[str, Fraction, Fraction]
168: # Assumed ranges (lower end, upper end) for this example. They are not estimates of any market.
169: FACTORS: tuple[Factor, ...] = (
170: ("symbols quoted", Fraction(20), Fraction(60)),
171: ("quote updates per symbol per minute", Fraction(2), Fraction(10)),
172: ("minutes of quoting per day", Fraction(300), Fraction(390)),
173: )
175: # Narrowed ranges, one factor at a time, for Part 2.
176: NARROWED: dict[str, tuple[Fraction, Fraction]] = {
177: "symbols quoted": (Fraction(20), Fraction(40)),
178: "quote updates per symbol per minute": (Fraction(4), Fraction(8)),
179: "minutes of quoting per day": (Fraction(330), Fraction(360)),
180: }
182: GRID_POINTS = 5
185: def product_bounds(factors: tuple[Factor, ...]) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
186: """Lower and upper bounds of the product of the factor ranges. Every lower end must be positive."""
187: lower = Fraction(1)
188: upper = Fraction(1)
189: for name, lo, hi in factors:
190: if lo <= 0 or hi < lo:
191: raise ValueError(f"factor {name!r} needs 0 < lower end <= upper end")
192: lower *= lo
193: upper *= hi
194: return lower, upper
197: def log_width(lower: Fraction, upper: Fraction) -> float:
198: """The log-error width ln(upper / lower) of an interval with positive ends, in natural-log units."""
199: return math.log(float(upper / lower))
202: def grid_products(factors: tuple[Factor, ...]) -> list[Fraction]:
203: """Products over a regular grid of GRID_POINTS values in each range, both ends included."""
204: axes = [[lo + (hi - lo) * Fraction(k, GRID_POINTS - 1) for k in range(GRID_POINTS)] for _, lo, hi in factors]
205: products: list[Fraction] = []
206: for combo in itertools.product(*axes):
207: value = Fraction(1)
208: for x in combo:
209: value *= x
210: products.append(value)
211: return products
214: def remainder(n: int, x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
215: """(1 + x)^n - 1 - n x: the part of the expansion of (1 + x)^n after its first-order term."""
216: return (1 + x) ** n - 1 - n * x
219: def binomial_tail(n: int, x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
220: """The sum of C(n, j) x^j for j from 2 to n, computed term by term."""
221: return sum((math.comb(n, j) * x**j for j in range(2, n + 1)), Fraction(0))
224: def lagrange_bound(n: int, x: Fraction) -> Fraction:
225: """n(n - 1)/2 times (1 + x)^(n - 2) times x^2, the bound on the remainder for 0 <= x."""
226: return Fraction(n * (n - 1), 2) * (1 + x) ** (n - 2) * x * x
229: def part_one() -> None:
230: print("Part 1 · product of assumed factor ranges")
231: lower, upper = product_bounds(FACTORS)
232: print(f"product lower bound = {int(lower)}")
233: print(f"product upper bound = {int(upper)}")
234: print(f"upper / lower = {float(upper / lower):.10f}")
235: width = log_width(lower, upper)
236: print(f"log-error width ln(upper / lower) = {width:.10f}")
237: print(f"geometric midpoint sqrt(lower * upper) = {math.sqrt(float(lower * upper)):.10f}")
238: parts = [log_width(lo, hi) for _, lo, hi in FACTORS]
239: for (name, lo, hi), part in zip(FACTORS, parts, strict=True):
240: print(f" factor log-width, {name} ({lo} to {hi}) = {part:.10f}")
241: print(f"sum of factor log-widths = {sum(parts):.10f}")
242: print(f"sum equals product log-width: {abs(sum(parts) - width) < 1e-12}")
243: grid = grid_products(FACTORS)
244: inside = sum(lower <= p <= upper for p in grid)
245: print(f"grid products inside the bounds: {inside} of {len(grid)}")
248: def part_two() -> None:
249: print("Part 2 · narrowing one factor at a time (total log-error width)")
250: base = log_width(*product_bounds(FACTORS))
251: print(f"baseline total log-error width = {base:.10f}")
252: for name, lo_hi in NARROWED.items():
253: changed = tuple((n, lo_hi[0], lo_hi[1]) if n == name else (n, lo, hi) for n, lo, hi in FACTORS)
254: total = log_width(*product_bounds(changed))
255: print(f"narrow {name} to {lo_hi[0]} to {lo_hi[1]}: total {total:.10f}, reduction {base - total:.10f}")
258: def part_three() -> None:
259: print("Part 3 · second-order remainder of (1 + x)^n for 0 <= x")
260: ok_bound = True
261: ok_tail = True
262: for n in (2, 5, 10):
263: for x in (Fraction(1, 100), Fraction(1, 20)):
264: exact = (1 + x) ** n
265: rem = remainder(n, x)
266: bound = lagrange_bound(n, x)
267: ok_bound = ok_bound and 0 <= rem <= bound
268: ok_tail = ok_tail and rem == binomial_tail(n, x)
269: print(
270: f"n={n} x={float(x):.2f}: first-order 1 + n x = {float(1 + n * x):.10f}, "
271: f"exact = {float(exact):.10f}, remainder = {float(rem):.10f}, bound = {float(bound):.10f}"
272: )
273: print(f"0 <= remainder <= bound in every case: {ok_bound}")
274: print(f"remainder equals the binomial tail in every case: {ok_tail}")
277: def main() -> None:
278: part_one()
279: part_two()
280: part_three()
283: main()

314: 4 Where the canon breaks
315: The product bounds are attained (Theorem 1.2). The limits that matter for the results above are listed below.
317: A product interval is not a probability statement. Theorem 1.2 holds for every combination of factor values. It gives no probability to any of them, so a width of \(\ln 19.5\) does not say where the product is likely to lie inside \([12\,000, 234\,000]\). A statement about likelihood needs a model of the joint behaviour of the factors, and this lesson does not supply one.
318: Correlated errors. Theorem 1.2 needs no assumption about how the factors depend on one another, so it holds when they are correlated. It says nothing about their joint behaviour. If several factors share a common cause, a model that treats them as separate ranges can misdescribe the product, and the lesson makes no claim about the size of that effect.
319: The Taylor bound is stated for nonnegative \(x\). Theorem 1.5 covers \(x \ge 0\) and integer \(n \ge 2\). The lesson does not state a bound for negative \(x\), and the bound grows with \(x\), as Example 1.7 shows at \(x = 1/20\).
324: 5 Misconceptions
327: “A more precise factor makes the estimate much more accurate, whatever the other factors are.”
328: Under Proposition 1.3 the total log-error width is the sum of the factor widths, so refining one factor lowers the total by that factor's drop, and the drop does not depend on the other factors. As a share of the baseline total, which includes the other factors' widths, the same drop is smaller when the other factors are wider, so the relative gain from refining does depend on them. In Example 1.4 narrowing minutes from \([300, 390]\) to \([330, 360]\) removes 0.1754 of the total width, which is 5.9 percent of 2.9704. Narrowing quote updates from \([2, 10]\) to \([4, 8]\) removes 0.9163, which is 30.8 percent. Lab 1, Part 2 prints both reductions. Among the three narrowings of Lab 1, Part 2, refining the quote-updates factor removes the most width.
331: “The product interval of a Fermi estimate is a confidence interval.”
332: Theorem 1.2 is a worst-case statement over every combination of values in the ranges, and it assigns no probability to any combination (Section 4). The proof uses only the ranges, so no probability can come out of it. A confidence statement needs a model that Theorem 1.2 does not contain.
335: “The mental estimate \(1 + nx\) is accurate enough without a bound.”
336: The error is \((1 + x)^n - 1 - nx\), and it depends on \(n\) and \(x\). At \(n = 10\) and \(x = 1/20\) the estimate is 1.5 and the exact value is 1.6289 to four places, so the miss is 0.1289. Theorem 1.5 gives 0.1662 as an upper bound on that miss, and Lab 1, Part 3 prints both numbers. Without a bound, the estimate alone does not say how large the miss can be.

348: M1 · Product bounds for three ranges
349: Let \(x_1 \in [2, 3]\), \(x_2 \in [5, 7]\) and \(x_3 \in [1/2, 1]\). Give the bounds of Theorem 1.2 for \(x_1 x_2 x_3\), and the log-error width of the product interval in closed form.
350: Solution
351: The lower bound is \(2 \cdot 5 \cdot \tfrac12 = 5\) and the upper bound is \(3 \cdot 7 \cdot 1 = 21\), so the product lies in \([5, 21]\). The log-error width is \(\ln(21/5) = \ln 4.2\). The factor widths are \(\ln(3/2)\), \(\ln(7/5)\) and \(\ln 2\), and their sum is \(\ln(1.5 \cdot 1.4 \cdot 2) = \ln 4.2\), which agrees with Proposition 1.3.
355: M2 · A two-factor width
356: For \(x_1 \in [1, 4]\) and \(x_2 \in [2, 8]\), give the product interval and its log-error width, and check the width against the sum of the factor widths.
357: Solution
358: The product interval is \([1 \cdot 2, 4 \cdot 8] = [2, 32]\). Its ratio is \(32/2 = 16\), so the log-error width is \(\ln 16\), which is 2.7726 to four places. The factor widths are \(\ln(4/1) = \ln 4\) and \(\ln(8/2) = \ln 4\), and \(\ln 4 + \ln 4 = \ln 16\), as Proposition 1.3 requires.
362: M3 · The Taylor bound by hand
363: For \(n = 3\) and \(x = 1/10\), compute \((1 + x)^3 - 1 - 3x\) exactly, and compute the bound of Theorem 1.5. Check the inequality.
364: Solution
365: Here \((11/10)^3 = 1331/1000\), so the remainder is \(1331/1000 - 1 - 3/10 = 31/1000\), which is 0.031. The bound is \(\tfrac{3 \cdot 2}{2} \cdot (11/10)^{1} \cdot (1/100) = 3 \cdot \tfrac{11}{10} \cdot \tfrac{1}{100} = 33/1000\), which is 0.033. Since \(31/1000 \le 33/1000\), the inequality of Theorem 1.5 holds.

374: Let \(0 < l \le x \le u\). Prove that \(1/u \le 1/x \le 1/l\). Then let \(a \in [a_l, a_u]\) and \(b \in [b_l, b_u]\) with all ends positive, and use Theorem 1.2 to bound \(a/b\). State every hypothesis you use.
375: Solution
376: Hypotheses used: \(0 < l \le x \le u\), and positivity of the ends for \(a\) and \(b\). First, \(1/l - 1/x = (x - l)/(lx) \ge 0\) because \(x \ge l > 0\), so \(1/x \le 1/l\). Similarly \(1/x - 1/u = (u - x)/(xu) \ge 0\), so \(1/u \le 1/x\). For the quotient, \(1/b\) is a factor with range \([1/b_u, 1/b_l]\), and its ends are positive. Theorem 1.2 applied to the two factors \(a\) and \(1/b\) gives \(a_l / b_u \le a/b \le a_u / b_l\), with both ends attained.
380: P2 · Bernoulli's inequality for nonnegative x
381: Prove that \((1 + x)^n \ge 1 + nx\) for every integer \(n \ge 1\) and every real \(x \ge 0\). List the hypotheses that the induction uses.
382: Solution
383: Hypotheses used: \(n\) is a positive integer and \(x \ge 0\), so \(1 + x > 0\). For \(n = 1\) the two sides are equal. Assume \((1 + x)^n \ge 1 + nx\). Multiplying both sides by \(1 + x > 0\) gives \((1 + x)^{n+1} \ge (1 + nx)(1 + x) = 1 + (n+1)x + nx^2\). Since \(nx^2 \ge 0\), the right side is at least \(1 + (n+1)x\). This completes the induction.
387: P3 · The binomial-tail identity
388: For an integer \(n \ge 2\) and real \(x\), prove that \((1 + x)^n - 1 - nx = \sum_{j=2}^{n} \binom{n}{j} x^j\). Use the binomial theorem \((1 + x)^n = \sum_{j=0}^{n} \binom{n}{j} x^j\) as a known fact.
389: Solution
390: Hypotheses used: \(n \ge 2\) is an integer, and the binomial theorem holds for real \(x\). In the binomial expansion the \(j = 0\) term is \(\binom{n}{0} x^0 = 1\) and the \(j = 1\) term is \(\binom{n}{1} x = nx\). Subtracting these two terms and 1 from both sides leaves exactly the sum from \(j = 2\) to \(n\).

421: Solution
422: Take \([1, 2]\) for both factors. The product interval is \([1, 4]\), with ratio 4. Each factor has log-error width \(\ln 2\), and \(\ln 2 + \ln 2 = \ln 4\), which is the log-error width of the product interval, as Proposition 1.3 states.
428: Research
430: R1 · A stated threshold for refinement
431: Open design question. Refining factors does not all change the total by the same amount. Write a criterion that a presenter could state before refining a factor. The criterion must (a) give a numerical threshold on the factor's log-error width, (b) justify the threshold from Proposition 1.3, and (c) say what changes in the total when the threshold is crossed. Apply it to the three ranges of Example 1.4.
432: Solution
433: Criterion: refine a factor when its log-error width is more than half of the total width. Justification: by Proposition 1.3 the total is the sum of the factor widths. If factor \(i\) has width \(w_i > \tfrac12 \sum_j w_j\), then \(w_i > \sum_{j \ne i} w_j\). Narrowing factor \(i\) can remove at most \(w_i\) from the total, and narrowing all the other factors together can remove at most \(\sum_{j \ne i} w_j\), so the single factor has the larger possible effect. Application to Example 1.4: the total width is \(\ln 19.5 = 2.9704\), and half of it is 1.4852. The quote-updates width \(\ln 5 = 1.6094\) exceeds that half, so the criterion selects that factor. The symbols width \(\ln 3 = 1.0986\) and the minutes width \(\ln 1.3 = 0.2624\) do not. A presenter who uses a different threshold must justify it in the same way.
437: R2 · Which hypotheses the product bound uses
438: Open question. Does Theorem 1.2 need the factors to be independent, or to have any joint distribution? List every hypothesis the proof uses, and decide the question. The answer must name each hypothesis the proof uses and say which of them is an assumption about the joint behaviour of the factors.
439: Solution
440: The proof uses only (H1), the integer \(k \ge 1\), and (H2), the bounds \(0 < l_i \le x_i \le u_i\) for each \(i\). The lemma uses only inequalities between positive reals, and the induction applies the lemma to the partial product and the next factor. No independence, no joint distribution and no probability appears. So the answer is no: the bound needs no assumption about joint behaviour, and it also says nothing about joint behaviour. A claim that depends on the joint distribution requires a separate assumption, which this theorem does not supply.
444: R3 · Does the factor with the larger log-error width always give the larger reduction?
445: Open question with a stated criterion. Decide whether, for every pair of factors and every narrowing of each, the factor with the larger log-error width gives the larger reduction in the total. Criterion: give a proof, or a pair of factors and narrowings that shows the claim false, with each log-error width computed.
446: Solution
447: The claim is false. Let factor A have range \([1, 3]\), with width \(\ln 3 = 1.0986\), which is the larger width. Narrow A to \([1, 2]\), a reduction of \(\ln(3/2) = 0.4055\). Let factor B have range \([1, 2.5]\), with width \(\ln 2.5 = 0.9163\), which is smaller than A's width. Narrow B to \([1, 1.01]\), a reduction of \(\ln(2.5/1.01) = 0.9063\). Factor B, with the smaller width, gives the larger reduction, so the claim does not hold for every narrowing. Lab 1, Part 2 compares only three narrowings, and its result is a finite comparison.
