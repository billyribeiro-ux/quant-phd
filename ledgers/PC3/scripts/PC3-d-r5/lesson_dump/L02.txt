  51: A puzzle that asks for the chance of one event once another is known states that condition in words; the words may not fix the sampling rule, so the answer depends on it. It states how a probability is restricted to a known event on a finite sample space, derives the formula that reverses a conditional (Proposition 2.4) and the formula that splits a probability over a partition (Proposition 2.5), and applies both to coin, urn and Monty Hall puzzles under two host rules (Examples 2.6 to 2.9).   53: 1Finite sample spaces and conditional probability
  56: Definition 2.1 (events on a finite probability space)
  57: Let \(\Omega\) be a finite probability space, as defined in M4 Lesson 1, with weights \(p(\omega)\) that sum to 1. An event, as in M4 Lesson 1, is a subset \(A \subseteq \Omega\), and \(\Prob(A) = \sum_{\omega \in A} p(\omega)\). The space has equally likely outcomes when \(p(\omega) = 1/|\Omega|\) for every \(\omega\), and then \(\Prob(A) = |A| / |\Omega|\).
  61: Assumption A1 (finite space)
  62: Every sample space in this lesson is finite, as in Definition 2.1. Every conditional probability is taken given an event \(B\) with \(\Prob(B) > 0\). No theorem below covers an infinite or continuous space.
  66: Definition 2.2 (conditional probability)
  67: Assuming A1, and \(\Prob(B) > 0\), the conditional probability of \(A\) given \(B\) is \(\Prob(A \mid B) = \Prob(A \cap B) / \Prob(B)\).
  71: Remark 2.3 (the M4 definition specialized)
  72: Let \(X = \indic_A\) and \(Y = \indic_B\) be the indicators of the two events. The M4 conditional distribution of \(X\) given \(Y = 1\) is \(\Prob(X = 1 \mid Y = 1) = \Prob(X = 1, Y = 1) / \Prob(Y = 1)\), which is Definition 2.2. Definition 2.2 is therefore the elementary case of that definition, and the two agree.
  76: Proposition 2.4 (Bayes' rule)
  78: (H1) Assuming A1, \(\Prob(A) > 0\) and \(\Prob(B) > 0\).
  80: Conclusion: \(\Prob(A \mid B) = \dfrac{\Prob(B \mid A)\,\Prob(A)}{\Prob(B)}\). This is Bayes' rule.
  84: Proof
  85: Definition 2.2 applied to \(A\) given \(B\) and to \(B\) given \(A\) gives \(\Prob(A \cap B) = \Prob(A \mid B)\,\Prob(B)\) and \(\Prob(A \cap B) = \Prob(B \mid A)\,\Prob(A)\). Both conditional probabilities are defined by (H1). Equating the two expressions for \(\Prob(A \cap B)\) and dividing by \(\Prob(B) > 0\) gives the claim. \(\blacksquare\)
  89: Proposition 2.5 (law of total probability)
  91: (H1) Assuming A1, \(A_1, \dots, A_m\) are events that partition \(\Omega\), so that they are disjoint and their union is \(\Omega\).
  92: (H2) \(\Prob(A_i) > 0\) for each \(i\).
  94: Conclusion: for every event \(B\), \(\Prob(B) = \sum_{i=1}^{m} \Prob(B \mid A_i)\,\Prob(A_i)\).
  98: Proof
  99: By (H1), \(B\) is the disjoint union of the sets \(B \cap A_i\), so \(\Prob(B) = \sum_i \Prob(B \cap A_i)\), because \(\Prob\) is a sum of weights over the outcomes of each event. By Definition 2.2 and (H2), \(\Prob(B \cap A_i) = \Prob(B \mid A_i)\,\Prob(A_i)\). Substituting gives the claim. \(\blacksquare\)
 102: 2Coins, urns and symmetry
 105: Assumption A2 (fair independent flips)
 106: Two flips of a fair coin are independent, so each of the four sequences \(HH\), \(HT\), \(TH\) and \(TT\) has probability \(1/4\). This is the product of the two fair-flip probabilities, and the space has equally likely outcomes.
 110: Example 2.6 (two flips, two readings)
 111: Assume A2. The event "at least one head" is \(\{HH, HT, TH\}\), with probability \(3/4\). The event "both heads" is \(\{HH\}\). By Definition 2.2, \(\Prob(\text{both heads} \mid \text{at least one head}) = (1/4)/(3/4) = 1/3\). The event "the first flip is heads" is \(\{HH, HT\}\), with probability \(1/2\), and \(\Prob(\text{both heads} \mid \text{first flip heads}) = (1/4)/(1/2) = 1/2\). The two conditions differ, so the two answers differ. Lab 1, Part 4 enumerates the four outcomes and prints both values.
 115: Example 2.7 (two draws from an urn, by symmetry)
 116: An urn holds three red balls and two blue balls. Two balls are drawn in order, without replacement. Model the draws as one of the 20 ordered pairs of distinct balls, with every pair equally likely, which is the equal-weight space of Definition 2.1. Let the event "second red" be the set of pairs whose second entry is red. The map that swaps the two entries of each pair is a bijection of the 20 pairs, and it carries the event "second red" onto the event "first red". Since every pair has the same weight, \(\Prob(\text{second red}) = \Prob(\text{first red})\). Counting gives \(12/20 = 3/5\) for each. Lab 1, Part 4 enumerates the pairs and prints both values and the equality. The event "first red and second red" has probability \(6/20 = 3/10\).
 117: The symmetry does not extend to conditional probabilities. Given that the first ball is blue, one blue and three red balls remain, and the second draw is red with probability \(\Prob(\text{second red} \mid \text{first blue}) = 3/4\), which Lab 1, Part 4 prints.
 121: Example 2.8 (Monty Hall under two host rules)
 122: A car is behind one of three doors, each equally likely. You pick a door. The host then opens a door other than yours that hides a goat. You may keep your door or switch to the remaining unopened door. Under rule A, the host always opens a goat door, and chooses uniformly when two goat doors are available. Under rule B, the host opens one of the two unpicked doors uniformly at random, and the game is analysed on the event that the revealed door hides a goat.
 123: Rule A. Staying wins exactly when your pick is the car, which has probability \(1/3\). If your pick is a goat, the host must open the other goat, and the remaining unopened door is the car. So switching wins exactly when your pick is a goat, which has probability \(2/3\). Both probabilities are the same for every first pick, because the car is equally likely to be behind each door. Lab 1, Part 2 enumerates all nine (car, pick) pairs with the host's choices and prints \(1/3\) for staying and \(2/3\) for switching.
 124: Rule B. The event that the revealed door hides a goat has probability \(2/3\). If your pick is the car, the host's door is always a goat, so this contributes \(1/3\), and staying wins on all of it. If your pick is a goat, the host reveals the goat with probability \(1/2\), contributing \(1/3\), and switching wins on all of it. Conditioning on the goat event, each conditional probability is \((1/3)/(2/3) = 1/2\). Lab 1, Part 2 prints \(1/2\) and \(1/2\).
 128: Example 2.9 (a bag chosen by a coin)
 129: A fair coin chooses bag 1, which holds three red and one blue ball, or bag 2, which holds one red and three blue. A ball is drawn from the chosen bag, and it is red. By Proposition 2.5, \(\Prob(\text{red}) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac34 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac14 = \tfrac12\). By Proposition 2.4, \(\Prob(\text{bag 1} \mid \text{red}) = (\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac34)/\tfrac12 = \tfrac34\). Lab 1, Part 3 enumerates the four (bag, colour) outcomes with their weights and prints \(3/4\) by enumeration and \(3/4\) by Bayes' rule.
 133: Lab 1 · The drill set, Monty Hall, the urn and the bag, by enumeration
 134: The lab has four parts, all exact fractions. Part 1 generates 12 items from seed 0. Each item is an event about 3 to 8 fair flips, and its probability is computed twice, by enumerating every sequence and by a closed formula. The printed score is 12 of 12 if the two methods agree on every item. Part 2 enumerates Monty Hall under rule A and rule B and prints 1/3 and 2/3 for rule A, and 1/2 and 1/2 for rule B. Part 3 enumerates the bag example and prints 1/2 for the probability of red, and 3/4 for the posterior by enumeration and by Bayes' rule. Part 4 prints the two-flip conditionals 1/3 and 1/2, the reverse conditional 1, the urn probabilities 3/5, 3/5 and 3/10, and the urn conditional 3/4.

 137: Standard library only. The drill items are generated from random.Random(SEED), so the same seed gives the same

 139: flips (exact enumeration) and by a closed-form formula. The score counts the items on which the two agree. The
 140: items are generated items, not data, and no file is read. Every probability is a fractions.Fraction.
 142: Part 2 enumerates every (car door, picked door, host door) triple. Part 3 enumerates every (bag, colour) pair.

 144: enumeration computes each stated probability exactly. The lab does not estimate any probability by simulation.

 162: class Item:
 163: """One drill item: the event, the number of flips, and the parameter of the event."""
 165: number: int
 166: text: str

 172: def make_items(seed: int, count: int) -> list[Item]:
 173: """Generate count drill items from the seed. Each item has 3 to 8 fair flips."""
 174: rng = random.Random(seed)
 175: items: list[Item] = []
 176: for number in range(1, count + 1):
 177: flips = rng.randint(3, 8)
 178: if rng.random() < 0.5:

 181: text = f"at least {k} {noun} in {flips} fair flips"

 190: def enumerate_probability(item: Item) -> Fraction:
 191: """The exact probability of the item's event, by counting all 2^flips equally likely sequences."""
 192: favourable = 0
 193: for seq in product((0, 1), repeat=item.flips):
 194: heads = sum(seq)
 195: if item.kind == "at-least":
 196: hit = heads >= item.arg
 197: else:
 198: hit = seq[item.arg - 1] == 1 and all(s == 0 for s in seq[: item.arg - 1])
 199: favourable += hit
 200: return Fraction(favourable, 2**item.flips)
 203: def closed_form(item: Item) -> Fraction:
 204: """The same probability from the formulas: a binomial tail, or 1/2^j for the first head on flip j."""
 205: if item.kind == "at-least":
 206: return Fraction(sum(math.comb(item.flips, i) for i in range(item.arg, item.flips + 1)), 2**item.flips)
 207: return Fraction(1, 2**item.arg)

 211: print(f"Part 1 · drill set from seed {SEED}, {ITEM_COUNT} generated items")
 212: items = make_items(SEED, ITEM_COUNT)
 213: agree = 0
 214: for item in items:
 215: exact = enumerate_probability(item)
 216: formula = closed_form(item)
 217: same = exact == formula
 218: agree += same
 219: print(f"item {item.number:2d}: {item.text}: probability {exact}, formula {formula}, equal {same}")
 220: print(f"closed form equals enumeration on {agree} of {len(items)} items")
 223: def switch_probabilities(rule: str) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
 224: """Stay and switch win probabilities, given that the host has opened a goat door.
 226: rule "always-goat": the host always opens a goat door among the doors not picked (a uniform choice when
 227: two goat doors remain). rule "random": the host opens one of the two unpicked doors uniformly at random,
 228: and the outcomes in which that door hides the car are discarded, which conditions on a goat being revealed.
 229: """
 230: stay = Fraction(0)
 231: switch = Fraction(0)
 232: total = Fraction(0)
 233: for car, pick in product(DOORS, DOORS):
 234: others = [d for d in DOORS if d != pick]
 235: for opened in others:
 236: if rule == "always-goat":
 237: goats = [d for d in others if d != car]
 238: if opened not in goats:
 239: continue
 240: p_open = Fraction(1, len(goats))
 241: elif rule == "random":
 242: if opened == car:
 243: continue
 244: p_open = Fraction(1, 2)
 245: else:
 246: raise ValueError(f"unknown host rule {rule!r}")
 247: weight = Fraction(1, 3) * Fraction(1, 3) * p_open
 248: total += weight
 249: switch_door = next(d for d in others if d != opened)
 250: if pick == car:
 251: stay += weight
 252: if switch_door == car:
 253: switch += weight
 254: return stay / total, switch / total
 257: def part_two() -> None:

 261: stay_b, switch_b = switch_probabilities("random")
 262: print(f"host opens at random, given a goat: P(stay wins) = {stay_b}, P(switch wins) = {switch_b}")
 263: print(f"the two host rules give different answers: {switch_a != switch_b}")
 266: def part_three() -> None:
 267: print("Part 3 · Bayes' rule on two bags, exact enumeration of (bag, colour)")
 268: bags = {"bag 1": (3, 1), "bag 2": (1, 3)}  # (red, blue) counts
 269: joint: dict[tuple[str, str], Fraction] = {}
 270: for bag, (red, blue) in bags.items():
 271: n = red + blue
 272: joint[(bag, "red")] = Fraction(1, 2) * Fraction(red, n)
 273: joint[(bag, "blue")] = Fraction(1, 2) * Fraction(blue, n)
 274: p_red = sum(v for (_, colour), v in joint.items() if colour == "red")
 275: posterior = joint[("bag 1", "red")] / p_red
 276: print(f"P(red) = {p_red}")
 277: print(f"P(bag 1 | red) by enumeration = {posterior}")
 278: prior = Fraction(1, 2)
 279: likelihood = Fraction(3, 4)
 280: print(f"P(bag 1 | red) by Bayes' rule = {likelihood * prior / p_red}")
 283: def conditional(
 284: outcomes: Sequence[tuple[str, ...]],
 285: event: Callable[[tuple[str, ...]], bool],
 286: given: Callable[[tuple[str, ...]], bool],
 287: ) -> Fraction:
 288: """P(event | given) over equally likely outcomes: the count of both over the count of the given event."""
 289: kept = [o for o in outcomes if given(o)]
 290: both = [o for o in kept if event(o)]
 291: return Fraction(len(both), len(kept))
 294: def part_four() -> None:

 363: 3Where the canon breaks
 364: Each of the following is a place where the wording of a puzzle must be fixed before an answer can be stated.
 366: The sampling rule decides the answer. Example 2.8 has the same three doors and the same first pick, and switching wins with probability \(2/3\) under rule A and \(1/2\) under rule B. A puzzle that does not state how the host chooses has no single answer, and the answer must state its rule.
 367: Symmetry needs a symmetry of the model. Example 2.7 uses a swap of the two positions, which preserves the equally likely weights. The same argument does not give conditional probabilities, as the blue-first case of Example 2.7 shows.
 368: The conditioning event must be stated. "At least one head" and "the first flip is heads" describe different events, and Example 2.6 gives different answers for them. The lesson does not decide which event a report describes.
 369: Finite spaces only. Assumption A1 limits every result to finite spaces. Conditional probability on an event of probability zero, or on a continuous space, is outside Definition 2.2, and this lesson does not treat it.
 374: 4Misconceptions
 377: “Monty Hall is 1/2 by symmetry, because only two doors remain.”
 378: The doors are symmetric before the host acts, but the host's rule is not symmetric between the car and a goat. Under rule A switching wins with probability \(2/3\), by the derivation in Example 2.8, and Lab 1, Part 2 prints it. The value \(1/2\) is the answer under rule B, where the host's choice is random and the game is conditioned on the reveal.
 381: “‘At least one head’ and ‘the first flip is heads’ give the same conditional probability.”
 382: The conditioning events differ. Assumed fair and independent, the first gives \(\Prob(\text{both heads} \mid \text{at least one head}) = 1/3\), and the second gives \(\Prob(\text{both heads} \mid \text{first flip heads}) = 1/2\) (Example 2.6). Lab 1, Part 4 prints both values.
 385: “\(\Prob(A \mid B) = \Prob(B \mid A)\), so the two conditionals can be swapped.”
 386: For two flips, \(\Prob(\text{both heads} \mid \text{at least one head}) = 1/3\), while \(\Prob(\text{at least one head} \mid \text{both heads}) = 1\). Lab 1, Part 4 prints both. Bayes' rule (Proposition 2.4) is the exact relation between the two, and it needs \(\Prob(A)\) and \(\Prob(B)\) as well.

 396: Mechanics
 398: M1 · A conditional on a die
 399: Two fair six-sided dice are rolled, all 36 outcomes equally likely. Find \(\Prob(\text{sum} = 7 \mid \text{first die} = 3)\).
 400: Solution
 401: Hypotheses used: A1 and equally likely outcomes. The event "first die is 3" has 6 outcomes. Among them, "sum is 7" requires the second die to be 4, which is one outcome. By Definition 2.2, \(\Prob(\text{sum} = 7 \mid \text{first die} = 3) = (1/36)/(6/36) = 1/6\).
 405: M2 · Bayes' rule with given numbers
 406: Let \(\Prob(A) = 3/10\), \(\Prob(B \mid A) = 1/2\) and \(\Prob(B \mid A^c) = 1/5\). Find \(\Prob(A \mid B)\) exactly.
 407: Solution
 408: Hypotheses used: A1, and \(\Prob(A) > 0\) and \(\Prob(A^c) > 0\). By Proposition 2.5, \(\Prob(B) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{3}{10} + \tfrac15 \cdot \tfrac{7}{10} = \tfrac{15}{100} + \tfrac{14}{100} = \tfrac{29}{100}\). By Proposition 2.4, \(\Prob(A \mid B) = (\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{3}{10}) / \tfrac{29}{100} = \tfrac{15}{100} \cdot \tfrac{100}{29} = \tfrac{15}{29}\).
 412: M3 · Two draws without replacement
 413: An urn holds four red and two blue balls. Two balls are drawn without replacement, in order. Find the probability that both are red.
 414: Solution
 415: Hypotheses used: A1 and equally likely ordered draws. The first draw is red with probability \(4/6\). Given a red first draw, five balls remain, three of them red, so the second draw is red with probability \(3/5\). The product is \((4/6)(3/5) = 2/5\). Counting agrees: there are \(4 \cdot 3 = 12\) ordered red pairs out of \(6 \cdot 5 = 30\) ordered pairs.
 421: Proof
 423: P1 · Independence of events and conditionals
 424: Assume A1 and \(\Prob(B) > 0\). Prove that \(\Prob(A \mid B) = \Prob(A)\) if and only if \(\Prob(A \cap B) = \Prob(A)\Prob(B)\).
 425: Solution
 426: Hypotheses used: A1 and \(\Prob(B) > 0\). By Definition 2.2, \(\Prob(A \mid B) = \Prob(A)\) holds exactly when \(\Prob(A \cap B)/\Prob(B) = \Prob(A)\). Multiplying both sides by \(\Prob(B) > 0\) gives \(\Prob(A \cap B) = \Prob(A)\Prob(B)\). Each step is an equivalence, since multiplying and dividing by a positive number preserve equality.
 430: P2 · Law of total probability with three cells
 431: Let \(A_1, A_2, A_3\) partition \(\Omega\) with positive probabilities. Prove Proposition 2.5 for \(m = 3\) by writing out the three disjoint pieces of \(B\) and using additivity.
 432: Solution
 433: Hypotheses used: A1, the partition property, and positivity of each \(\Prob(A_i)\). Since the \(A_i\) cover \(\Omega\), \(B = (B \cap A_1) \cup (B \cap A_2) \cup (B \cap A_3)\). The three pieces are disjoint because the \(A_i\) are disjoint. Summing weights over the disjoint pieces gives \(\Prob(B) = \Prob(B \cap A_1) + \Prob(B \cap A_2) + \Prob(B \cap A_3)\). Each term equals \(\Prob(B \mid A_i)\Prob(A_i)\) by Definition 2.2, because \(\Prob(A_i) > 0\).
 437: P3 · Conditioning gives a probability measure
 438: Assume A1 and \(\Prob(B) > 0\). Define \(\Prob_B(A) = \Prob(A \mid B)\). Prove that \(\Prob_B(\Omega) = 1\), and that \(\Prob_B(A_1 \cup A_2) = \Prob_B(A_1) + \Prob_B(A_2)\) for disjoint \(A_1\) and \(A_2\).
 439: Solution
 440: Hypotheses used: A1, \(\Prob(B) > 0\), and the additivity of \(\Prob\) over disjoint events. First, \(\Omega \cap B = B\), so \(\Prob_B(\Omega) = \Prob(B)/\Prob(B) = 1\). Second, if \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) are disjoint, then \((A_1 \cup A_2) \cap B = (A_1 \cap B) \cup (A_2 \cap B)\), and these two pieces are disjoint. Additivity gives \(\Prob((A_1 \cup A_2) \cap B) = \Prob(A_1 \cap B) + \Prob(A_2 \cap B)\). Dividing by \(\Prob(B) > 0\) gives the additivity of \(\Prob_B\).

 448: E1 · A generated item checked by hand
 449: Lab 1, Part 1 prints the exact probability 57/64 for item 4, "at least 2 heads in 6 fair flips". Verify it by counting the complement.
 450: Solution
 451: Hypotheses used: six independent fair flips, so each of the \(2^6 = 64\) sequences has weight \(1/64\). The complement of "at least 2 heads" is "0 heads or 1 head". There is one sequence with 0 heads and \(\binom{6}{1} = 6\) with 1 head. So \(\Prob(\text{at least 2 heads}) = 1 - 1/64 - 6/64 = 57/64\). The lab's enumeration and its formula both print 57/64.
 455: E2 · Which conditional does the report state?
 456: From Lab 1, Part 4, the two coin conditionals are 1/3 and 1/2. A report says, "the coin was heads at least once, and I tell you one of the flips was heads, which one I don't say." Which of the two conditionals does the report describe, and why?
 457: Solution
 458: Hypotheses used: the report states an event, not a flip. "At least one head" is the event used for the first conditional, so the report describes the first conditional, with value 1/3. The second conditional needs the report to name the first flip. The report does not name a flip, so it does not describe the second event. The answer depends on the wording of the report, which is the point of Section 3.
 462: E3 · Differences between the two host rules
 463: From Lab 1, Part 2, find the difference between the switch-win probability under rule A and under rule B, and state what the difference measures.
 464: Solution
 465: The printed values are \(2/3\) under rule A and \(1/2\) under rule B. Their difference is \(2/3 - 1/2 = 1/6\). The difference is a comparison of two specific rules and does not describe any other rule.

 473: R1 · A sentence that fixes the Monty Hall rule
 474: Open design question. Write one sentence that a puzzle setter could print with the Monty Hall game, so that the answer is fixed by enumeration. Criterion: the sentence must determine the host's choice in every case that the enumeration of Example 2.8 covers, and the answer must follow from it without further assumption.
 475: Solution
 476: One sentence that meets the criterion: "After your pick, the host always opens a door that hides a goat and is not your pick; if both remaining doors hide goats, the host chooses between them with equal probability." This is rule A. It fixes the host's choice in every case, the cases where the pick is the car included, and the enumeration of Example 2.8 gives \(2/3\) for switching. A sentence describing rule B would need to say that the host opens a random door and that the game is analysed on the goat event, and it would give \(1/2\).
 480: R2 · Which conditioning event a report describes
 481: Open design question. Given a report about a finite experiment, state a rule for deciding which event its conditional probability refers to. Criterion: the rule must name the event from the wording alone, and must say what the rule does when two events are both consistent with the wording. Apply it to the two readings of Example 2.6.
 482: Solution
 483: Rule: the event is the one the report names by a noun phrase or a predicate about the outcome; if the wording names a particular flip, the event is about that flip; if the wording names only a property of the whole outcome, such as "at least one head", the event is about the whole outcome. When two events are both consistent with the wording, the report must give both conditional values or name one of them. Applied to Example 2.6: "at least one head" names a property of the whole outcome, so the event is \(\{HH, HT, TH\}\), and the conditional is 1/3. "The first flip is heads" names a particular flip, so the event is \(\{HH, HT\}\), and the conditional is 1/2.
 487: R3 · A puzzle whose answer differs from 1/2 under its own rule
 488: Open construction. Write a two-draw puzzle with three red balls and one blue ball, in which the conditional probability of a red second draw given a blue first draw is determined by the draw rule, and compute it exactly. Criterion: the rule must be stated, and the answer must be checked by enumeration.
 489: Solution
 490: Puzzle: an urn holds three red balls and one blue ball. Two balls are drawn in order without replacement, and the draws are equally likely as ordered pairs. Given that the first ball is blue, the urn holds three red balls and no blue balls. The second draw is therefore red with probability \(3/3 = 1\). Enumeration: the ordered pairs with a blue first entry are \((b, r_1)\), \((b, r_2)\) and \((b, r_3)\), all three with a red second entry, so the conditional probability is \(3/3 = 1\). The answer is not 1/2, because the blue first draw removes the blue ball from the urn, which the rule for the draws makes explicit.
