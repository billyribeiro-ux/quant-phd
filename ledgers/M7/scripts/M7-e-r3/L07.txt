0014 A plan of several choices can be found working backward one period at a time. This lesson defines a finite-horizon decision problem, its plans and its value function, and proves the principle of optimality: a plan that is optimal from a state has optimal later parts of the plan from the states it reaches. The proof gives the backward recursion. Lesson L08 calls that recursion the Bellman equation (its Definition 1.2). Lab 1 computes the recursion for a small problem and checks it against an enumeration of its plans. The lab shows where the principle stops: a greedy plan, which takes the largest period reward at each step, can have optimal later parts and still not be optimal.
0015 1Finite-horizon problems, plans and value
0016 Time runs over \(t = 0, 1, \ldots, H\), where the horizon \(H\) is a whole number with \(H \ge 1\). Rewards are real numbers, written as elements of \(\R\). The sets and maps below are fixed before any decision is made. Lab 1 uses an integer problem, and every amount in it is in whole cents or in utils, a label for an integer reward.
0017 Definition 1.1 (finite-horizon decision problem)
0018 A finite-horizon decision problem has the following data. A finite set \(S\) of states. For each \(s \in S\), a nonempty finite set \(A(s)\) of feasible actions. A transition map \(f\) with \(f(s,a) \in S\) for every \(s \in S\) and \(a \in A(s)\), so the next state is a known function of the current state and action; the transitions are deterministic. A period reward \(r(s,a) \in \R\) for each \(s\) and \(a \in A(s)\). A terminal reward \(B(s) \in \R\) for each \(s \in S\), paid at time \(H\).
0019 Definition 1.2 (plans, total reward and value)
0020 Let \(0 \le t \le H\) and \(s \in S\). A plan from \((t,s)\) is a sequence \(p = (a_t, \ldots, a_{H-1})\) for which the states \(s_t = s\) and \(s_{k+1} = f(s_k, a_k)\) satisfy \(a_k \in A(s_k)\) for each \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\). Its total reward is \[ J_t(p) = \sum_{k=t}^{H-1} r(s_k, a_k) + B(s_H). \] The value function is \(V_t(s) = \max_p J_t(p)\), the maximum over the plans from \((t,s)\). A plan from \((t,s)\) is optimal if \(J_t(p) = V_t(s)\). Because each \(A(s)\) is finite and nonempty and \(H\) is finite, the set of plans from \((t,s)\) is finite and nonempty, so the maximum exists.
0021 Remark 1.3 (where finiteness is used)
0022 Finiteness is used for the existence of the maxima in Definition 1.2 and Theorem 2.1. Over an infinite action set the largest value may not be attained. Take \(H = 1\), one state \(s\), \(A(s) = (0,1)\), \(r(s,a) = a\), \(f(s,a) = s\) and \(B(s) = 0\). Then \(V_0(s)\) is the supremum 1, and no plan attains it, because each \(a \in (0,1)\) gives the total \(a < 1\).
0023 Definition 1.4 (continuation and the principle of optimality)
0024 Let \(p = (a_t, \ldots, a_{H-1})\) be a plan from \((t,s)\), with states \(s_k\) along it. For \(t \le k \le H-1\), the continuation of \(p\) from time \(k\) is \((a_k, \ldots, a_{H-1})\), a plan from \((k, s_k)\). The principle of optimality is the statement that, if \(p\) is optimal from \((t,s)\), then for each \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\) the continuation of \(p\) from time \(k\) is optimal from \((k, s_k)\). Theorem 2.2 proves it for the problem of Definition 1.1. Remark 2.5 shows that the converse does not hold.
0025 2The backward recursion and the principle of optimality
0026 The next theorem is the backward recursion. Its proof runs downward in time, which is the form of mathematical induction from M1 that this lesson uses. Lesson L08 calls the equation in Theorem 2.1 the Bellman equation (Definition 1.2 of L08).
0027 Theorem 2.1 (backward recursion)
0028 Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, \(V_H(s) = B(s)\) for every \(s \in S\). For \(0 \le t \le H-1\) and every \(s \in S\), \[ V_t(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \bigl\{ r(s,a) + V_{t+1}\bigl(f(s,a)\bigr) \bigr\}. \]
0029 Proof
0030 The proof is by downward induction on \(t\). At \(t = H\) the only plan from \((H,s)\) is the empty plan, whose total reward is \(J_H = B(s)\), so \(V_H(s) = B(s)\).
0031 Suppose the formula holds at time \(t+1\), and let \(s \in S\). A plan from \((t,s)\) is a pair \((a, q)\), where \(a \in A(s)\) is the first action and \(q\) is a plan from \((t+1, f(s,a))\). Conversely, every such pair is a plan from \((t,s)\), because the state at time \(t+1\) is \(f(s,a)\). For such a pair the total reward splits as \[ J_t(a,q) = r(s,a) + J_{t+1}(q), \] since the first period contributes \(r(s,a)\) and the remaining periods and the terminal reward contribute \(J_{t+1}(q)\). Taking the maximum over pairs means taking the maximum over \(a \in A(s)\) of the maximum over \(q\): \[ V_t(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \Bigl\{ r(s,a) + \max_{q} J_{t+1}(q) \Bigr\} = \max_{a \in A(s)} \bigl\{ r(s,a) + V_{t+1}\bigl(f(s,a)\bigr) \bigr\}. \] Both maxima range over finite nonempty sets, so they exist.
0032 Theorem 2.2 (principle of optimality)
0033 Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, let \(0 \le t \le H-1\), let \(s \in S\), and let \(p^{*} = (a^{*}_t, \ldots, a^{*}_{H-1})\) be an optimal plan from \((t,s)\), with states \(s^{*}_k\) along it. Then for every \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\), the continuation of \(p^{*}\) from time \(k\) is optimal from \((k, s^{*}_k)\).
0034 Proof
0035 Fix \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\). Let \(c = (a^{*}_k, \ldots, a^{*}_{H-1})\) be the continuation of \(p^{*}\) from time \(k\), and let \(q\) be any plan from \((k, s^{*}_k)\). Form the plan \(p'\) that follows \(p^{*}\) up to time \(k\) and then follows \(q\). Its states up to time \(k\) are \(s^{*}_t, \ldots, s^{*}_k\), so the actions in the first part are feasible, and \(q\) starts from \(s^{*}_k\). So \(p'\) is a plan from \((t,s)\).
0036 Write \(\Sigma = \sum_{j=t}^{k-1} r(s^{*}_j, a^{*}_j)\). The total reward splits at time \(k\) for both plans: \[ J_t(p') = \Sigma + J_k(q), \qquad J_t(p^{*}) = \Sigma + J_k(c). \] Since \(p^{*}\) is optimal, \(J_t(p') \le J_t(p^{*})\), so \(J_k(q) \le J_k(c)\). This holds for every plan \(q\) from \((k, s^{*}_k)\). Since \(c\) is itself such a plan, \(J_k(c)\) is the maximum over these plans, so \(J_k(c) = V_k(s^{*}_k)\).
0037 Theorem 2.3 (first-step characterization)
0038 Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, let \(0 \le t \le H-1\), \(s \in S\), \(a \in A(s)\), and let \(q\) be a plan from \((t+1, f(s,a))\). The plan \((a, q)\) from \((t,s)\) is optimal if and only if both of the following hold: (i) \(r(s,a) + V_{t+1}(f(s,a)) = V_t(s)\), so \(a\) attains the maximum in Theorem 2.1; and (ii) \(q\) is optimal from \((t+1, f(s,a))\).
0039 Proof
0040 By the split in the proof of Theorem 2.1, \(J_t(a,q) = r(s,a) + J_{t+1}(q)\). Since \(J_{t+1}(q) \le V_{t+1}(f(s,a))\) by the definition of \(V_{t+1}\), and \(r(s,a) + V_{t+1}(f(s,a))\) is at most the maximum in Theorem 2.1, the chain \[ J_t(a,q) \le r(s,a) + V_{t+1}\bigl(f(s,a)\bigr) \le V_t(s) \] holds. The plan is optimal, meaning \(J_t(a,q) = V_t(s)\), exactly when both inequalities are equalities. The first is an equality exactly when (ii) holds, and the second exactly when (i) holds.
0041 Corollary 2.4 (an optimal plan from the recursion)
0042 Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, let \(0 \le t \le H\) and \(s \in S\). Form the plan from \((t,s)\) forward: at each time \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\) and state \(s_k\), choose an action that attains the maximum in Theorem 2.1 at \((k, s_k)\). The resulting plan is optimal from \((t,s)\).
0043 Proof
0044 Induction on the number of remaining periods \(H - t\). If \(H - t = 0\), the empty plan is the only plan and is optimal. Otherwise the plan has the form \((a, q)\), where \(a\) attains the maximum at \((t,s)\), and \(q\) is the plan built by the same rule from \((t+1, f(s,a))\). By the induction hypothesis \(q\) is optimal from \((t+1, f(s,a))\), and condition (i) of Theorem 2.3 holds because \(a\) attains the maximum. By Theorem 2.3 the plan \((a, q)\) is optimal.

0050 Remark 2.5 (the converse of the principle fails)
0051 Theorem 2.2 says that the continuations of an optimal plan are optimal. The converse does not hold: a plan whose continuations from each later time \(k > t\) are all optimal need not be optimal. Take \(H = 2\), \(S = \{x, y\}\), \(A(x) = \{a, b\}\), \(A(y) = \{c\}\), \(f(x,a) = f(x,b) = y\), \(f(y,c) = y\), \(r(x,a) = 0\), \(r(x,b) = 1\), \(r(y,c) = 0\), and \(B(x) = B(y) = 0\). Then \(V_1(y) = 0\), and \(V_0(x) = \max\{0 + 0,\, 1 + 0\} = 1\), attained only by \(b\). The plan \(p = (a, c)\) from \((0,x)\) has \(J_0(p) = 0 < 1\), so it is not optimal. Its continuation from time 1 is \((c)\) from \((1,y)\), the only plan from that state, so it is optimal. The principle gives a necessary condition for optimality, not a sufficient one.
0052 3Where the canon breaks
0053 Additive rewards. The proof of Theorem 2.2 uses that the total reward splits into a prefix and a continuation, \(J_t(p') = \Sigma + J_k(q)\). Under a different objective the split can fail, and the principle with it. Problem R2 gives an objective for which Theorem 2.2 is false.
0054 Finite sets and deterministic transitions. Remark 1.3 shows that a maximum over an infinite action set may fail to exist. This lesson also assumes that the next state is a known function of the current state and action. Problems with random transitions need a weighted average over the next states, which this lesson does not treat.
0055 A fixed horizon. Each result in §2 holds for one finite \(H\). This lesson does not treat an infinite horizon.
0056 Enumeration does not scale. Problem R3 shows that with \(m\) actions in every state, there are \(m^{H-t}\) plans from \((t,s)\). The recursion computes each \(V_t(s)\) from the \(|A(s)|\) candidate sums at \((t,s)\), so the whole table takes at most \(H\) times \(|S|\) times \(m\) candidate sums. Lab 1 uses the enumeration only as a check.
0057 4Misconceptions
0058 “The best first step is the one with the largest period reward.”
0059 At wealth 400 cents, the largest period reward is at consumption 400, worth 130 utils. The greedy plan takes it and earns a total of 130 utils. The optimal plan takes 100 cents in each period and earns 240 utils (greedy_total 130 and optimal_total 240 in Part C of Lab 1). The first step must be judged by its effect on the value of the state it leads to, which the recursion of Theorem 2.1 includes.
0060 “If every continuation of a plan from a later time is optimal, the plan is optimal.”
0061 Remark 2.5 gives a two-period problem in which the plan \((a, c)\) has an optimal continuation and is not optimal. Part C of Lab 1 shows the same effect: the greedy plan 400, 0, 0, 0 has its three continuations from t = 1, 2 and 3 printed as optimal, and greedy_plan_optimal False. The first action belongs to no continuation, so the continuations cannot judge it. Theorem 2.3 adds the missing condition, which is the optimal first action.
0062 “A state has the same value whatever the time left.”
0063 The recursion in Theorem 2.1 uses \(V_{t+1}\), so the value of a state depends on how many periods remain. Part A of Lab 1 shows the wealth-400 values 240, 220, 200, 130 and 4 utils at t = 0 to 4. A state's value at time \(t\) reflects the periods that are still to come.
0064 5Problem set
0065 Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems M1 to M3 use the two-period problem below. Its states are \(x\) and \(y\), with \(A(x) = \{u, v\}\), \(A(y) = \{w\}\), \(f(x,u) = y\), \(f(x,v) = x\), \(f(y,w) = y\), \(r(x,u) = 3\), \(r(x,v) = 5\), \(r(y,w) = 2\), \(B(x) = 0\) and \(B(y) = 1\), with \(H = 2\).
0066 Mechanics
0067 M1 · The value function of the two-period problem
0068 Compute \(V_1(x)\), \(V_1(y)\) and \(V_0(x)\) for the problem above.
0069 Solution
0070 At time 1 there is one period left. From \(x\), action \(u\) gives \(3 + B(y) = 4\) and action \(v\) gives \(5 + B(x) = 5\), so \(V_1(x) = 5\). From \(y\), the only action \(w\) gives \(2 + B(y) = 3\), so \(V_1(y) = 3\). At time 0, from \(x\), action \(u\) gives \(3 + V_1(y) = 6\) and action \(v\) gives \(5 + V_1(x) = 10\), so \(V_0(x) = 10\), attained by \(v\).
0071 M2 · The optimal plan from \((0,x)\)
0072 For the problem above, state the optimal plan from \((0,x)\) and its total reward.
0073 Solution
0074 Corollary 2.4 says to take a maximizing action at each step. At time 0 from \(x\), the maximizing action is \(v\), as M1 shows. The state stays \(x\), and at time 1 from \(x\) the maximizing action is again \(v\), since \(V_1(x) = 5\) comes from \(v\). The optimal plan is \((v, v)\), with total reward \(5 + 5 = 10\), which equals \(V_0(x)\).
0075 M3 · Every plan from \((0,x)\)
0076 List every plan from \((0,x)\) for the problem above, with its total reward, and confirm that the maximum is 10.
0077 Solution
0078 A plan from \((0,x)\) is a pair of actions. The plan \((u, w)\) moves to \(y\) and then stays there, with total \(3 + (2 + B(y)) = 3 + 3 = 6\). The plan \((v, u)\) stays at \(x\) and then moves to \(y\), with total \(5 + (3 + B(y)) = 5 + 4 = 9\). The plan \((v, v)\) stays at \(x\) twice, with total \(5 + 5 = 10\). The plan \((u, v)\) is not feasible, because after \(u\) the state is \(y\) and \(v\) is not in \(A(y)\). So there are three plans, with totals 6, 9 and 10, and the maximum is 10.
0079 Proof
0080 P1 · The last step of the recursion
0081 Prove the case \(t = H - 1\) of Theorem 2.1 directly from Definition 1.2, without downward induction.
0082 Solution
0083 A plan from \((H-1,s)\) is a single action \(a \in A(s)\), with states \(s\) and then \(f(s,a)\). Its total reward is \(r(s,a) + B(f(s,a))\), because the sum has one term and the terminal reward is paid at time \(H\) from state \(f(s,a)\). So the plans from \((H-1,s)\) are indexed by \(a \in A(s)\), and the maximum of their totals is \(\max_{a \in A(s)} \{r(s,a) + B(f(s,a))\}\). This is the formula of Theorem 2.1 at \(t = H-1\), because \(V_H = B\).
0084 P2 · The principle at time one
0085 Let \(H = 2\) and let \((a, b)\) be an optimal plan from \((0,s)\), where \(b\) is the action at time 1. Show directly that \(b\) is optimal from \((1, f(s,a))\).
0086 Solution
0087 Write \(s_1 = f(s,a)\). Suppose some \(b' \in A(s_1)\) gives a larger total from \((1, s_1)\) than \(b\) does. Then \((a, b')\) is a plan from \((0,s)\), because \(b' \in A(s_1)\). Its total is \(r(s,a) + \bigl[ r(s_1,b') + B(f(s_1,b')) \bigr]\), and the total of \((a,b)\) is \(r(s,a) + \bigl[ r(s_1,b) + B(f(s_1,b)) \bigr]\). The shared term \(r(s,a)\) cancels, so \((a,b')\) has the larger total. That contradicts the optimality of \((a,b)\). Hence \(b\) is optimal from \((1, s_1)\). This is Theorem 2.2 with \(k = 1\) and \(H = 2\).
0088 P3 · Corollary 2.4 for two periods
0089 For \(H = 2\), use Theorem 2.3 to show that choosing a maximizing first action and then a maximizing second action gives an optimal plan from \((0,s)\).
0090 Solution
0091 Let \(a\) attain the maximum in Theorem 2.1 at \((0,s)\), and let \(b\) attain the maximum at \((1, f(s,a))\). The one-period plan \((b)\) from \((1, f(s,a))\) is optimal, because the maximum at time 1 is \(V_1(s_1) = r(s_1,b) + B(f(s_1,b))\) with \(s_1 = f(s,a)\), which \((b)\) attains. Condition (i) of Theorem 2.3 holds because \(a\) is maximizing, and condition (ii) holds by the optimality of \((b)\). So \((a, b)\) is optimal from \((0,s)\).

0097 E2 · The greedy plan as a fraction of the optimum

0101 E3 · Count the feasible consumption sequences
0102 Part B of Lab 1 prints plans_checked_from_0_400 70. Explain the number 70 from the grid and the horizon, without rerunning the code.
0103 Solution
0104 Write each consumption as \(100 c'_j\) with \(c'_j\) a whole number, \(j = 1, \ldots, 4\). Feasibility from 400 cents means \(c'_1 + \cdots + c'_4 \le 4\), with each \(c'_j \ge 0\). Adding a slack variable \(c'_5 \ge 0\) turns this into five nonnegative whole numbers that sum to 4. There are \(\binom{8}{4} = 70\) such solutions. This matches the printed count, which is the number of feasible sequences the enumeration checked.

0106 R1 · Where the proof of Theorem 2.1 needs a maximum
0107 Remark 1.3 says that a maximum may fail to exist over an infinite action set. Explain how the existence of a value can fail in Definition 1.2 and what Theorem 2.1 then says.
0108 Solution
0109 The proof of Theorem 2.1 writes \(V_t(s)\) as a maximum over \(a\) and over plans \(q\). With \(A(s) = (0,1)\), \(r(s,a) = a\), \(H = 1\) and \(B = 0\), the set of totals from \((0,s)\) is \((0,1)\), whose supremum is 1 with no attaining plan. Defining \(V_0(s)\) as the supremum keeps the formula meaningful, but then no optimal plan exists, and Theorem 2.2 has no optimal plan to start from. The formula then holds with the supremum in place of the maximum by the definition of supremum, but no plan attains it, so Theorem 2.2 has no optimal plan to start from.
0110 R2 · An objective for which the principle fails
0111 Replace the total reward of Definition 1.2 by \(J(p) = \max_k r(s_k, a_k)\), the largest period reward along the plan, with \(B = 0\). Take \(H = 2\), \(S = \{s, x\}\), \(A(s) = \{a_0\}\), \(A(x) = \{b, c\}\), \(f(s,a_0) = x\), \(f(x,b) = f(x,c) = x\), \(r(s,a_0) = 10\), \(r(x,b) = 5\) and \(r(x,c) = 3\). Show that the plan \((a_0, c)\) is optimal from \((0,s)\) but its continuation \((c)\) is not optimal from \((1,x)\).
0112 Solution
0113 From \((1,x)\) the two one-period plans have objectives 5 and 3, so \(V_1(x) = 5\) under this objective, and \((c)\), with objective 3, is not optimal. From \((0,s)\) the only action is \(a_0\), so the plans are \((a_0, b)\) and \((a_0, c)\). Their objectives are \(\max\{10, 5\} = 10\) and \(\max\{10, 3\} = 10\). Both equal 10, which is \(V_0(s)\), so \((a_0, c)\) is optimal. The continuation of an optimal plan is not optimal here. The step that fails is the additive split of Theorem 2.2's proof, because \(\max\{10, \cdot\}\) does not add the tail's value to a fixed prefix.
0114 R3 · Counting plans against the recursion
0115 Suppose \(|A(s)| = m\) for every \(s \in S\). Show that there are \(m^{H-t}\) plans from \((t,s)\), and compare that count with the number of candidate sums in the recursion for the same problem.
0116 Solution
0117 A plan from \((t,s)\) is a sequence of \(H - t\) actions, where each action is chosen from the set of feasible actions at the state reached. Each state has \(m\) feasible actions, so there are \(m\) choices at each of the \(H - t\) times, and \(m^{H-t}\) plans in all. The recursion computes \(V_k(s')\) from the \(m\) candidate sums at each state \(s'\) and each time \(k\) from \(t\) to \(H-1\). That is at most \(H \cdot |S| \cdot m\) candidate sums for the whole table. The plan count grows exponentially in \(H - t\), and the candidate count grows linearly in \(H\).
