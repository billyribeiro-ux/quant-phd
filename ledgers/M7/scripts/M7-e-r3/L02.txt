0012 Dlore
0013 Xrefuted
0014 The minimum-variance problem asks for the weights, which must add up to one, that minimize the variance of a portfolio. Adding a constraint to a minimization changes the first-order condition: the gradient of the objective need not vanish, but at a minimizer where the constraint gradient is non-zero it must line up with the gradient of the constraint. This lesson states that condition, proves it under the regularity hypothesis that the constraint gradient is non-zero and one stated result from M2 (Assumption 1, defined in Section 2), and shows that it is not enough on its own. For the budget constraint it gives an exact closed form, which Lab 1 checks in exact arithmetic.
0015 1Constrained minimization
0016 Let \(U \subseteq \R^n\) be open, and let \(f : U \to \R\) and \(h : U \to \R\) be continuously differentiable. The set \(\{x \in U : h(x) = 0\}\) is the feasible set of the equality constraint \(h(x) = 0\), the set of points that satisfy it. A point \(x^*\) of the feasible set is a local minimizer of \(f\) on the feasible set when \(f(x^*) \le f(x)\) for every feasible \(x\) in some open ball around \(x^*\). Local and global minimizers are different objects, and Example 2.6 shows a point that satisfies the Lagrange condition of Definition 2.1 below and is not even a local minimizer.
0017 The gradient of \(f\) at \(x\) is the column vector \(\nabla f(x)\) of its partial derivatives, and \(\nabla h(x)\) is the gradient of the constraint function. The geometric picture behind the next definition is a motivation only: no result on this page uses it. Its content is that \(\nabla h(x)\), when non-zero, is the normal vector of the level set of \(h\) at \(x\).
0018 2The Lagrange condition
0019 Definition 2.1 (Lagrange multiplier)
0020 Let \(f\) and \(h\) be as above, and let \(x^*\) be a feasible point. A real number \(\lambda\) is a Lagrange multiplier of \(f\) at \(x^*\) for the constraint \(h(x) = 0\) when \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda \nabla h(x^*)\). A feasible point that satisfies this equation for some real \(\lambda\) is called stationary for the constrained problem.
0021 The sign convention is a choice: with \(\nabla f + \lambda \nabla h = 0\) the multiplier would change sign. This lesson uses the form \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla h\), so every multiplier in this lesson is read with that sign.
0022 Assumption 1 (implicit function theorem, from M2)
0023 Let \(h\) be continuously differentiable on an open set \(U \subseteq \R^n\), let \(x^*\) be a point of \(U\) with \(h(x^*) = 0\), and let \(\partial_k h(x^*) \ne 0\). Then there are a neighbourhood \(N\) of \(x^*_{-k}\) and a continuously differentiable \(\varphi : N \to \R\) with \(\varphi(x^*_{-k}) = x^*_k\), \(h(x_{-k}, \varphi(x_{-k})) = 0\) for \(x_{-k} \in N\), and \(\nabla \varphi(x^*_{-k}) = -\nabla_{-k} h(x^*) / \partial_k h(x^*)\). This is the implicit function theorem, which M2 states. It is assumed here and not proved on this page.
0024 Theorem 2.2 (necessity of the Lagrange condition)
0025 Assuming Assumption 1, let \(U \subseteq \R^n\) be open, let \(f, h : U \to \R\) be continuously differentiable, and let \(x^*\) be a point of \(U\) with \(h(x^*) = 0\) and \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). Suppose \(x^*\) is a local minimizer of \(f\) on the feasible set \(\{x \in U : h(x) = 0\}\). Then there is a real \(\lambda\) with \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda \nabla h(x^*)\).
0026 The hypotheses are: \(U\) open, both functions continuously differentiable, the point feasible, the regularity condition \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\), and minimality on the feasible set. The regularity condition is stated as a hypothesis, not assumed silently; Example 2.5 shows that the conclusion can fail without it.
0027 Proof
0028 Write \(p = \nabla f(x^*)\) and \(g = \nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). Suppose, for contradiction, that \(p\) is not a real multiple of \(g\). Put \(c = (p \cdot g)/(g \cdot g)\) and \(r = p - c g\). Then \(r \cdot g = p \cdot g - c\, g \cdot g = 0\), and \(r \ne 0\) because \(p\) is not a multiple of \(g\). Let \(d = -r\). Then \(g \cdot d = 0\), and
0029 \[p \cdot d = -p \cdot r = -\bigl(|r|^2 + c\, g \cdot r\bigr) = -|r|^2 < 0,\]
0030 where the middle step uses \(p = r + c g\), and the last step uses \(g \cdot r = 0\).
0031 Because \(g \ne 0\), some coordinate \(k\) has \(\partial_k h(x^*) \ne 0\). Write \(x = (x_{-k}, x_k)\). Assumption 1 gives a neighbourhood of \(x^*_{-k}\) and a continuously differentiable function \(\varphi\) on it with \(\varphi(x^*_{-k}) = x^*_k\), \(h(x_{-k}, \varphi(x_{-k})) = 0\) and \(\nabla\varphi(x^*_{-k}) = -\nabla_{-k} h(x^*) / \partial_k h(x^*)\). Define \(\gamma(t) = (x^*_{-k} + t d_{-k},\ \varphi(x^*_{-k} + t d_{-k}))\) for small \(|t|\). Then \(\gamma\) is continuously differentiable, \(h(\gamma(t)) = 0\) (so \(\gamma(t)\) is feasible), \(\gamma(0) = x^*\), and \(\gamma'(0) = d\). The last equality holds in the \(k\)-th coordinate because \(g \cdot d = 0\) gives \(d_k = -\sum_{j \ne k} g_j d_j / g_k = \nabla\varphi(x^*_{-k}) \cdot d_{-k}\).
0032 Since \(f\) is differentiable at \(x^*\), \(f(\gamma(t)) = f(x^*) + t\, p \cdot d + o(t)\) as \(t \to 0\). Because \(p \cdot d < 0\), there is a \(t_0 > 0\) with \(f(\gamma(t)) < f(x^*)\) for \(0 < t < t_0\). Those \(\gamma(t)\) are feasible points arbitrarily close to \(x^*\), which contradicts the minimality of \(x^*\). So \(p\) is a multiple of \(g\): \(p = \lambda g\) for some real \(\lambda\). \(\blacksquare\)
0033 Remark 2.3 (the proof uses only the local curve)
0034 For each prescribed tangent \(d\) in the tangent space \(\{v : \nabla h(x^*) \cdot v = 0\}\), the proof constructs a feasible curve through \(x^*\) with that tangent. Minimality rules out every such curve along which \(f\) decreases to first order, and the rest of the argument in the proof of Theorem 2.2 then forces \(\nabla f(x^*)\) to be a multiple of \(\nabla h(x^*)\).
0035 Example 2.5 (the regularity hypothesis is needed)
0036 Let \(U = \R^2\), \(h(x, y) = y^2 - x^3\) and \(f(x, y) = x\). At the origin \(\nabla h = (-3x^2, 2y) = (0, 0)\), so the regularity condition fails. The feasible set \(\{y^2 = x^3\}\) forces \(x = (y^2)^{1/3} \ge 0\), so \(f = x \ge 0 = f(0, 0)\) on it, and the origin is a minimizer. But \(\nabla f(0, 0) = (1, 0)\) is not a multiple of \(\nabla h(0, 0) = (0, 0)\), because \(\lambda (0, 0) = (0, 0)\) for every \(\lambda\). So the minimizer has no Lagrange multiplier, and Theorem 2.2 does not apply.
0037 Example 2.6 (a stationary point that is not a minimizer)
0038 Let \(f(x, y) = y^3\) and \(h(x, y) = x\) on \(\R^2\). Here \(\nabla h = (1, 0) \ne 0\), and \(\nabla f(0, 0) = (0, 3y^2)\) at \(y = 0\) is \((0, 0) = 0 \cdot \nabla h\), so the origin satisfies the Lagrange condition with \(\lambda = 0\). The feasible set is the \(y\)-axis. For every \(y > 0\) the point \((0, -y)\) is feasible and \(f(0, -y) = -y^3 < 0 = f(0, 0)\). These points lie arbitrarily close to the origin, so the origin is not a local minimizer, and therefore not a global one. The witness \((0, -1)\) alone already refutes global minimality.
0039 Proposition 2.4 (uniqueness of the multiplier)
0040 Let \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). If \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are both Lagrange multipliers of \(f\) at \(x^*\) for the constraint \(h = 0\), then \(\lambda = \mu\).
0041 Proof
0042 Subtracting the two conditions gives \((\lambda - \mu) \nabla h(x^*) = 0\). Since \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\), its coordinates are not all zero, so \(\lambda - \mu = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
0043 3A closed form under a budget constraint
0044 A symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix \(\Sigma\) is positive definite when \(x^{\top} \Sigma x > 0\) for every \(x \ne 0\). Positive definiteness is stronger than the non-negativity of Lesson 1, Proposition 3.2, because it excludes a zero value at a non-zero \(x\). Let \(\mathbf{1}\) be the vector of \(n\) ones, and consider
0045 \[\min_{w \in \R^n}\; f(w) = \tfrac12\, w^{\top} \Sigma w \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1.\]
0046 The constraint \(h(w) = \mathbf{1}^{\top} w - 1\) has gradient \(\nabla h = \mathbf{1} \ne 0\), so it meets the regularity hypothesis of Theorem 2.2. Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.2 applies to every local minimizer of \(f\) on the budget set. The next proposition does not rely on Theorem 2.2, because it proves the minimum directly.
0047 Proposition 3.2 (the minimum-variance weights)
0048 Let \(\Sigma\) be a positive definite \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(\Sigma\) is invertible. Put \(u = \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{1}\), \(s = \mathbf{1}^{\top} u\) and \(\lambda = 1/s\), and let \(w^* = \lambda u\). Then:
0049 (a) \(s > 0\), so \(\lambda\) is well defined; (b) \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w^* = 1\); (c) \(\nabla f(w^*) = \Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\), so \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier at \(w^*\); (d) \(w^*\) is the unique minimizer of \(f\) on \(\{w : \mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1\}\).
0050 The hypotheses are that \(\Sigma\) is symmetric and positive definite, and that the constraint is the budget \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1\). No condition on the sign of \(w\) is imposed.
0051 Proof
0052 Invertibility. If \(\Sigma x = 0\) for some \(x\), then \(x^{\top} \Sigma x = 0\), which forces \(x = 0\) by positive definiteness. So the only solution of \(\Sigma x = 0\) is zero, and \(\Sigma\) is invertible.
0053 (a). The vector \(u\) satisfies \(\Sigma u = \mathbf{1} \ne 0\), so \(u \ne 0\). Then \(s = \mathbf{1}^{\top} u = (\Sigma u)^{\top} u = u^{\top} \Sigma u > 0\), using symmetry of \(\Sigma\) in \((\Sigma u)^{\top} = u^{\top} \Sigma\).
0054 (b). \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}^{\top} u = \lambda s = 1\).
0055 (c). \(\nabla f(w) = \Sigma w\), because \(\nabla (\tfrac12 w^{\top} \Sigma w) = \tfrac12 (\Sigma + \Sigma^{\top}) w = \Sigma w\). Then \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \Sigma u = \lambda \mathbf{1}\).
0056 (d). Let \(w\) satisfy \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1\), and put \(d = w - w^*\), so \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\). Expanding,
0057 \[f(w) = f(w^*) + w^{*\top} \Sigma d + \tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d.\]
0058 By (c) and symmetry, \(w^{*\top} \Sigma d = (\Sigma w^*)^{\top} d = \lambda \mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\). So \(f(w) - f(w^*) = \tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d\). This is non-negative, and it is zero only when \(d = 0\), because \(\Sigma\) is positive definite. Hence \(w^*\) is the unique minimizer. \(\blacksquare\)
0059 Proposition 3.3 (the matrix of Lab 1 is positive definite)
0060 Let \(A\) be any real \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A^{\top} A + I\) is symmetric and positive definite.
0061 Proof
0062 The matrix \((A^{\top} A)^{\top} = A^{\top} A\), so \(A^{\top} A + I\) is symmetric. For any \(x\), \(x^{\top}(A^{\top} A + I) x = (Ax)^{\top}(Ax) + x^{\top} x = \|Ax\|^2 + \|x\|^2\). If \(x \ne 0\), the second term is positive, and the first is non-negative, so the sum is positive. \(\blacksquare\)
0063 Lab 1 · Minimum-variance weights in exact arithmetic
0064 Predicted from Proposition 3.2 and Proposition 3.3 before running. The matrix \(\Sigma = A^{\top} A + I\) is symmetric and positive definite, so the closed form applies. Proposition 3.2 predicts three things. The budget \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w\) equals 1 exactly. The gradient \(\Sigma w\) equals \(\lambda \mathbf{1}\) exactly, so every residual \(\Sigma w - \lambda \mathbf{1}\) is zero. For every direction \(d\) with \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\) and every step \(t\), the increase \(f(w + t d) - f(w)\) equals \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\), which is non-negative. The entries of \(A\), \(\Sigma\), \(\lambda\) and \(w\) are computed from a fixed seed and printed; the lab does not predict them.

0068 4Where the canon breaks
0069 The proposition above is proved on the page, and Theorem 2.2 is proved on the page from Assumption 1. The hypotheses carry the weight, and four points need care.
0070 Stationarity is necessary, not sufficient. Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.2 gives a necessary condition. Example 2.6 shows a stationary point that is not a minimizer, so a solution of \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla h\) must still be checked.
0071 The regularity hypothesis cannot be dropped. Example 2.5 gives a minimizer at which \(\nabla h = 0\) and no multiplier exists. Theorem 2.2 is silent there, and the reader should not read it as a claim about such points.
0072 A local minimizer is not a global one. Theorem 2.2 is a statement about local minimizers. Proposition 3.2 proves global minimality, and only for the budget problem with positive definite \(\Sigma\). This lesson gives no global argument for other problems.
0073 The budget constraint is an equality. The closed form \(w^*\) can have negative entries, as Lab 1 shows. The solution of the problem with \(w \ge 0\) is a different problem, which Lesson 3 treats with the KKT conditions.
0074 5Misconceptions
0075 “At a constrained minimum the gradient of the objective is zero.”
0076 Theorem 2.2, under Assumption 1, says the gradient is a multiple of the constraint gradient. For \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) on the line \(x + y = 1\), the minimizer is \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) and \(\nabla f = (1, 1) = 1 \cdot \nabla h\), which is not zero. Lab 1 shows the same for the budget problem: its residual line reports \(\Sigma w^* - \lambda \mathbf{1} = 0\) with \(\lambda = 177/55\), so \(\Sigma w^* = \tfrac{177}{55} \mathbf{1}\) is not zero because the multiplier is not zero.
0077 “A point that satisfies the Lagrange condition is a minimizer.”
0078 Example 2.6 shows that it need not be. The condition is necessary, and the sufficiency for the budget problem comes from the quadratic structure and positive definiteness in Proposition 3.2, not from the condition alone.
0079 “The Lagrange condition holds at every minimizer, whatever the constraint.”
0080 Theorem 2.2 requires \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). Example 2.5 is a minimizer where \(\nabla h\) vanishes and no multiplier exists, so the hypothesis is needed for the conclusion.

0083 Mechanics
0084 M1 · The point of the line nearest the origin
0085 Minimize \(x^2 + y^2\) subject to \(x + y = 1\). Find the minimizer and the multiplier.
0086 Solution
0087 Here \(f = x^2 + y^2\) and \(h = x + y - 1\), so \(\nabla f = (2x, 2y)\) and \(\nabla h = (1, 1) \ne 0\). The Lagrange condition gives \((2x, 2y) = \lambda (1, 1)\), so \(x = y = \lambda / 2\). The constraint gives \(\lambda = 1\), so \(x = y = \tfrac12\). The multiplier is \(\lambda = 1\), and the minimizer is \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). The point is the foot of the perpendicular from the origin to the line, and it is the closest point of the line to the origin, since \(x^2 + y^2\) is the squared distance.
0088 M2 · The closed form for a diagonal matrix
0089 Let \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)\). Apply Proposition 3.2: compute \(u\), \(s\), \(\lambda\), \(w^*\) and \(f(w^*)\).
0090 Solution
0091 Solving \(\Sigma u = \mathbf{1}\) gives \(u = (1, \tfrac12)\). Then \(s = 1 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac32\), so \(\lambda = \tfrac23\) and \(w^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\). The budget is \(\tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = 1\). The gradient is \(\Sigma w^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac23) = \tfrac23 \mathbf{1}\), as Proposition 3.2 (c) requires. The objective is \(f(w^*) = \tfrac12 (\tfrac49 \cdot 1 + \tfrac19 \cdot 2) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac13\).
0092 M3 · Checking the increase formula by hand
0093 With \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)\) and \(w^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\) from Problem M2, let \(d = (1, -1)\) and \(t = 1\). Compute \(f(w^* + t d) - f(w^*)\) directly and compare it with \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\).
0094 Solution
0095 The point \(w^* + d = (\tfrac53, -\tfrac23)\) gives \(f = \tfrac12 (\tfrac{25}{9} + 2 \cdot \tfrac49) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{33}{9} = \tfrac{11}{6}\). From Problem M2, \(f(w^*) = \tfrac13 = \tfrac26\). The increase is \(\tfrac{11}{6} - \tfrac{2}{6} = \tfrac{9}{6} = \tfrac32\). The formula gives \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1 \cdot d^{\top} \Sigma d = \tfrac12 (1 + 2) = \tfrac32\), so the two agree, as the proof of Proposition 3.2 (d) predicts.
0096 Proof
0097 P1 · The multiplier of a regular constraint is unique
0098 Prove Proposition 2.4 from the definition of a Lagrange multiplier, and state where the regularity hypothesis is used.
0099 Solution
0100 If \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) both satisfy \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda \nabla h(x^*)\) and \(\nabla f(x^*) = \mu \nabla h(x^*)\), then subtracting gives \((\lambda - \mu) \nabla h(x^*) = 0\). The regularity hypothesis \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\) says that this vector has a non-zero coordinate, so the scalar \(\lambda - \mu\) must be zero. The hypothesis is used only in the last step, and without it any \(\lambda\) would satisfy the condition when \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\).
0101 P2 · Positive definiteness is needed for uniqueness
0102 Let \(\Sigma = 0\) in \(\R^2\), with the budget \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\). Show that every feasible point is a minimizer, and identify the step of Proposition 3.2 that fails.
0103 Solution
0104 With \(\Sigma = 0\), \(f(w) = 0\) for every \(w\), so every point of the budget line attains the minimum value 0. The minimizer is not unique. In the proof of Proposition 3.2, step (invertibility) fails, because \(\Sigma\) has no inverse and \(u\) is undefined. Step (d) also fails at its last claim: the increase \(\tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d\) is zero for every \(d\), not zero only when \(d = 0\). Positive definiteness is the hypothesis that prevents both failures.
0105 P3 · The minimum value of the budget problem
0106 Under the hypotheses of Proposition 3.2, show that \(f(w^*) = \tfrac{1}{2s}\), and that \(f(w^*) = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\).
0107 Solution
0108 By Proposition 3.2 (c), \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\), so \(w^{*\top} \Sigma w^* = \lambda\, \mathbf{1}^{\top} w^* = \lambda\) by the budget (b). Therefore \(f(w^*) = \tfrac12 \lambda\). Since \(\lambda = 1/s\), this is \(\tfrac{1}{2s}\). The value is the reciprocal of \(s = \mathbf{1}^{\top} \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{1}\), halved.
0109 Empirical
0110 E1 · Checking the first row of Sigma times w (Lab 1)
0111 Lab 1 prints \(\Sigma\) with first row \(4\ 2\ 5\) and \(w = (47/55, 17/55, -9/55)\). Compute the first coordinate of \(\Sigma w\) from these printed values and compare it with \(\lambda\).
0112 Solution
0113 The first coordinate is \(4 \cdot \tfrac{47}{55} + 2 \cdot \tfrac{17}{55} + 5 \cdot \left(-\tfrac{9}{55}\right) = \tfrac{188 + 34 - 45}{55} = \tfrac{177}{55}\). This equals the printed multiplier \(\lambda = 177/55\), and the lab's residual line reports that every coordinate of \(\Sigma w - \lambda \mathbf{1}\) is exactly zero. The check uses only printed values, and the arithmetic is exact.
0114 E2 · What the two 36-of-36 lines do and do not show (Lab 1)
0115 Lab 1 prints that the increase equals \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\) in 36 of 36 tested cases and is non-negative in 36 of 36. State what these counts establish and what they do not.
0116 Solution
0117 The counts establish the identity and the non-negativity for the six directions and six step sizes in the lab, because the arithmetic is exact. They do not establish optimality over all directions, which is the work of Proposition 3.2 (d). The proof there covers every feasible direction, and the lab is a numerical instance of it. The identity itself holds for every positive definite \(\Sigma\) by the proof of Proposition 3.2 (d). The count does not extend the claim to other matrices; the proof does.
0118 E3 · Reading the objective value (Lab 1)
0119 Lab 1 prints \(f(w) = 177/110\). Recover this value from the printed multiplier, without recomputing \(\Sigma w\) entry by entry.
0120 Solution
0121 By Problem P3, \(f(w^*) = \lambda / 2 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{177}{55} = \tfrac{177}{110}\). This matches the printed value, so the objective line is consistent with the multiplier line.

0123 R1 · The sign constraint changes the problem
0124 The Lab 1 solution has \(w_3 = -9/55 < 0\). Explain why this solution does not answer the question of the minimum-variance problem with \(w \ge 0\), and say which lesson treats that question.
0125 Solution
0126 Proposition 3.2 minimizes over the budget set with no sign restriction, so its unique minimizer is the minimizer of the problem without the constraint \(w \ge 0\). The minimizer has a negative entry, so it violates \(w \ge 0\). A minimizer of the problem with \(w \ge 0\) must lie in the feasible set of that problem, and the lab's point is not in it. The problem with a sign constraint is an inequality-constrained problem, treated in Lesson 3.
0127 R2 · Why the budget constraint satisfies the regularity hypothesis
0128 Show that the budget constraint \(h(w) = \mathbf{1}^{\top} w - 1\) satisfies the regularity hypothesis of Theorem 2.2 at every point, and explain why Proposition 3.2 does not need that theorem.
0129 Solution
0130 The gradient of \(h\) is the constant vector \(\mathbf{1}\), which is non-zero at every point, so the hypothesis \(\nabla h \ne 0\) holds everywhere. Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.2 then gives the necessary condition \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\). Proposition 3.2 does not need the theorem, because part (d) proves minimality directly: \(f(w) - f(w^*) = \tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d \ge 0\) for every feasible \(w\). That is a sufficiency argument, which the necessity theorem cannot provide.
0131 R3 · The multiplier as a rate of change of the minimum value
0132 Let \(c\) be a real number and consider the budget constraint \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = c\) with \(c\) in place of 1. Show that the minimum value is \(c^2 / (2s)\), and that its derivative with respect to \(c\) is \(c/s\). Check that at \(c = 1\) the derivative is the multiplier \(\lambda = 1/s\).
0133 Solution
0134 The proof of Proposition 3.2 with the budget \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = c\) in place of 1 gives the unique minimizer \(c w^*\), since every step is linear in the budget. Its objective is \(\tfrac12 c^2 w^{*\top} \Sigma w^* = \tfrac12 c^2 \lambda \cdot 1 = c^2 / (2s)\), using Problem P3. The derivative of \(c^2/(2s)\) with respect to \(c\) is \(c/s\), which equals \(\lambda\) at \(c = 1\). At \(c = 1\) the derivative of the minimum value equals the multiplier. This is one case computed on the page. The general statement for perturbations of the constraint is not proved on this page.
