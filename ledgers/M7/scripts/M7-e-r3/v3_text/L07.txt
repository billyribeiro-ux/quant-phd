0020 A plan of several choices can be found working backward one period at a time. This lesson defines a finite-horizon decision problem, its plans and its value function, and proves the principle of optimality: a plan that is optimal from a state has optimal later parts of the plan from the states it reaches. The proof gives the backward recursion. Lesson L08 calls that recursion the Bellman equation (its Definition 1.2). Lab 1 computes the recursion for a small problem and checks it against an enumeration of its plans. The lab shows where the principle stops: a greedy plan, which takes the largest period reward at each step, can have optimal later parts and still not be optimal.
0021 1Finite-horizon problems, plans and value
0022 Time runs over \(t = 0, 1, \ldots, H\), where the horizon \(H\) is a whole number with \(H \ge 1\). Rewards are real numbers, written as elements of \(\R\). The sets and maps below are fixed before any decision is made. Lab 1 uses an integer problem, and every amount in it is in whole cents or in utils, a label for an integer reward.
0023 Definition 1.1 (finite-horizon decision problem)
0024 A finite-horizon decision problem has the following data. A finite set \(S\) of states. For each \(s \in S\), a nonempty finite set \(A(s)\) of feasible actions. A transition map \(f\) with \(f(s,a) \in S\) for every \(s \in S\) and \(a \in A(s)\), so the next state is a known function of the current state and action; the transitions are deterministic. A period reward \(r(s,a) \in \R\) for each \(s\) and \(a \in A(s)\). A terminal reward \(B(s) \in \R\) for each \(s \in S\), paid at time \(H\).
0025 Definition 1.2 (plans, total reward and value)
0026 Let \(0 \le t \le H\) and \(s \in S\). A plan from \((t,s)\) is a sequence \(p = (a_t, \ldots, a_{H-1})\) for which the states \(s_t = s\) and \(s_{k+1} = f(s_k, a_k)\) satisfy \(a_k \in A(s_k)\) for each \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\). Its total reward is
0027 \[
0028 J_t(p) = \sum_{k=t}^{H-1} r(s_k, a_k) + B(s_H).
0029 \]
0030 The value function is \(V_t(s) = \max_p J_t(p)\), the maximum over the plans from \((t,s)\). A plan from \((t,s)\) is optimal if \(J_t(p) = V_t(s)\). Because each \(A(s)\) is finite and nonempty and \(H\) is finite, the set of plans from \((t,s)\) is finite and nonempty, so the maximum exists.
0031 Remark 1.3 (where finiteness is used)
0032 Finiteness is used for the existence of the maxima in Definition 1.2 and Theorem 2.1. Over an infinite action set the largest value may not be attained. Take \(H = 1\), one state \(s\), \(A(s) = (0,1)\), \(r(s,a) = a\), \(f(s,a) = s\) and \(B(s) = 0\). Then \(V_0(s)\) is the supremum 1, and no plan attains it, because each \(a \in (0,1)\) gives the total \(a < 1\).
0033 Definition 1.4 (continuation and the principle of optimality)
0034 Let \(p = (a_t, \ldots, a_{H-1})\) be a plan from \((t,s)\), with states \(s_k\) along it. For \(t \le k \le H-1\), the continuation of \(p\) from time \(k\) is \((a_k, \ldots, a_{H-1})\), a plan from \((k, s_k)\). The principle of optimality is the statement that, if \(p\) is optimal from \((t,s)\), then for each \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\) the continuation of \(p\) from time \(k\) is optimal from \((k, s_k)\). Theorem 2.2 proves it for the problem of Definition 1.1. Remark 2.5 shows that the converse does not hold.
0035 2The backward recursion and the principle of optimality
0036 The next theorem is the backward recursion. Its proof runs downward in time, which is the form of mathematical induction from M1 that this lesson uses. Lesson L08 calls the equation in Theorem 2.1 the Bellman equation (Definition 1.2 of L08).
0037 Theorem 2.1 (backward recursion)
0038 Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, \(V_H(s) = B(s)\) for every \(s \in S\). For \(0 \le t \le H-1\) and every \(s \in S\),
0039 \[
0040 V_t(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \bigl\{ r(s,a) + V_{t+1}\bigl(f(s,a)\bigr) \bigr\}.
0041 \]
0042 Proof
0043 The proof is by downward induction on \(t\). At \(t = H\) the only plan from \((H,s)\) is the empty plan, whose total reward is \(J_H = B(s)\), so \(V_H(s) = B(s)\).
0044 Suppose the formula holds at time \(t+1\), and let \(s \in S\). A plan from \((t,s)\) is a pair \((a, q)\), where \(a \in A(s)\) is the first action and \(q\) is a plan from \((t+1, f(s,a))\). Conversely, every such pair is a plan from \((t,s)\), because the state at time \(t+1\) is \(f(s,a)\). For such a pair the total reward splits as
0045 \[
0046 J_t(a,q) = r(s,a) + J_{t+1}(q),
0047 \]
0048 since the first period contributes \(r(s,a)\) and the remaining periods and the terminal reward contribute \(J_{t+1}(q)\). Taking the maximum over pairs means taking the maximum over \(a \in A(s)\) of the maximum over \(q\):
0049 \[
0050 V_t(s) = \max_{a \in A(s)} \Bigl\{ r(s,a) + \max_{q} J_{t+1}(q) \Bigr\} = \max_{a \in A(s)} \bigl\{ r(s,a) + V_{t+1}\bigl(f(s,a)\bigr) \bigr\}.
0051 \]
0052 Both maxima range over finite nonempty sets, so they exist.
0053 Theorem 2.2 (principle of optimality)
0054 Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, let \(0 \le t \le H-1\), let \(s \in S\), and let \(p^{*} = (a^{*}_t, \ldots, a^{*}_{H-1})\) be an optimal plan from \((t,s)\), with states \(s^{*}_k\) along it. Then for every \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\), the continuation of \(p^{*}\) from time \(k\) is optimal from \((k, s^{*}_k)\).
0055 Proof
0056 Fix \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\). Let \(c = (a^{*}_k, \ldots, a^{*}_{H-1})\) be the continuation of \(p^{*}\) from time \(k\), and let \(q\) be any plan from \((k, s^{*}_k)\). Form the plan \(p'\) that follows \(p^{*}\) up to time \(k\) and then follows \(q\). Its states up to time \(k\) are \(s^{*}_t, \ldots, s^{*}_k\), so the actions in the first part are feasible, and \(q\) starts from \(s^{*}_k\). So \(p'\) is a plan from \((t,s)\).
0057 Write \(\Sigma = \sum_{j=t}^{k-1} r(s^{*}_j, a^{*}_j)\). The total reward splits at time \(k\) for both plans:
0058 \[
0059 J_t(p') = \Sigma + J_k(q), \qquad J_t(p^{*}) = \Sigma + J_k(c).
0060 \]
0061 Since \(p^{*}\) is optimal, \(J_t(p') \le J_t(p^{*})\), so \(J_k(q) \le J_k(c)\). This holds for every plan \(q\) from \((k, s^{*}_k)\). Since \(c\) is itself such a plan, \(J_k(c)\) is the maximum over these plans, so \(J_k(c) = V_k(s^{*}_k)\).
0062 Theorem 2.3 (first-step characterization)
0063 Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, let \(0 \le t \le H-1\), \(s \in S\), \(a \in A(s)\), and let \(q\) be a plan from \((t+1, f(s,a))\). The plan \((a, q)\) from \((t,s)\) is optimal if and only if both of the following hold: (i) \(r(s,a) + V_{t+1}(f(s,a)) = V_t(s)\), so \(a\) attains the maximum in Theorem 2.1; and (ii) \(q\) is optimal from \((t+1, f(s,a))\).
0064 Proof
0065 By the split in the proof of Theorem 2.1, \(J_t(a,q) = r(s,a) + J_{t+1}(q)\). Since \(J_{t+1}(q) \le V_{t+1}(f(s,a))\) by the definition of \(V_{t+1}\), and \(r(s,a) + V_{t+1}(f(s,a))\) is at most the maximum in Theorem 2.1, the chain
0066 \[
0067 J_t(a,q) \le r(s,a) + V_{t+1}\bigl(f(s,a)\bigr) \le V_t(s)
0068 \]
0069 holds. The plan is optimal, meaning \(J_t(a,q) = V_t(s)\), exactly when both inequalities are equalities. The first is an equality exactly when (ii) holds, and the second exactly when (i) holds.
0070 Corollary 2.4 (an optimal plan from the recursion)
0071 Under the hypotheses of Definition 1.1, let \(0 \le t \le H\) and \(s \in S\). Form the plan from \((t,s)\) forward: at each time \(k\) with \(t \le k \le H-1\) and state \(s_k\), choose an action that attains the maximum in Theorem 2.1 at \((k, s_k)\). The resulting plan is optimal from \((t,s)\).
0072 Proof
0073 Induction on the number of remaining periods \(H - t\). If \(H - t = 0\), the empty plan is the only plan and is optimal. Otherwise the plan has the form \((a, q)\), where \(a\) attains the maximum at \((t,s)\), and \(q\) is the plan built by the same rule from \((t+1, f(s,a))\). By the induction hypothesis \(q\) is optimal from \((t+1, f(s,a))\), and condition (i) of Theorem 2.3 holds because \(a\) attains the maximum. By Theorem 2.3 the plan \((a, q)\) is optimal.
0074 Lab 1 · Backward recursion checked against brute force
0075 SIMULATED: a fixed toy problem with integer inputs, not market data. Four periods, five wealth states in cents (0, 100, 200, 300, 400), consumption in cents, and integer rewards in utils. Stated before running: Part A should give \(V_0(400) = 240\) utils, attained by consumption of 100 cents in each of the four periods. Part B should print recursion_matches_brute_force True, which compares the recursion with brute force at each of the 20 pairs of time and wealth (t = 0 to 3, five states each), and should print 70 for the feasible sequences from (0, 400). Part C should show the greedy plan 400, 0, 0, 0 earning 130 utils, not optimal, while each of its continuations from t = 1, 2 and 3 is optimal. The code draws no random numbers, so the output is the same on every run.
0076 """M7 L07 lab 1: backward recursion for a finite-horizon toy problem, checked against brute-force enumeration.
0077 SIMULATED: a fixed toy problem with integer inputs. It is not market data and it uses no random draws. Wealth and
0078 consumption are whole cents. Period rewards and the bequest are integers in utils, so every value is
0079 exact and no floating-point error enters.
0080 The problem has H = 4 periods, t = 0 to 3. The state at time t is wealth W in {0, 100, 200, 300, 400} cents. The
0081 action is consumption c, a multiple of 100 cents with c <= W. Next wealth is W - c. The period reward is u(c) from the
0082 table below. The reward at time H = 4 is the bequest W // 100 utils.
0083 Part A prints the value table V_t(W) from the backward recursion, for t = 0 to 4.
0084 Part B enumerates every consumption sequence of length H - t from each state, takes the best total, and compares it
0085 with the recursion. It prints the number of feasible sequences checked from (0, 400).
0086 Part C prints the optimal plan from (0, 400), checks that each continuation of it (the printed names say tail)
0087 is optimal from the state it reaches, and compares the greedy plan (the largest period reward at each step)
0088 with the optimum.
0089 """
0090 from __future__ import annotations
0091 from itertools import product
0092 HORIZON = 4
0093 GRID = (0, 100, 200, 300, 400)
0094 REWARD = {0: 0, 100: 60, 200: 100, 300: 120, 400: 130}
0095 def period_reward(c: int) -> int:
0096 """Utility in utils of consuming c cents in one period."""
0097 return REWARD[c]
0098 def bequest(w: int) -> int:
0099 """Reward in utils for the wealth w cents left at time H, counted in whole hundreds."""
0100 return w // 100
0101 def feasible(w: int) -> tuple[int, ...]:
0102 """Consumption choices from wealth w cents: every grid amount c with c <= w."""
0103 return tuple(c for c in GRID if c <= w)
0104 def backward_recursion() -> list[dict[int, int]]:
0105 """values[t][w] is V_t(w) in utils. V_H is the bequest; each earlier value is the maximum of the recursion."""
0106 values: list[dict[int, int]] = [{} for _ in range(HORIZON + 1)]
0107 values[HORIZON] = {w: bequest(w) for w in GRID}
0108 for t in range(HORIZON - 1, -1, -1):
0109 for w in GRID:
0110 values[t][w] = max(period_reward(c) + values[t + 1][w - c] for c in feasible(w))
0111 return values
0112 def brute_force(t: int, w: int) -> tuple[int, int]:
0113 """Best total reward from (t, w) over every consumption sequence, and how many feasible sequences were checked."""
0114 best: int | None = None
0115 checked = 0
0116 for plan in product(GRID, repeat=HORIZON - t):
0117 wealth = w
0118 total = 0
0119 feasible_plan = True
0120 for c in plan:
0121 if c > wealth:
0122 feasible_plan = False
0123 break
0124 total += period_reward(c)
0125 wealth -= c
0126 if not feasible_plan:
0127 continue
0128 total += bequest(wealth)
0129 checked += 1
0130 if best is None or total > best:
0131 best = total
0132 if best is None:
0133 raise RuntimeError("no feasible plan")
0134 return best, checked
0135 def optimal_plan(values: list[dict[int, int]], w0: int) -> tuple[list[int], list[int]]:
0136 """Follow the recursion's maximising consumption from (0, w0). Returns the plan and the wealth path."""
0137 plan: list[int] = []
0138 path = [w0]
0139 w = w0
0140 for t in range(HORIZON):
0141 c = max(feasible(w), key=lambda x: period_reward(x) + values[t + 1][w - x])
0142 plan.append(c)
0143 w -= c
0144 path.append(w)
0145 return plan, path
0146 def greedy_plan(w0: int) -> tuple[list[int], list[int]]:
0147 """Take the largest period reward available at each step, ignoring the future. Returns the plan and wealth path."""
0148 plan: list[int] = []
0149 path = [w0]
0150 w = w0
0151 for _ in range(HORIZON):
0152 c = max(feasible(w), key=period_reward)
0153 plan.append(c)
0154 w -= c
0155 path.append(w)
0156 return plan, path
0157 def plan_total(consumptions: list[int], w0: int) -> int:
0158 """Total reward in utils of a consumption sequence started at wealth w0, including the bequest."""
0159 total = 0
0160 w = w0
0161 for c in consumptions:
0162 total += period_reward(c)
0163 w -= c
0164 return total + bequest(w)
0165 def main() -> None:
0166 """Print Parts A, B and C. Every value is an integer, so every line of the output is exact."""
0167 values = backward_recursion()
0168 print("Part A: value table V_t(W) in utils, from the backward recursion")
0169 for t in range(HORIZON + 1):
0170 cells = " ".join(f"W{w}={values[t][w]}" for w in GRID)
0171 print(f"t {t} {cells}")
0172 print("Part B: brute force over every consumption sequence")
0173 all_match = True
0174 for t in range(HORIZON):
0175 for w in GRID:
0176 best, _ = brute_force(t, w)
0177 all_match = all_match and best == values[t][w]
0178 _, checked = brute_force(0, 400)
0179 print(f"plans_checked_from_0_400 {checked}")
0180 print(f"recursion_matches_brute_force {all_match}")
0181 print("Part C: optimal plan from (0, 400), its continuations, and the greedy plan")
0182 plan, path = optimal_plan(values, 400)
0183 print("optimal_consumption " + " ".join(str(c) for c in plan))
0184 print("optimal_wealth_path " + " ".join(str(w) for w in path))
0185 print(f"optimal_total {plan_total(plan, 400)}")
0186 tails_optimal = all(plan_total(plan[k:], path[k]) == values[k][path[k]] for k in range(HORIZON))
0187 print(f"optimal_plan_every_tail_optimal {tails_optimal}")
0188 greedy, greedy_path = greedy_plan(400)
0189 print("greedy_consumption " + " ".join(str(c) for c in greedy))
0190 print(f"greedy_total {plan_total(greedy, 400)}")
0191 for k in range(1, HORIZON):
0192 tail_optimal = plan_total(greedy[k:], greedy_path[k]) == values[k][greedy_path[k]]
0193 print(f"greedy_tail_from_t {k} wealth {greedy_path[k]} optimal {tail_optimal}")
0194 print(f"greedy_plan_optimal {plan_total(greedy, 400) == values[0][400]}")
0195 main()

0217 Remark 2.5 (the converse of the principle fails)
0218 Theorem 2.2 says that the continuations of an optimal plan are optimal. The converse does not hold: a plan whose continuations from each later time \(k > t\) are all optimal need not be optimal. Take \(H = 2\), \(S = \{x, y\}\), \(A(x) = \{a, b\}\), \(A(y) = \{c\}\), \(f(x,a) = f(x,b) = y\), \(f(y,c) = y\), \(r(x,a) = 0\), \(r(x,b) = 1\), \(r(y,c) = 0\), and \(B(x) = B(y) = 0\). Then \(V_1(y) = 0\), and \(V_0(x) = \max\{0 + 0,\, 1 + 0\} = 1\), attained only by \(b\). The plan \(p = (a, c)\) from \((0,x)\) has \(J_0(p) = 0 < 1\), so it is not optimal. Its continuation from time 1 is \((c)\) from \((1,y)\), the only plan from that state, so it is optimal. The principle gives a necessary condition for optimality, not a sufficient one.
0219 3Where the canon breaks
0220 Additive rewards. The proof of Theorem 2.2 uses that the total reward splits into a prefix and a continuation, \(J_t(p') = \Sigma + J_k(q)\). Under a different objective the split can fail, and the principle with it. Problem R2 gives an objective for which Theorem 2.2 is false.
0221 Finite sets and deterministic transitions. Remark 1.3 shows that a maximum over an infinite action set may fail to exist. This lesson also assumes that the next state is a known function of the current state and action. Problems with random transitions need a weighted average over the next states, which this lesson does not treat.
0222 A fixed horizon. Each result in §2 holds for one finite \(H\). This lesson does not treat an infinite horizon.
0223 Enumeration does not scale. Problem R3 shows that with \(m\) actions in every state, there are \(m^{H-t}\) plans from \((t,s)\). The recursion computes each \(V_t(s)\) from the \(|A(s)|\) candidate sums at \((t,s)\), so the whole table takes at most \(H\) times \(|S|\) times \(m\) candidate sums. Lab 1 uses the enumeration only as a check.
0224 4Misconceptions
0225 “The best first step is the one with the largest period reward.”
0226 At wealth 400 cents, the largest period reward is at consumption 400, worth 130 utils. The greedy plan takes it and earns a total of 130 utils. The optimal plan takes 100 cents in each period and earns 240 utils (greedy_total 130 and optimal_total 240 in Part C of Lab 1). The first step must be judged by its effect on the value of the state it leads to, which the recursion of Theorem 2.1 includes.
0227 “If every continuation of a plan from a later time is optimal, the plan is optimal.”
0228 Remark 2.5 gives a two-period problem in which the plan \((a, c)\) has an optimal continuation and is not optimal. Part C of Lab 1 shows the same effect: the greedy plan 400, 0, 0, 0 has its three continuations from t = 1, 2 and 3 printed as optimal, and greedy_plan_optimal False. The first action belongs to no continuation, so the continuations cannot judge it. Theorem 2.3 adds the missing condition, which is the optimal first action.
0229 “A state has the same value whatever the time left.”
0230 The recursion in Theorem 2.1 uses \(V_{t+1}\), so the value of a state depends on how many periods remain. Part A of Lab 1 shows the wealth-400 values 240, 220, 200, 130 and 4 utils at t = 0 to 4. A state's value at time \(t\) reflects the periods that are still to come.
0231 5Problem set
0232 Every problem has a full worked solution. Attempt each one before opening it. Problems M1 to M3 use the two-period problem below. Its states are \(x\) and \(y\), with \(A(x) = \{u, v\}\), \(A(y) = \{w\}\), \(f(x,u) = y\), \(f(x,v) = x\), \(f(y,w) = y\), \(r(x,u) = 3\), \(r(x,v) = 5\), \(r(y,w) = 2\), \(B(x) = 0\) and \(B(y) = 1\), with \(H = 2\).
0233 Mechanics
0234 M1 · The value function of the two-period problem
0235 Compute \(V_1(x)\), \(V_1(y)\) and \(V_0(x)\) for the problem above.
0236 Solution
0237 At time 1 there is one period left. From \(x\), action \(u\) gives \(3 + B(y) = 4\) and action \(v\) gives \(5 + B(x) = 5\), so \(V_1(x) = 5\). From \(y\), the only action \(w\) gives \(2 + B(y) = 3\), so \(V_1(y) = 3\). At time 0, from \(x\), action \(u\) gives \(3 + V_1(y) = 6\) and action \(v\) gives \(5 + V_1(x) = 10\), so \(V_0(x) = 10\), attained by \(v\).
0238 M2 · The optimal plan from \((0,x)\)
0239 For the problem above, state the optimal plan from \((0,x)\) and its total reward.
0240 Solution
0241 Corollary 2.4 says to take a maximizing action at each step. At time 0 from \(x\), the maximizing action is \(v\), as M1 shows. The state stays \(x\), and at time 1 from \(x\) the maximizing action is again \(v\), since \(V_1(x) = 5\) comes from \(v\). The optimal plan is \((v, v)\), with total reward \(5 + 5 = 10\), which equals \(V_0(x)\).
0242 M3 · Every plan from \((0,x)\)
0243 List every plan from \((0,x)\) for the problem above, with its total reward, and confirm that the maximum is 10.
0244 Solution
0245 A plan from \((0,x)\) is a pair of actions. The plan \((u, w)\) moves to \(y\) and then stays there, with total \(3 + (2 + B(y)) = 3 + 3 = 6\). The plan \((v, u)\) stays at \(x\) and then moves to \(y\), with total \(5 + (3 + B(y)) = 5 + 4 = 9\). The plan \((v, v)\) stays at \(x\) twice, with total \(5 + 5 = 10\). The plan \((u, v)\) is not feasible, because after \(u\) the state is \(y\) and \(v\) is not in \(A(y)\). So there are three plans, with totals 6, 9 and 10, and the maximum is 10.
0246 Proof
0247 P1 · The last step of the recursion
0248 Prove the case \(t = H - 1\) of Theorem 2.1 directly from Definition 1.2, without downward induction.
0249 Solution
0250 A plan from \((H-1,s)\) is a single action \(a \in A(s)\), with states \(s\) and then \(f(s,a)\). Its total reward is \(r(s,a) + B(f(s,a))\), because the sum has one term and the terminal reward is paid at time \(H\) from state \(f(s,a)\). So the plans from \((H-1,s)\) are indexed by \(a \in A(s)\), and the maximum of their totals is \(\max_{a \in A(s)} \{r(s,a) + B(f(s,a))\}\). This is the formula of Theorem 2.1 at \(t = H-1\), because \(V_H = B\).
0251 P2 · The principle at time one
0252 Let \(H = 2\) and let \((a, b)\) be an optimal plan from \((0,s)\), where \(b\) is the action at time 1. Show directly that \(b\) is optimal from \((1, f(s,a))\).
0253 Solution
0254 Write \(s_1 = f(s,a)\). Suppose some \(b' \in A(s_1)\) gives a larger total from \((1, s_1)\) than \(b\) does. Then \((a, b')\) is a plan from \((0,s)\), because \(b' \in A(s_1)\). Its total is \(r(s,a) + \bigl[ r(s_1,b') + B(f(s_1,b')) \bigr]\), and the total of \((a,b)\) is \(r(s,a) + \bigl[ r(s_1,b) + B(f(s_1,b)) \bigr]\). The shared term \(r(s,a)\) cancels, so \((a,b')\) has the larger total. That contradicts the optimality of \((a,b)\). Hence \(b\) is optimal from \((1, s_1)\). This is Theorem 2.2 with \(k = 1\) and \(H = 2\).
0255 P3 · Corollary 2.4 for two periods
0256 For \(H = 2\), use Theorem 2.3 to show that choosing a maximizing first action and then a maximizing second action gives an optimal plan from \((0,s)\).
0257 Solution
0258 Let \(a\) attain the maximum in Theorem 2.1 at \((0,s)\), and let \(b\) attain the maximum at \((1, f(s,a))\). The one-period plan \((b)\) from \((1, f(s,a))\) is optimal, because the maximum at time 1 is \(V_1(s_1) = r(s_1,b) + B(f(s_1,b))\) with \(s_1 = f(s,a)\), which \((b)\) attains. Condition (i) of Theorem 2.3 holds because \(a\) is maximizing, and condition (ii) holds by the optimality of \((b)\). So \((a, b)\) is optimal from \((0,s)\).

0272 Research
0273 R1 · Where the proof of Theorem 2.1 needs a maximum
0274 Remark 1.3 says that a maximum may fail to exist over an infinite action set. Explain how the existence of a value can fail in Definition 1.2 and what Theorem 2.1 then says.
0275 Solution
0276 The proof of Theorem 2.1 writes \(V_t(s)\) as a maximum over \(a\) and over plans \(q\). With \(A(s) = (0,1)\), \(r(s,a) = a\), \(H = 1\) and \(B = 0\), the set of totals from \((0,s)\) is \((0,1)\), whose supremum is 1 with no attaining plan. Defining \(V_0(s)\) as the supremum keeps the formula meaningful, but then no optimal plan exists, and Theorem 2.2 has no optimal plan to start from. The formula then holds with the supremum in place of the maximum by the definition of supremum, but no plan attains it, so Theorem 2.2 has no optimal plan to start from.
0277 R2 · An objective for which the principle fails
0278 Replace the total reward of Definition 1.2 by \(J(p) = \max_k r(s_k, a_k)\), the largest period reward along the plan, with \(B = 0\). Take \(H = 2\), \(S = \{s, x\}\), \(A(s) = \{a_0\}\), \(A(x) = \{b, c\}\), \(f(s,a_0) = x\), \(f(x,b) = f(x,c) = x\), \(r(s,a_0) = 10\), \(r(x,b) = 5\) and \(r(x,c) = 3\). Show that the plan \((a_0, c)\) is optimal from \((0,s)\) but its continuation \((c)\) is not optimal from \((1,x)\).
0279 Solution
0280 From \((1,x)\) the two one-period plans have objectives 5 and 3, so \(V_1(x) = 5\) under this objective, and \((c)\), with objective 3, is not optimal. From \((0,s)\) the only action is \(a_0\), so the plans are \((a_0, b)\) and \((a_0, c)\). Their objectives are \(\max\{10, 5\} = 10\) and \(\max\{10, 3\} = 10\). Both equal 10, which is \(V_0(s)\), so \((a_0, c)\) is optimal. The continuation of an optimal plan is not optimal here. The step that fails is the additive split of Theorem 2.2's proof, because \(\max\{10, \cdot\}\) does not add the tail's value to a fixed prefix.
0281 R3 · Counting plans against the recursion
0282 Suppose \(|A(s)| = m\) for every \(s \in S\). Show that there are \(m^{H-t}\) plans from \((t,s)\), and compare that count with the number of candidate sums in the recursion for the same problem.
0283 Solution
0284 A plan from \((t,s)\) is a sequence of \(H - t\) actions, where each action is chosen from the set of feasible actions at the state reached. Each state has \(m\) feasible actions, so there are \(m\) choices at each of the \(H - t\) times, and \(m^{H-t}\) plans in all. The recursion computes \(V_k(s')\) from the \(m\) candidate sums at each state \(s'\) and each time \(k\) from \(t\) to \(H-1\). That is at most \(H \cdot |S| \cdot m\) candidate sums for the whole table. The plan count grows exponentially in \(H - t\), and the candidate count grows linearly in \(H\).
