0011 Lagrange Multipliers for Equality Constraints
0012 Uses: the gradient and the implicit function theorem (M2); positive semidefinite matrices (M3); Lesson 1, Proposition 3.2 for the sign of a quadratic form
0013 Introduces: Lagrange multiplier
0014 Ttheorem
0015 Areplicated
0016 Bpublished, not settled
0017 Csingle study or lab result
0018 Dlore
0019 Xrefuted
0020 The minimum-variance problem asks for the weights, which must add up to one, that minimize the variance of a portfolio. Adding a constraint to a minimization changes the first-order condition: the gradient of the objective need not vanish, but at a minimizer where the constraint gradient is non-zero it must line up with the gradient of the constraint. This lesson states that condition, proves it under the regularity hypothesis that the constraint gradient is non-zero and one stated result from M2 (Assumption 1, defined in Section 2), and shows that it is not enough on its own. For the budget constraint it gives an exact closed form, which Lab 1 checks in exact arithmetic.
0021 1Constrained minimization
0022 Let \(U \subseteq \R^n\) be open, and let \(f : U \to \R\) and \(h : U \to \R\) be continuously differentiable. The set \(\{x \in U : h(x) = 0\}\) is the feasible set of the equality constraint \(h(x) = 0\), the set of points that satisfy it. A point \(x^*\) of the feasible set is a local minimizer of \(f\) on the feasible set when \(f(x^*) \le f(x)\) for every feasible \(x\) in some open ball around \(x^*\). Local and global minimizers are different objects, and Example 2.6 shows a point that satisfies the Lagrange condition of Definition 2.1 below and is not even a local minimizer.
0023 The gradient of \(f\) at \(x\) is the column vector \(\nabla f(x)\) of its partial derivatives, and \(\nabla h(x)\) is the gradient of the constraint function. The geometric picture behind the next definition is a motivation only: no result on this page uses it. Its content is that \(\nabla h(x)\), when non-zero, is the normal vector of the level set of \(h\) at \(x\).
0024 2The Lagrange condition
0025 Definition 2.1 (Lagrange multiplier)
0026 Let \(f\) and \(h\) be as above, and let \(x^*\) be a feasible point. A real number \(\lambda\) is a Lagrange multiplier of \(f\) at \(x^*\) for the constraint \(h(x) = 0\) when \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda \nabla h(x^*)\). A feasible point that satisfies this equation for some real \(\lambda\) is called stationary for the constrained problem.
0027 The sign convention is a choice: with \(\nabla f + \lambda \nabla h = 0\) the multiplier would change sign. This lesson uses the form \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla h\), so every multiplier in this lesson is read with that sign.
0028 Assumption 1 (implicit function theorem, from M2)
0029 Let \(h\) be continuously differentiable on an open set \(U \subseteq \R^n\), let \(x^*\) be a point of \(U\) with \(h(x^*) = 0\), and let \(\partial_k h(x^*) \ne 0\). Then there are a neighbourhood \(N\) of \(x^*_{-k}\) and a continuously differentiable \(\varphi : N \to \R\) with \(\varphi(x^*_{-k}) = x^*_k\), \(h(x_{-k}, \varphi(x_{-k})) = 0\) for \(x_{-k} \in N\), and \(\nabla \varphi(x^*_{-k}) = -\nabla_{-k} h(x^*) / \partial_k h(x^*)\). This is the implicit function theorem, which M2 states. It is assumed here and not proved on this page.
0030 Theorem 2.2 (necessity of the Lagrange condition)
0031 Assuming Assumption 1, let \(U \subseteq \R^n\) be open, let \(f, h : U \to \R\) be continuously differentiable, and let \(x^*\) be a point of \(U\) with \(h(x^*) = 0\) and \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). Suppose \(x^*\) is a local minimizer of \(f\) on the feasible set \(\{x \in U : h(x) = 0\}\). Then there is a real \(\lambda\) with \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda \nabla h(x^*)\).
0032 The hypotheses are: \(U\) open, both functions continuously differentiable, the point feasible, the regularity condition \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\), and minimality on the feasible set. The regularity condition is stated as a hypothesis, not assumed silently; Example 2.5 shows that the conclusion can fail without it.
0033 Proof
0034 Write \(p = \nabla f(x^*)\) and \(g = \nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). Suppose, for contradiction, that \(p\) is not a real multiple of \(g\). Put \(c = (p \cdot g)/(g \cdot g)\) and \(r = p - c g\). Then \(r \cdot g = p \cdot g - c\, g \cdot g = 0\), and \(r \ne 0\) because \(p\) is not a multiple of \(g\). Let \(d = -r\). Then \(g \cdot d = 0\), and
0035 \[p \cdot d = -p \cdot r = -\bigl(|r|^2 + c\, g \cdot r\bigr) = -|r|^2 < 0,\]
0036 where the middle step uses \(p = r + c g\), and the last step uses \(g \cdot r = 0\).
0037 Because \(g \ne 0\), some coordinate \(k\) has \(\partial_k h(x^*) \ne 0\). Write \(x = (x_{-k}, x_k)\). Assumption 1 gives a neighbourhood of \(x^*_{-k}\) and a continuously differentiable function \(\varphi\) on it with \(\varphi(x^*_{-k}) = x^*_k\), \(h(x_{-k}, \varphi(x_{-k})) = 0\) and \(\nabla\varphi(x^*_{-k}) = -\nabla_{-k} h(x^*) / \partial_k h(x^*)\). Define \(\gamma(t) = (x^*_{-k} + t d_{-k},\ \varphi(x^*_{-k} + t d_{-k}))\) for small \(|t|\). Then \(\gamma\) is continuously differentiable, \(h(\gamma(t)) = 0\) (so \(\gamma(t)\) is feasible), \(\gamma(0) = x^*\), and \(\gamma'(0) = d\). The last equality holds in the \(k\)-th coordinate because \(g \cdot d = 0\) gives \(d_k = -\sum_{j \ne k} g_j d_j / g_k = \nabla\varphi(x^*_{-k}) \cdot d_{-k}\).
0038 Since \(f\) is differentiable at \(x^*\), \(f(\gamma(t)) = f(x^*) + t\, p \cdot d + o(t)\) as \(t \to 0\). Because \(p \cdot d < 0\), there is a \(t_0 > 0\) with \(f(\gamma(t)) < f(x^*)\) for \(0 < t < t_0\). Those \(\gamma(t)\) are feasible points arbitrarily close to \(x^*\), which contradicts the minimality of \(x^*\). So \(p\) is a multiple of \(g\): \(p = \lambda g\) for some real \(\lambda\). \(\blacksquare\)
0039 Remark 2.3 (the proof uses only the local curve)
0040 For each prescribed tangent \(d\) in the tangent space \(\{v : \nabla h(x^*) \cdot v = 0\}\), the proof constructs a feasible curve through \(x^*\) with that tangent. Minimality rules out every such curve along which \(f\) decreases to first order, and the rest of the argument in the proof of Theorem 2.2 then forces \(\nabla f(x^*)\) to be a multiple of \(\nabla h(x^*)\).
0041 Example 2.5 (the regularity hypothesis is needed)
0042 Let \(U = \R^2\), \(h(x, y) = y^2 - x^3\) and \(f(x, y) = x\). At the origin \(\nabla h = (-3x^2, 2y) = (0, 0)\), so the regularity condition fails. The feasible set \(\{y^2 = x^3\}\) forces \(x = (y^2)^{1/3} \ge 0\), so \(f = x \ge 0 = f(0, 0)\) on it, and the origin is a minimizer. But \(\nabla f(0, 0) = (1, 0)\) is not a multiple of \(\nabla h(0, 0) = (0, 0)\), because \(\lambda (0, 0) = (0, 0)\) for every \(\lambda\). So the minimizer has no Lagrange multiplier, and Theorem 2.2 does not apply.
0043 Example 2.6 (a stationary point that is not a minimizer)
0044 Let \(f(x, y) = y^3\) and \(h(x, y) = x\) on \(\R^2\). Here \(\nabla h = (1, 0) \ne 0\), and \(\nabla f(0, 0) = (0, 3y^2)\) at \(y = 0\) is \((0, 0) = 0 \cdot \nabla h\), so the origin satisfies the Lagrange condition with \(\lambda = 0\). The feasible set is the \(y\)-axis. For every \(y > 0\) the point \((0, -y)\) is feasible and \(f(0, -y) = -y^3 < 0 = f(0, 0)\). These points lie arbitrarily close to the origin, so the origin is not a local minimizer, and therefore not a global one. The witness \((0, -1)\) alone already refutes global minimality.
0045 Proposition 2.4 (uniqueness of the multiplier)
0046 Let \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). If \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) are both Lagrange multipliers of \(f\) at \(x^*\) for the constraint \(h = 0\), then \(\lambda = \mu\).
0047 Proof
0048 Subtracting the two conditions gives \((\lambda - \mu) \nabla h(x^*) = 0\). Since \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\), its coordinates are not all zero, so \(\lambda - \mu = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)
0049 3A closed form under a budget constraint
0050 A symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix \(\Sigma\) is positive definite when \(x^{\top} \Sigma x > 0\) for every \(x \ne 0\). Positive definiteness is stronger than the non-negativity of Lesson 1, Proposition 3.2, because it excludes a zero value at a non-zero \(x\). Let \(\mathbf{1}\) be the vector of \(n\) ones, and consider
0051 \[\min_{w \in \R^n}\; f(w) = \tfrac12\, w^{\top} \Sigma w \quad \text{subject to} \quad \mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1.\]
0052 The constraint \(h(w) = \mathbf{1}^{\top} w - 1\) has gradient \(\nabla h = \mathbf{1} \ne 0\), so it meets the regularity hypothesis of Theorem 2.2. Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.2 applies to every local minimizer of \(f\) on the budget set. The next proposition does not rely on Theorem 2.2, because it proves the minimum directly.
0053 Proposition 3.2 (the minimum-variance weights)
0054 Let \(\Sigma\) be a positive definite \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(\Sigma\) is invertible. Put \(u = \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{1}\), \(s = \mathbf{1}^{\top} u\) and \(\lambda = 1/s\), and let \(w^* = \lambda u\). Then:
0055 (a) \(s > 0\), so \(\lambda\) is well defined; (b) \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w^* = 1\); (c) \(\nabla f(w^*) = \Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\), so \(\lambda\) is the Lagrange multiplier at \(w^*\); (d) \(w^*\) is the unique minimizer of \(f\) on \(\{w : \mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1\}\).
0056 The hypotheses are that \(\Sigma\) is symmetric and positive definite, and that the constraint is the budget \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1\). No condition on the sign of \(w\) is imposed.
0057 Proof
0058 Invertibility. If \(\Sigma x = 0\) for some \(x\), then \(x^{\top} \Sigma x = 0\), which forces \(x = 0\) by positive definiteness. So the only solution of \(\Sigma x = 0\) is zero, and \(\Sigma\) is invertible.
0059 (a). The vector \(u\) satisfies \(\Sigma u = \mathbf{1} \ne 0\), so \(u \ne 0\). Then \(s = \mathbf{1}^{\top} u = (\Sigma u)^{\top} u = u^{\top} \Sigma u > 0\), using symmetry of \(\Sigma\) in \((\Sigma u)^{\top} = u^{\top} \Sigma\).
0060 (b). \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}^{\top} u = \lambda s = 1\).
0061 (c). \(\nabla f(w) = \Sigma w\), because \(\nabla (\tfrac12 w^{\top} \Sigma w) = \tfrac12 (\Sigma + \Sigma^{\top}) w = \Sigma w\). Then \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \Sigma u = \lambda \mathbf{1}\).
0062 (d). Let \(w\) satisfy \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1\), and put \(d = w - w^*\), so \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\). Expanding,
0063 \[f(w) = f(w^*) + w^{*\top} \Sigma d + \tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d.\]
0064 By (c) and symmetry, \(w^{*\top} \Sigma d = (\Sigma w^*)^{\top} d = \lambda \mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\). So \(f(w) - f(w^*) = \tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d\). This is non-negative, and it is zero only when \(d = 0\), because \(\Sigma\) is positive definite. Hence \(w^*\) is the unique minimizer. \(\blacksquare\)
0065 Proposition 3.3 (the matrix of Lab 1 is positive definite)
0066 Let \(A\) be any real \(n \times n\) matrix. Then \(A^{\top} A + I\) is symmetric and positive definite.
0067 Proof
0068 The matrix \((A^{\top} A)^{\top} = A^{\top} A\), so \(A^{\top} A + I\) is symmetric. For any \(x\), \(x^{\top}(A^{\top} A + I) x = (Ax)^{\top}(Ax) + x^{\top} x = \|Ax\|^2 + \|x\|^2\). If \(x \ne 0\), the second term is positive, and the first is non-negative, so the sum is positive. \(\blacksquare\)
0069 Lab 1 · Minimum-variance weights in exact arithmetic
0070 Predicted from Proposition 3.2 and Proposition 3.3 before running. The matrix \(\Sigma = A^{\top} A + I\) is symmetric and positive definite, so the closed form applies. Proposition 3.2 predicts three things. The budget \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w\) equals 1 exactly. The gradient \(\Sigma w\) equals \(\lambda \mathbf{1}\) exactly, so every residual \(\Sigma w - \lambda \mathbf{1}\) is zero. For every direction \(d\) with \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} d = 0\) and every step \(t\), the increase \(f(w + t d) - f(w)\) equals \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\), which is non-negative. The entries of \(A\), \(\Sigma\), \(\lambda\) and \(w\) are computed from a fixed seed and printed; the lab does not predict them.

0072 Standard library only. No randomness and no network. The inputs come from a fixed linear congruential generator,
0073 so every run reads the same numbers. Every computation uses fractions.Fraction, so the residuals are exact zeros
0074 or exact non-zero values, not rounding errors.
0075 SIMULATED: the symmetric positive definite matrix Sigma is generated by this lab from a fixed seed.
0076 It is an illustrative input, not an estimate from data. The lab does not estimate Sigma and does not
0077 claim that it describes any market.
0078 The problem is: minimize f(w) = (1/2) w' Sigma w over w in R^3, subject to 1' w = 1. The lab
0079 1. forms Sigma = A' A + I from a 3 x 3 integer matrix A (so Sigma is symmetric and positive definite);
0080 2. solves Sigma u = 1 by exact Gaussian elimination, sets lambda = 1 / (1' u) and w = lambda u;
0081 3. checks that the budget holds exactly, that the gradient Sigma w equals lambda times the constraint normal 1
0082 exactly, and that f(w + t d) - f(w) equals (1/2) t^2 d' Sigma d for six directions d with 1' d = 0 and six
0083 values of t, so the increase is non-negative in every tested case.
0084 Checks on six directions and six step sizes are checks on those cases only. The claim for all directions is the
0085 proof on the page: Proposition 3.2 of the lesson gives the minimizer under the assumption that Sigma is positive
0086 definite, and Proposition 3.3 shows that A' A + I is positive definite for every real square A.
0087 """
0088 from __future__ import annotations
0089 from fractions import Fraction
0090 SEED = 1
0091 SIZE = 3
0092 def lcg_integers(seed: int, count: int) -> list[int]:
0093 """Deterministic integers in -3 to 3 from a linear congruential generator with fixed constants."""
0094 state = seed
0095 values: list[int] = []
0096 for _ in range(count):
0097 state = (1103515245 * state + 12345) % 2**31
0098 values.append(state % 7 - 3)
0099 return values
0100 def gram_plus_identity(entries: list[int], size: int) -> list[list[Fraction]]:
0101 """Sigma = A' A + I for the size x size matrix A given row by row in entries."""
0102 a = [[entries[i * size + j] for j in range(size)] for i in range(size)]
0103 sigma: list[list[Fraction]] = []
0104 for i in range(size):
0105 row: list[Fraction] = []
0106 for j in range(size):
0107 value = sum(a[k][i] * a[k][j] for k in range(size)) + (1 if i == j else 0)
0108 row.append(Fraction(value))
0109 sigma.append(row)
0110 return sigma
0111 def solve(matrix: list[list[Fraction]], rhs: list[Fraction]) -> list[Fraction]:
0112 """Solve matrix x = rhs by Gaussian elimination with exact fractions and partial pivoting."""
0113 n = len(rhs)
0114 rows = [[*matrix[i], rhs[i]] for i in range(n)]
0115 for col in range(n):
0116 pivot = next((r for r in range(col, n) if rows[r][col] != 0), None)
0117 if pivot is None:
0118 raise ValueError("the matrix is singular, so the system has no unique solution")
0119 rows[col], rows[pivot] = rows[pivot], rows[col]
0120 for r in range(n):
0121 if r != col and rows[r][col] != 0:
0122 factor = rows[r][col] / rows[col][col]
0123 rows[r] = [a - factor * b for a, b in zip(rows[r], rows[col], strict=True)]
0124 return [rows[i][n] / rows[i][i] for i in range(n)]
0125 def matvec(matrix: list[list[Fraction]], x: list[Fraction]) -> list[Fraction]:
0126 """The product matrix times x, exact."""
0127 return [sum((a * b for a, b in zip(row, x, strict=True)), Fraction(0)) for row in matrix]
0128 def quadratic(sigma: list[list[Fraction]], x: list[Fraction]) -> Fraction:
0129 """The value x' Sigma x, exact."""
0130 return sum((a * b for a, b in zip(x, matvec(sigma, x), strict=True)), Fraction(0))
0131 def directions() -> list[list[Fraction]]:
0132 """Six directions d with 1' d = 0 (components sum to zero), exact."""
0133 raw = [(1, -1, 0), (0, 1, -1), (1, 0, -1), (2, -1, -1), (-1, 2, -1), (1, 1, -2)]
0134 return [[Fraction(c) for c in d] for d in raw]
0135 def main() -> None:
0136 entries = lcg_integers(SEED, SIZE * SIZE)
0137 print(f"A entries (row by row, seed {SEED}): {entries}")
0138 sigma = gram_plus_identity(entries, SIZE)
0139 print("Sigma = A' A + I:")
0140 for row in sigma:
0141 print(" " + " ".join(str(x) for x in row))
0142 ones = [Fraction(1)] * SIZE
0143 u = solve(sigma, ones)
0144 total = sum(u, Fraction(0))
0145 lam = 1 / total
0146 w = [lam * x for x in u]
0147 print(f"1' Sigma^-1 1 = {total}, multiplier lambda = {lam}")
0148 print("minimum-variance weights w = lambda Sigma^-1 1:")
0149 print(" " + " ".join(str(x) for x in w))
0150 budget = sum(w, Fraction(0))
0151 print(f"budget 1' w = {budget}, equal to 1 exactly: {budget == 1}")
0152 gradient = matvec(sigma, w)
0153 residual = [g - lam * one for g, one in zip(gradient, ones, strict=True)]
0154 exact_zero = all(r == 0 for r in residual)
0155 print(f"gradient Sigma w minus lambda 1: {[str(r) for r in residual]}, all exactly zero: {exact_zero}")
0156 fw = quadratic(sigma, w) / 2
0157 print(f"objective f(w) = (1/2) w' Sigma w = {fw}")
0158 steps = [Fraction(-1, 2), Fraction(1, 2), Fraction(1), Fraction(-1), Fraction(2), Fraction(3, 2)]
0159 identity_ok = 0
0160 increases_ok = 0
0161 tested = 0

0171 print(f"f(w + t d) - f(w) is non-negative in {increases_ok} of {tested} tested cases")

0187 4Where the canon breaks
0188 The proposition above is proved on the page, and Theorem 2.2 is proved on the page from Assumption 1. The hypotheses carry the weight, and four points need care.
0189 Stationarity is necessary, not sufficient. Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.2 gives a necessary condition. Example 2.6 shows a stationary point that is not a minimizer, so a solution of \(\nabla f = \lambda \nabla h\) must still be checked.
0190 The regularity hypothesis cannot be dropped. Example 2.5 gives a minimizer at which \(\nabla h = 0\) and no multiplier exists. Theorem 2.2 is silent there, and the reader should not read it as a claim about such points.
0191 A local minimizer is not a global one. Theorem 2.2 is a statement about local minimizers. Proposition 3.2 proves global minimality, and only for the budget problem with positive definite \(\Sigma\). This lesson gives no global argument for other problems.
0192 The budget constraint is an equality. The closed form \(w^*\) can have negative entries, as Lab 1 shows. The solution of the problem with \(w \ge 0\) is a different problem, which Lesson 3 treats with the KKT conditions.
0193 5Misconceptions
0194 “At a constrained minimum the gradient of the objective is zero.”
0195 Theorem 2.2, under Assumption 1, says the gradient is a multiple of the constraint gradient. For \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) on the line \(x + y = 1\), the minimizer is \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) and \(\nabla f = (1, 1) = 1 \cdot \nabla h\), which is not zero. Lab 1 shows the same for the budget problem: its residual line reports \(\Sigma w^* - \lambda \mathbf{1} = 0\) with \(\lambda = 177/55\), so \(\Sigma w^* = \tfrac{177}{55} \mathbf{1}\) is not zero because the multiplier is not zero.
0196 “A point that satisfies the Lagrange condition is a minimizer.”
0197 Example 2.6 shows that it need not be. The condition is necessary, and the sufficiency for the budget problem comes from the quadratic structure and positive definiteness in Proposition 3.2, not from the condition alone.
0198 “The Lagrange condition holds at every minimizer, whatever the constraint.”
0199 Theorem 2.2 requires \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\). Example 2.5 is a minimizer where \(\nabla h\) vanishes and no multiplier exists, so the hypothesis is needed for the conclusion.

0202 Mechanics
0203 M1 · The point of the line nearest the origin
0204 Minimize \(x^2 + y^2\) subject to \(x + y = 1\). Find the minimizer and the multiplier.
0205 Solution
0206 Here \(f = x^2 + y^2\) and \(h = x + y - 1\), so \(\nabla f = (2x, 2y)\) and \(\nabla h = (1, 1) \ne 0\). The Lagrange condition gives \((2x, 2y) = \lambda (1, 1)\), so \(x = y = \lambda / 2\). The constraint gives \(\lambda = 1\), so \(x = y = \tfrac12\). The multiplier is \(\lambda = 1\), and the minimizer is \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). The point is the foot of the perpendicular from the origin to the line, and it is the closest point of the line to the origin, since \(x^2 + y^2\) is the squared distance.
0207 M2 · The closed form for a diagonal matrix
0208 Let \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)\). Apply Proposition 3.2: compute \(u\), \(s\), \(\lambda\), \(w^*\) and \(f(w^*)\).
0209 Solution
0210 Solving \(\Sigma u = \mathbf{1}\) gives \(u = (1, \tfrac12)\). Then \(s = 1 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac32\), so \(\lambda = \tfrac23\) and \(w^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\). The budget is \(\tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = 1\). The gradient is \(\Sigma w^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac23) = \tfrac23 \mathbf{1}\), as Proposition 3.2 (c) requires. The objective is \(f(w^*) = \tfrac12 (\tfrac49 \cdot 1 + \tfrac19 \cdot 2) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac23 = \tfrac13\).
0211 M3 · Checking the increase formula by hand
0212 With \(\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1, 2)\) and \(w^* = (\tfrac23, \tfrac13)\) from Problem M2, let \(d = (1, -1)\) and \(t = 1\). Compute \(f(w^* + t d) - f(w^*)\) directly and compare it with \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\).
0213 Solution
0214 The point \(w^* + d = (\tfrac53, -\tfrac23)\) gives \(f = \tfrac12 (\tfrac{25}{9} + 2 \cdot \tfrac49) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{33}{9} = \tfrac{11}{6}\). From Problem M2, \(f(w^*) = \tfrac13 = \tfrac26\). The increase is \(\tfrac{11}{6} - \tfrac{2}{6} = \tfrac{9}{6} = \tfrac32\). The formula gives \(\tfrac12 \cdot 1 \cdot d^{\top} \Sigma d = \tfrac12 (1 + 2) = \tfrac32\), so the two agree, as the proof of Proposition 3.2 (d) predicts.
0215 Proof
0216 P1 · The multiplier of a regular constraint is unique
0217 Prove Proposition 2.4 from the definition of a Lagrange multiplier, and state where the regularity hypothesis is used.
0218 Solution
0219 If \(\lambda\) and \(\mu\) both satisfy \(\nabla f(x^*) = \lambda \nabla h(x^*)\) and \(\nabla f(x^*) = \mu \nabla h(x^*)\), then subtracting gives \((\lambda - \mu) \nabla h(x^*) = 0\). The regularity hypothesis \(\nabla h(x^*) \ne 0\) says that this vector has a non-zero coordinate, so the scalar \(\lambda - \mu\) must be zero. The hypothesis is used only in the last step, and without it any \(\lambda\) would satisfy the condition when \(\nabla f(x^*) = 0\).
0220 P2 · Positive definiteness is needed for uniqueness
0221 Let \(\Sigma = 0\) in \(\R^2\), with the budget \(w_1 + w_2 = 1\). Show that every feasible point is a minimizer, and identify the step of Proposition 3.2 that fails.
0222 Solution
0223 With \(\Sigma = 0\), \(f(w) = 0\) for every \(w\), so every point of the budget line attains the minimum value 0. The minimizer is not unique. In the proof of Proposition 3.2, step (invertibility) fails, because \(\Sigma\) has no inverse and \(u\) is undefined. Step (d) also fails at its last claim: the increase \(\tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d\) is zero for every \(d\), not zero only when \(d = 0\). Positive definiteness is the hypothesis that prevents both failures.
0224 P3 · The minimum value of the budget problem
0225 Under the hypotheses of Proposition 3.2, show that \(f(w^*) = \tfrac{1}{2s}\), and that \(f(w^*) = \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\).
0226 Solution
0227 By Proposition 3.2 (c), \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\), so \(w^{*\top} \Sigma w^* = \lambda\, \mathbf{1}^{\top} w^* = \lambda\) by the budget (b). Therefore \(f(w^*) = \tfrac12 \lambda\). Since \(\lambda = 1/s\), this is \(\tfrac{1}{2s}\). The value is the reciprocal of \(s = \mathbf{1}^{\top} \Sigma^{-1} \mathbf{1}\), halved.

0233 E2 · What the two 36-of-36 lines do and do not show (Lab 1)
0234 Lab 1 prints that the increase equals \(\tfrac12 t^2 d^{\top} \Sigma d\) in 36 of 36 tested cases and is non-negative in 36 of 36. State what these counts establish and what they do not.
0235 Solution
0236 The counts establish the identity and the non-negativity for the six directions and six step sizes in the lab, because the arithmetic is exact. They do not establish optimality over all directions, which is the work of Proposition 3.2 (d). The proof there covers every feasible direction, and the lab is a numerical instance of it. The identity itself holds for every positive definite \(\Sigma\) by the proof of Proposition 3.2 (d). The count does not extend the claim to other matrices; the proof does.
0237 E3 · Reading the objective value (Lab 1)

0239 Solution

0242 R1 · The sign constraint changes the problem
0243 The Lab 1 solution has \(w_3 = -9/55 < 0\). Explain why this solution does not answer the question of the minimum-variance problem with \(w \ge 0\), and say which lesson treats that question.
0244 Solution
0245 Proposition 3.2 minimizes over the budget set with no sign restriction, so its unique minimizer is the minimizer of the problem without the constraint \(w \ge 0\). The minimizer has a negative entry, so it violates \(w \ge 0\). A minimizer of the problem with \(w \ge 0\) must lie in the feasible set of that problem, and the lab's point is not in it. The problem with a sign constraint is an inequality-constrained problem, treated in Lesson 3.
0246 R2 · Why the budget constraint satisfies the regularity hypothesis
0247 Show that the budget constraint \(h(w) = \mathbf{1}^{\top} w - 1\) satisfies the regularity hypothesis of Theorem 2.2 at every point, and explain why Proposition 3.2 does not need that theorem.
0248 Solution
0249 The gradient of \(h\) is the constant vector \(\mathbf{1}\), which is non-zero at every point, so the hypothesis \(\nabla h \ne 0\) holds everywhere. Under Assumption 1, Theorem 2.2 then gives the necessary condition \(\Sigma w^* = \lambda \mathbf{1}\). Proposition 3.2 does not need the theorem, because part (d) proves minimality directly: \(f(w) - f(w^*) = \tfrac12 d^{\top} \Sigma d \ge 0\) for every feasible \(w\). That is a sufficiency argument, which the necessity theorem cannot provide.
0250 R3 · The multiplier as a rate of change of the minimum value
0251 Let \(c\) be a real number and consider the budget constraint \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = c\) with \(c\) in place of 1. Show that the minimum value is \(c^2 / (2s)\), and that its derivative with respect to \(c\) is \(c/s\). Check that at \(c = 1\) the derivative is the multiplier \(\lambda = 1/s\).
0252 Solution
0253 The proof of Proposition 3.2 with the budget \(\mathbf{1}^{\top} w = c\) in place of 1 gives the unique minimizer \(c w^*\), since every step is linear in the budget. Its objective is \(\tfrac12 c^2 w^{*\top} \Sigma w^* = \tfrac12 c^2 \lambda \cdot 1 = c^2 / (2s)\), using Problem P3. The derivative of \(c^2/(2s)\) with respect to \(c\) is \(c/s\), which equals \(\lambda\) at \(c = 1\). At \(c = 1\) the derivative of the minimum value equals the multiplier. This is one case computed on the page. The general statement for perturbations of the constraint is not proved on this page.
