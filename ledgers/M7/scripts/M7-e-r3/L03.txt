0012 Dlore
0013 Xrefuted
0014 A minimizer of a function subject to constraints can sit on the boundary of the set of points that satisfy every constraint (the feasible set, Definition 1.1), where some constraints hold with equality and others do not. The KKT conditions are four checkable tests that a candidate point and a set of multipliers must pass. This lesson states them, proves that they are sufficient for optimality when the problem is convex, shows by example that sufficiency fails without convexity, and applies them to a two-asset allocation problem. Lab 1 checks each condition with exact arithmetic.
0015 1The problem and the conditions
0016 Definition 1.1 (the problem (P))
0017 Let \(f\), \(g_1, \dots, g_m\) and \(h_1, \dots, h_p\) be real functions on \(\R^n\), each differentiable. The problem (P) is to minimize \(f(x)\) over \(x \in \R^n\) subject to \(g_i(x) \le 0\) for \(i = 1, \dots, m\) and \(h_j(x) = 0\) for \(j = 1, \dots, p\). The feasible set \(F\) is the set of points that satisfy all \(m + p\) constraints. A point \(x^\ast\) is optimal if \(x^\ast \in F\) and \(f(x^\ast) \le f(x)\) for every \(x \in F\). The value \(p^\ast\) is the infimum of \(f\) over \(F\), when \(F\) is non-empty. A constraint \(g_i(x) \le 0\) is active (or binding) at \(x\) when \(g_i(x) = 0\), and slack when \(g_i(x) < 0\).
0018 Definition 1.2 (the KKT conditions)
0019 The Lagrangian of (P) is \(L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x)\), for \(\lambda \in \R^m\) and \(\nu \in \R^p\). A pair \((x^\ast, (\lambda, \nu))\) is a Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) point of (P) when it satisfies four conditions:
0020 (K1) stationarity. \(\nabla f(x^\ast) + \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i(x^\ast) + \sum_j \nu_j \nabla h_j(x^\ast) = 0\). This says that \(\nabla_x L(x^\ast, \lambda, \nu) = 0\).
0021 (K2) primal feasibility. \(x^\ast \in F\).
0022 (K3) dual feasibility. \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) for every \(i\).
0023 (K4) complementary slackness. \(\lambda_i \, g_i(x^\ast) = 0\) for every \(i\).
0024 A KKT residual is the amount by which a candidate fails one of these four conditions. A residual of zero means the condition holds. The numbers \(\lambda_i\) and \(\nu_j\) are the multipliers of the candidate.
0025 Remark 1.3 (what K4 says)
0026 Condition (K4) says that for each \(i\), at least one of \(\lambda_i\) and \(g_i(x^\ast)\) is zero. It does not say which one. A slack constraint must have a zero multiplier, and an active constraint can have a zero multiplier: for minimizing \(x^2\) subject to \(-x \le 0\), the point \(x = 0\) is active, the gradient of \(x^2\) there is zero, and (K1) holds with multiplier zero. Section 4 uses the slack case and the active case with a positive multiplier.
0027 Lemma 1.4 (gradient inequality)
0028 Let \(\varphi : \R^n \to \R\) be convex and differentiable. Then for all \(x, y \in \R^n\), \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(y) + \nabla \varphi(y)^{\top}(x - y)\). In particular, if \(\nabla \varphi(y) = 0\), then \(y\) minimizes \(\varphi\) over \(\R^n\).
0029 Proof
0030 Convexity means \(\varphi(\theta a + (1 - \theta) b) \le \theta \varphi(a) + (1 - \theta) \varphi(b)\) for \(\theta \in [0, 1]\). Fix \(y\) and \(x\). For \(t \in (0, 1]\), take \(a = x\), \(b = y\), \(\theta = t\), which gives \(\varphi(y + t(x - y)) \le t\varphi(x) + (1 - t)\varphi(y)\). Subtract \(\varphi(y)\) and divide by \(t > 0\): \[\frac{\varphi(y + t(x - y)) - \varphi(y)}{t} \le \varphi(x) - \varphi(y).\] As \(t \downarrow 0\), the left side tends to \(\nabla \varphi(y)^{\top}(x - y)\), by the definition of the derivative in the direction \(x - y\). The inequality is preserved in the limit, so \(\nabla \varphi(y)^{\top}(x - y) \le \varphi(x) - \varphi(y)\). If \(\nabla \varphi(y) = 0\), this gives \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(y)\) for every \(x\). \(\blacksquare\)
0031 Lemma 1.5 (a quadratic with a positive semidefinite matrix is convex)
0032 Let \(\Sigma\) be a symmetric \(n \times n\) matrix with \(x^{\top}\Sigma x \ge 0\) for all \(x\), and let \(c \in \R^n\). Then \(q(w) = \tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w - c^{\top}w\) is convex on \(\R^n\), and its gradient is \(\nabla q(w) = \Sigma w - c\).
0033 Proof
0034 Let \(x, y \in \R^n\) and \(\theta \in [0, 1]\), and put \(z = \theta x + (1 - \theta) y\). Expand the quadratic part using the symmetry of \(\Sigma\). The linear part \(-c^{\top}\) is the same on both sides, because \(c^{\top}z = \theta c^{\top}x + (1 - \theta) c^{\top}y\), so it cancels. For the quadratic part, \[\begin{aligned} &\theta \tfrac12 x^{\top}\Sigma x + (1 - \theta) \tfrac12 y^{\top}\Sigma y - \tfrac12 z^{\top}\Sigma z \\ &\quad= \tfrac12 \theta (1 - \theta)\,(x - y)^{\top}\Sigma (x - y). \end{aligned}\] The check: the coefficient of \(x^{\top}\Sigma x\) on the left is \(\theta/2 - \theta^2/2 = \theta(1-\theta)/2\), the same for \(y^{\top}\Sigma y\), and the cross term \(-\theta(1-\theta)\,x^{\top}\Sigma y\) matches \(-\theta(1-\theta)\,x^{\top}\Sigma y\) on the right. Since \(\Sigma\) is positive semidefinite, the right side is at least zero, so \(q(z) \le \theta q(x) + (1 - \theta) q(y)\). The gradient formula follows by differentiating \(\tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w\) with \(\Sigma\) symmetric. \(\blacksquare\)
0035 2Sufficiency under convexity
0036 Theorem 2.1 (KKT conditions are sufficient)
0037 Assume that \(f\) and each \(g_i\) are convex and differentiable on \(\R^n\), and that each \(h_j\) is affine, that is, of the form \(a^{\top}x + b\). Let \((x^\ast, (\lambda, \nu))\) be a KKT point of (P). Then \(x^\ast\) is optimal for (P), and \(f(x^\ast) = p^\ast\).
0038 Proof
0039 Let \(\varphi(x) = L(x, \lambda, \nu) = f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i g_i(x) + \sum_j \nu_j h_j(x)\). Each term is convex: \(f\) by hypothesis; \(\lambda_i g_i\) because \(\lambda_i \ge 0\) by (K3) and \(g_i\) is convex; \(\nu_j h_j\) because \(h_j\) is affine. A sum of convex functions is convex, so \(\varphi\) is convex and differentiable. Its gradient at \(x^\ast\) is \(\nabla f(x^\ast) + \sum_i \lambda_i \nabla g_i(x^\ast) + \sum_j \nu_j \nabla h_j(x^\ast)\), which is zero by (K1). By Lemma 1.4, \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(x^\ast)\) for every \(x \in \R^n\).
0040 Now evaluate the two ends. At \(x^\ast\), (K4) gives \(\lambda_i g_i(x^\ast) = 0\) and (K2) gives \(h_j(x^\ast) = 0\), so \(\varphi(x^\ast) = f(x^\ast)\). Let \(x\) be any point of \(F\). Then \(g_i(x) \le 0\) and \(\lambda_i \ge 0\), so \(\lambda_i g_i(x) \le 0\), and \(h_j(x) = 0\). Hence \(\varphi(x) \le f(x)\). Combining, \[f(x) \ge \varphi(x) \ge \varphi(x^\ast) = f(x^\ast).\] This holds for every \(x \in F\), and \(x^\ast \in F\) by (K2), so \(x^\ast\) is optimal and \(f(x^\ast) = p^\ast\). \(\blacksquare\)
0041 Remark 2.2 (what the proof uses)
0042 The proof uses the four conditions, the convexity and differentiability of \(f\) and of the \(g_i\), and the affineness of the \(h_j\). It does not use any hypothesis beyond these, and it does not need the multipliers to be unique. Sufficiency is a statement about a candidate that is already known to pass the four tests. Example 3.1 (Section 3) shows that an optimal point can have no KKT point at all, so whether a candidate exists is a separate question.
0043 Example 2.3 (a KKT point that is not optimal)
0044 Take \(n = 1\), \(f(x) = x^3\), and no constraints. Then \(f'(0) = 0\), so \(x^\ast = 0\) with no multipliers satisfies (K1) to (K4) trivially. But \(f(-1) = -1 < 0 = f(0)\), so \(0\) is not optimal. Also \(f(1) = 1 > 0 = f(0)\), so \(0\) is not a maximizer either. In fact \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(x \to -\infty\), so this problem has no minimizer at all. The function is not convex: at \(x = -1\) and \(y = 0\), with \(\theta = \tfrac12\), the midpoint value is \(f(-\tfrac12) = -\tfrac18\), while the average of the endpoint values is \(\tfrac12 f(-1) + \tfrac12 f(0) = -\tfrac12\). Since \(-\tfrac18 > -\tfrac12\), the convexity inequality fails. The example has no constraints and \(f\) is differentiable, so convexity of \(f\) is the one hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 that fails.
0045 3An optimum with no multipliers
0046 Example 3.1 (an optimum with no multipliers)
0047 Take \(n = 1\), \(f(x) = x\), \(m = 1\), \(g_1(x) = x^2\), and no equality constraints. The feasible set is \(F = \{x : x^2 \le 0\} = \{0\}\), so \(x^\ast = 0\) is the only feasible point and therefore optimal. Here \(f\) is affine and \(g_1\) is convex, by Lemma 1.5 with \(\Sigma = 2\) and \(c = 0\). The hypotheses of Theorem 2.1 hold. Yet no KKT point exists at \(x^\ast\). The gradient of \(g_1\) at \(0\) is \(2 \cdot 0 = 0\), so (K1) reads \(1 + \lambda_1 \cdot 0 = 0\), which is false for every \(\lambda_1\). Slater's condition asks for a point \(\bar x\) with \(g_i(\bar x) < 0\) for every \(i\) and \(h_j(\bar x) = 0\) for every \(j\). Here no such point exists, since no \(\bar x\) satisfies \(\bar x^2 < 0\), so Slater's condition fails.
0048 The converse direction, that an optimum has a KKT point under Slater's condition, is not treated on this page.
0049 4A two-asset problem
0050 Consider the allocation problem
0051 \[\begin{aligned} \text{(P}_{\mathrm{A}}\text{)}\quad &\min_{w \in \R^2}\ f(w) = \tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w - \mu^{\top} w \\ &\text{subject to}\quad \mathbf{1}^{\top} w = 1,\quad w_1 \ge 0,\quad w_2 \ge 0. \end{aligned}\]
0052 with \(\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\) and a vector \(\mu \in \R^2\). The matrix \(\Sigma\) and the vector \(\mu\) are given inputs of this unit, chosen by hand as illustrative numbers. They are SIMULATED and are not market data. The objective has the form \(\tfrac{\gamma}{2} w^{\top}\Sigma w - \mu^{\top} w\) with \(\gamma = 1\), and returns are in one period with no annualization. The matrix is symmetric, and for any \(a, b\), \((a, b)\Sigma(a, b)^{\top} = a^2 + b^2 + (a + b)^2 \ge 0\), so Lemma 1.5 applies. The constraints are affine, so \(g_i(w) = -w_i\) are convex, and \(h(w) = \mathbf{1}^{\top}w - 1\) is affine. Theorem 2.1 therefore applies to any KKT point of (PA). In the conditions below, \(\nabla g_1 = (-1, 0)^{\top}\), \(\nabla g_2 = (0, -1)^{\top}\), and \(\nabla h = (1, 1)^{\top}\).
0053 Example 4.1 (the active instance, \(\mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac15)\))
0054 Let \(w^\ast = (1, 0)\). Then \(\nabla f(w^\ast) = \Sigma w^\ast - \mu = (2, 1) - (\tfrac32, \tfrac15) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac45)\). Feasibility (K2) holds: \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}w^\ast = 1\), \(g_1(w^\ast) = -1 \le 0\), and \(g_2(w^\ast) = 0 \le 0\). Complementary slackness (K4) for the first constraint has \(g_1(w^\ast) = -1 \neq 0\), so \(\lambda_1 = 0\). Stationarity (K1) reads, componentwise, \(\tfrac12 + \nu - \lambda_1 = 0\) and \(\tfrac45 + \nu - \lambda_2 = 0\). With \(\lambda_1 = 0\), the first gives \(\nu = -\tfrac12\), and the second gives \(\lambda_2 = \tfrac45 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac{3}{10}\). Then \(\lambda_2 \ge 0\) (K3), and \(\lambda_2 g_2(w^\ast) = \tfrac{3}{10} \cdot 0 = 0\) (K4). So \((w^\ast, (\lambda, \nu))\) with \(\lambda = (0, \tfrac{3}{10})\) and \(\nu = -\tfrac12\) is a KKT point. By Theorem 2.1, \(w^\ast\) is optimal, and \(p^\ast = f(w^\ast) = \tfrac12 \cdot 2 - \tfrac32 = -\tfrac12\). The binding constraint is \(w_2 \ge 0\), and it carries the positive multiplier.
0055 Example 4.2 (the inactive instance, \(\mu = (1, 1)\))
0056 Let \(w^\ast = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). Then \(\Sigma w^\ast = (\tfrac32, \tfrac32)\), so \(\nabla f(w^\ast) = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\). Both weights are positive, so \(g_1(w^\ast) = g_2(w^\ast) = -\tfrac12 \neq 0\), and (K4) forces \(\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0\). Stationarity then reads \(\tfrac12 + \nu = 0\) in both components, so \(\nu = -\tfrac12\). Feasibility and dual feasibility hold, so this is a KKT point, and Theorem 2.1 gives optimality. The optimal value is \(f(w^\ast) = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac32 - 1 = -\tfrac14\), where \(w^{\ast\top}\Sigma w^\ast = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac32 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac32 = \tfrac32\) and \(\mu^{\top}w^\ast = 1\).

0062 Remark 4.3 (the stationary point of the objective alone)
0063 Solving \(\nabla f(w) = 0\) with no constraints gives \(w = \Sigma^{-1}\mu\). For \(\mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac15)\) and \(\Sigma^{-1} = \tfrac13 \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\), this is \(w = (\tfrac{14}{15}, -\tfrac{11}{30})\). It has a negative second weight, and its weights sum to \(\tfrac{17}{30} \neq 1\), so it is not feasible. Feasibility and the multipliers are part of the test, and stationarity of \(f\) alone does not decide the constrained problem.
0064 5Where the canon breaks
0065 The conditions of this lesson carry four limits, and each one is shown by an example or a lab on the page.
0066 A KKT point is not automatically a minimizer. Example 2.3 gives a KKT point of a non-convex function that is not a minimizer, and that has no minimizer at all. Theorem 2.1 needs the convexity hypotheses.
0067 A minimizer can have no multipliers. Example 3.1 has a convex problem whose optimum has no KKT point, and Slater's condition fails there. The converse direction is not treated on this page.
0068 Lab 1 checks a finite set. The residuals are exact at the printed candidates, and the grid comparison covers 101 points. Neither one is the proof of optimality, which comes from Theorem 2.1.
0069 The lesson does not solve a KKT system in general. The two instances are solved by hand, with the active set chosen by inspection. Finding the active set for a larger problem is the subject of Lesson 5.
0070 6Misconceptions
0071 “Any point that satisfies the KKT conditions is the optimum.”
0072 Theorem 2.1 proves that a KKT point is optimal when \(f\) and the \(g_i\) are convex and the \(h_j\) are affine. Without convexity the claim can fail: Example 2.3 shows a KKT point of \(x^3\) that is not a minimizer. Without convexity, a KKT point can be a minimizer, a maximizer or neither, and the four conditions do not tell these apart. The function \(f(x) = (x^2 - 1)^2\) with no constraints is not convex, and its KKT points \(x = -1\) and \(x = 1\) are global minimizers with value 0. Its KKT point \(x = 0\) is a local maximizer, not a minimizer, and Problem R1 gives a KKT point that is a global maximizer.
0073 “Complementary slackness means every constraint is binding at the optimum.”
0074 Condition (K4) says \(\lambda_i g_i(x^\ast) = 0\), which allows either factor to be zero. In Example 4.1 the constraint \(w_1 \ge 0\) is slack, with \(g_1(w^\ast) = -1\), and its multiplier is zero. In Example 4.2 both constraints are slack and both multipliers are zero. The binding constraint in Example 4.1 has the positive multiplier \(\tfrac{3}{10}\). A positive multiplier forces its constraint to bind, by (K4). A zero multiplier does not force its constraint to be slack, so the two cases must be read off the point itself.
0075 “The point where the gradient of the objective vanishes is the answer.”
0076 Remark 4.3 gives the unconstrained stationary point of the two-asset objective, \((\tfrac{14}{15}, -\tfrac{11}{30})\). Its second weight is negative and its weights sum to \(\tfrac{17}{30}\), so it violates two constraints. Stationarity must be combined with feasibility and with the multipliers, which is what (K1) to (K4) do together.

0079 Mechanics
0080 M1 · KKT for a circle-and-line problem
0081 Minimize \(f(x, y) = x^2 + y^2\) subject to \(g(x, y) = 1 - x - y \le 0\). Find a KKT point and its optimal value.
0082 Solution
0083 Here \(\nabla f = (2x, 2y)\) and \(\nabla g = (-1, -1)\). Stationarity (K1) gives \(2x - \lambda = 0\) and \(2y - \lambda = 0\), so \(x = y = \lambda/2\). Complementary slackness (K4) with \(\lambda\) possibly positive forces \(g(x, y) = 0\), since a zero multiplier would give \(x = y = 0\), which violates \(x + y \ge 1\). So \(x + y = 1\), which with \(x = y\) gives \(x = y = \tfrac12\) and \(\lambda = 1\). Then \(\lambda \ge 0\) (K3). The point is optimal by Theorem 2.1, since \(f\) and \(g\) are convex, and \(p^\ast = f(\tfrac12, \tfrac12) = \tfrac12\).
0084 M2 · Comparing two feasible points in Example 4.2
0085 For the inactive instance (\(\mu = (1, 1)\)), compute \(f(\tfrac35, \tfrac25)\) exactly and compare it with the optimal value.
0086 Solution
0087 We have \(\Sigma w = (2 \cdot \tfrac35 + \tfrac25, \tfrac35 + 2 \cdot \tfrac25) = (\tfrac85, \tfrac75)\). Then \(w^{\top}\Sigma w = \tfrac35 \cdot \tfrac85 + \tfrac25 \cdot \tfrac75 = \tfrac{24}{25} + \tfrac{14}{25} = \tfrac{38}{25}\), so \(\tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w = \tfrac{19}{25}\). Also \(\mu^{\top}w = 1\). Hence \(f(\tfrac35, \tfrac25) = \tfrac{19}{25} - 1 = -\tfrac{6}{25}\). Since \(-\tfrac{6}{25} = -0.24 > -0.25 = -\tfrac14\), the point is feasible and worse than the optimum, as Example 4.2 requires.
0088 M3 · No multipliers at a non-optimal candidate
0089 For the active instance (\(\mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac15)\)), show that \(w = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\) is not a KKT point for any multipliers.
0090 Solution
0091 Both weights are positive, so \(g_1(w) = g_2(w) = -\tfrac12 \ne 0\), and (K4) forces \(\lambda = 0\). Then (K1) requires \(\nabla f(w) + \nu \mathbf{1} = 0\). Here \(\nabla f(w) = \Sigma w - \mu = (\tfrac32, \tfrac32) - (\tfrac32, \tfrac15) = (0, \tfrac{13}{10})\). The first component forces \(\nu = 0\), and then the second component is \(\tfrac{13}{10} \ne 0\). So no \(\nu\) works, and no KKT point exists at this candidate. Its objective value is \(\tfrac34 - \tfrac{17}{20} = -\tfrac{1}{10}\), which is above the optimum \(-\tfrac12\) of Example 4.1, so Theorem 2.1 gives no certificate here.
0092 Proof
0093 P1 · Convexity of a multiplier term
0094 Let \(g\) be convex and \(\lambda \ge 0\). Prove that \(\lambda g\) is convex, and state where \(\lambda \ge 0\) is used.
0095 Solution
0096 For \(\theta \in [0, 1]\), convexity of \(g\) gives \(g(\theta a + (1 - \theta)b) \le \theta g(a) + (1 - \theta) g(b)\). Multiply by \(\lambda \ge 0\), which preserves the inequality: \(\lambda g(\theta a + (1 - \theta) b) \le \theta \lambda g(a) + (1 - \theta) \lambda g(b)\). This is the convexity inequality for \(\lambda g\). The sign condition \(\lambda \ge 0\) is used at the multiplication step; for \(\lambda < 0\) the inequality reverses, so \(\lambda g\) satisfies the concavity inequality. For example, with \(g(x) = x^2\) and \(\lambda = -1\), \(\lambda g = -x^2\) is not convex: at \(x = 1\) and \(y = -1\) the midpoint value is \(0\), the average of the endpoint values is \(-1\), and \(0 > -1\).
0097 P2 · The gradient inequality for a square
0098 Verify Lemma 1.4 directly for \(\varphi(x) = x^2\) on \(\R\), for all \(x, y\).
0099 Solution
0100 Here \(\varphi'(y) = 2y\), and the claim is \(x^2 \ge y^2 + 2y(x - y)\). Expanding the right side gives \(y^2 + 2xy - 2y^2 = 2xy - y^2\). The difference is \(x^2 - 2xy + y^2 = (x - y)^2 \ge 0\). This is the lemma for this function, with equality exactly at \(x = y\).
0101 P3 · A lower bound on the active instance
0102 For the active instance (Example 4.1), prove that \(f(w) \ge -\tfrac12\) for every feasible \(w\), using the KKT point and not the full optimality argument.
0103 Solution
0104 Let \(w\) be feasible and put \(d = w - w^\ast\). For the quadratic \(q(w) = \tfrac12 w^{\top}\Sigma w - \mu^{\top}w\), expanding \(q(w^\ast + d)\) gives \(q(w^\ast) + (\Sigma w^\ast - \mu)^{\top} d + \tfrac12 d^{\top}\Sigma d\). The last term is non-negative because \(\Sigma\) is positive semidefinite, as shown in Section 4 for this matrix. Feasibility gives \(w_1 = 1 - w_2\), so \(d = (-w_2, w_2)\), and \((\Sigma w^\ast - \mu)^{\top} d = (\tfrac12)(-w_2) + (\tfrac45)(w_2) = \tfrac{3}{10} w_2\). Hence \(f(w) \ge -\tfrac12 + \tfrac{3}{10} w_2\), and \(w_2 \ge 0\) gives \(f(w) \ge -\tfrac12\). The coefficient \(\tfrac{3}{10}\) is the multiplier \(\lambda_2\) of Example 4.1.

0110 E2 · What the grid can and cannot show (formula input)
0111 Lab 1 reports the minimum over the grid \(w_1 = k/100\), \(k = 0, \dots, 100\). State the number of grid points, and say what the grid minimum does not establish.
0112 Solution
0113 The values \(k = 0, \dots, 100\) give \(101\) grid points, matching the lab's count. The grid minimum is the smallest value among these points, so it is not smaller than the true minimum over the whole segment. It does not establish that the true minimum lies on the grid, and only Theorem 2.1 establishes optimality.
0114 E3 · The objective at the inactive candidate (formula input)

0116 Solution

0119 R1 · A KKT point that is a maximum
0120 Show that \(x^\ast = 0\) satisfies (K1) to (K4) for the problem of maximizing \(x^2\) on \(\R\), written as minimizing \(-x^2\). Say whether it is a minimizer, and identify the hypothesis of Theorem 2.1 that fails.
0121 Solution
0122 Here \(f(x) = -x^2\) with no constraints. Its derivative at \(0\) is \(0\), so (K1) holds with no multipliers, and (K2) to (K4) hold trivially. But \(f(x) = -x^2 < 0\) for \(x \ne 0\), and \(f(0) = 0\), so \(0\) is the global maximum of \(f\), and \(f\) has no minimizer, because \(f(x) \to -\infty\) as \(|x| \to \infty\). The hypothesis that fails is the convexity of \(f\): \(-x^2\) is concave, not convex, and the proof of Theorem 2.1 uses convexity at the step \(\varphi(x) \ge \varphi(x^\ast)\).
0123 R2 · Which part of Slater's condition fails in Example 3.1
0124 In Example 3.1, verify that the objective and constraint are convex and differentiable, and identify the part of Slater's condition that fails.
0125 Solution
0126 The objective \(x\) is affine, hence convex, and \(g_1(x) = x^2\) is convex by Lemma 1.5 with \(\Sigma = 2\) and \(c = 0\). Both are differentiable. The point \(x^\ast = 0\) is optimal, as shown in Example 3.1. Slater's condition needs a point \(\bar x\) with \(\bar x^2 < 0\), and no point satisfies this, so the strict inequality part of Slater's condition fails. There are no equality constraints, so that part of the condition holds vacuously.
0127 R3 · Why sufficiency needs no Slater point
0128 Explain why Theorem 2.1 needs no Slater point, and what Example 3.1 shows about a statement that starts from an optimal point and must produce multipliers.
0129 Solution
0130 Theorem 2.1 starts from a point and multipliers that already satisfy (K1) to (K4), and it checks that the point is optimal. The proof uses the convexity of the functions and the four conditions, and it never has to produce a multiplier. A statement that starts from an optimal point and must produce multipliers runs the other way. Example 3.1 shows that such a statement cannot rest on convexity alone: the objective and constraint are convex, the point is optimal, and no multiplier exists, because the gradient \(\nabla g_1(0)\) is zero, which makes (K1) false for every multiplier. Slater's condition also fails there.
