52: A fair game has zero expected gain at every step, and a martingale is its formal model. A player may try to beat the game by choosing when to quit. The doubling strategy of Definition 10.5 is the classic attempt. This lesson proves, assuming Assumption CE of L07, an optional stopping theorem for bounded stopping times (Theorem 10.3, §1), checks the doubling strategy with finite capital directly (§2), and shows that the no-capital version breaks it (§3). The conclusion depends on a boundedness hypothesis that the proof uses.
54: 1The stopped martingale
56: Let \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\) be a probability space with a filtration \((\mathcal F_k)\), and let \(M=(M_k)_{k\ge 0}\) be a martingale: \(M\) is adapted, each \(M_k\) is integrable, and \(\E[M_{k+1}\mid\mathcal F_k]=M_k\). The identity is the one of L08, Definition 2.2. L08 states it over a finite horizon, and this lesson applies it for every \(k\ge 0\). Integrability is stated here as a hypothesis, because L08's finite sample space made every variable integrable without saying so. The martingale property gives \(\E[M_{k+1}\indic_A]=\E[M_k\indic_A]\) for every event \(A\in\mathcal F_k\). This follows from \(\E[M_{k+1}\mid\mathcal F_k]=M_k\) by the defining property of conditional expectation. L07 proves that property on a finite probability space, and on a general space it is Assumption CE of L07, stated there and not proved. Lemma 10.2, Theorem 10.3, the martingale claim of Lemma 10.6 and Proposition 10.9(c) are therefore stated assuming Assumption CE. Let \(\tau\) be a stopping time (L09).

82: Theorem 10.3 (optional stopping, bounded case)
84: \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\) is a probability space, and \((\mathcal F_k)_{k\ge 0}\) is a filtration on it.
85: \(M=(M_k)_{k\ge 0}\) is a martingale on \((\mathcal F_k)\).
86: \(\tau\) is a stopping time on \((\mathcal F_k)\), and there is a fixed whole number \(N\ge 0\) with \(\tau(\omega)\le N\) for every outcome \(\omega\).
87: Assumption CE holds (stated in §1, not proved here).
89: Assuming Assumption CE, \(M_\tau\) is integrable and \(\E[M_\tau]=\E[M_0]\). This statement is the optional stopping theorem for bounded stopping times.
93: Proof
94: Since \(\tau\le N\) everywhere, \(\tau\wedge N=\tau\), so \(M_\tau=M_{\tau\wedge N}\). Integrability follows from Lemma 10.2 with \(k=N\). Write the telescoping sum
95: \[
96: M_{\tau\wedge N}=M_0+\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\bigl(M_{\tau\wedge(k+1)}-M_{\tau\wedge k}\bigr).
97: \]
98: Each summand has expectation zero, by the proof of Lemma 10.2. Taking expectations of the whole sum gives \(\E[M_\tau]=\E[M_{\tau\wedge N}]=\E[M_0]\).
102: Remark 10.4 (which hypothesis does what)
103: The martingale property enters each summand in the proof through \(\E[\indic_{\{\tau>k\}}(M_{k+1}-M_k)]=0\). The stopping-time property enters through \(\{\tau>k\}\in\mathcal F_k\), which lets the indicator be chosen before the next step. The bound \(\tau\le N\) enters once, to replace \(M_\tau\) by the finite sum \(M_{\tau\wedge N}\). Section 3 removes the bound and shows the conclusion can fail.
106: 2The doubling strategy with finite capital
109: Definition 10.5 (the doubling strategy with capital K)
110: Let \(K\ge 1\) be an integer number of cents, the capital. Each bet is a fair coin: \(\varepsilon_k=+1\) (a win) or \(-1\) (a loss), independent over \(k\), each with probability \(1/2\). Bet \(k\) stakes \(2^{k-1}\) cents. The doubling strategy places bet \(k\) only if no earlier bet has won and the stake \(2^{k-1}\) does not exceed the wealth on hand. Its wealth after the bets placed is \(K\) plus the net gain \(D_\tau\), where \(D_k=\sum_{j\le k}2^{j-1}\varepsilon_j\) and \(\tau\) is the number of bets placed. Ruin means that the strategy stops without a win.
114: Lemma 10.6 (the number of bets)
116: \(K\ge 1\) is an integer number of cents, and \(m\) is the whole number with \(2^m\le K+1
117: The bets \(\varepsilon_k\) are independent fair coins, \(\mathcal F_k=\sigma(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_k)\), and \(D\) and \(\tau\) are as in Definition 10.5.
119: Let \(w\) be the index of the first win, with \(w=\infty\) if no bet wins. Then the number of bets placed is \(\tau=\min(w,m)\). Moreover \(\tau\) is a stopping time and \(\tau\le m\). Assuming Assumption CE, \(D\) is a martingale.
123: Proof
124: Suppose bets \(1,\ldots,k-1\) have all lost. Then the wealth on hand before bet \(k\) is \(K-(2^{k-1}-1)=K+1-2^{k-1}\), since the losses sum to \(2^{k-1}-1\). Bet \(k\) is affordable exactly when \(2^{k-1}\le K+1-2^{k-1}\), that is \(2^k\le K+1\), which holds exactly when \(k\le m\). So bets \(1,\ldots,m\) are placed while no win has occurred, and bet \(m+1\) is not placed after \(m\) losses. If \(w\le m\), bets \(1,\ldots,w\) are placed and the count is \(w=\min(w,m)\). If \(w>m\), bets \(1,\ldots,m\) are placed and all lose, and the count is \(m=\min(w,m)\).
125: The process \(D\) is a martingale: \(D_{k+1}-D_k=2^k\varepsilon_{k+1}\), and for \(A\in\mathcal F_k=\sigma(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_k)\), independence gives \(\E[\indic_A\,2^k\varepsilon_{k+1}]=2^k\Prob(A)\E[\varepsilon_{k+1}]=0\). For \(\tau\): \(\{\tau\le k\}\) is the event that a win occurs among the first \(k\) bets when \(k<m\), and it is the whole space when \(k\ge m\). Either way it depends only on \(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_k\), so it lies in \(\mathcal F_k\). Finally \(\tau\le m\) by the first formula.

138: Proof
139: No win by bet \(m\) means \(\varepsilon_1=\cdots=\varepsilon_m=-1\), an event of probability \(2^{-m}\). If bet \(w\le m\) wins, the losses before it sum to \(2^{w-1}-1\) and the win pays \(2^{w-1}\), so \(D_\tau=-(2^{w-1}-1)+2^{w-1}=1\), and the wealth is \(K+1\). After ruin, \(D_\tau=-(2^m-1)\), and the wealth is \(K+1-2^m\). The expectation is
140: \[
141: (1-2^{-m})(K+1)+2^{-m}(K+1-2^m)=K+1-2^{-m}\cdot 2^m=K.
142: \]
143: Assuming Assumption CE, this agrees with Theorem 10.3: \(D\) is a martingale with \(D_0=0\), and \(\tau\le m\) by Lemma 10.6, so \(\E[D_\tau]=0\) and \(\E[K+D_\tau]=K\).
146: 3The doubling strategy without a capital limit
149: Example 10.8 (no capital, the first win)
150: Let the bets continue with no capital limit, so \(\tau=w\), the index of the first win. Then \(\{\tau>n\}\) is the event that the first \(n\) bets all lose, so \(\Prob(\tau>n)=2^{-n}\). 306: 4Where the canon breaks
308: Unbounded time. Theorem 10.3 needs \(\tau\le N\). Without it, Proposition 10.9 gives \(\E[D_\tau]=1\neq 0=\E[D_0]\). The hypothesis is not a technicality: the example in §3 is the reason it is stated.
309: Finite almost surely is not bounded. Proposition 10.9(a) shows \(\tau
310: Ruin is not zero wealth. In Proposition 10.7 the ruin branch keeps \(K+1-2^m\) cents, which is 0 exactly when \(K+1\) is a power of two (\(K=1, 3, 7, 15,\ldots\)). A low ruin probability with leftover wealth far below \(K\) can still have mean \(K\). The lab's ruin wealths are 0, 3, 3, 37 and 489 for the five capitals.
311: Integer cents. Stakes are integers and the wealths are integers, so the expectation is exact. No approximation enters the proof or the lab.
316: 5Misconceptions
319: “Doubling wins one cent almost surely, so the strategy makes money.”
320: With capital \(K\), the strategy wins with probability \(1-2^{-m}\), which is 0.9980468750 at \(K=1000\). But the ruin branch leaves 489 cents, not 1000, and the expected final wealth is exactly \(K\) (Lab 1, Part A, and Proposition 10.7). A high win rate with a rare large loss has mean equal to the start.
323: “Optional stopping holds for every stopping time of a martingale.”
324: Theorem 10.3 needs \(\tau\le N\) for a fixed \(N\). Example 10.8 gives a stopping time \(\tau\) and a process \(D\) with \(D_0=0\), which is a martingale under Assumption CE (Lemma 10.6), for which \(\E[D_\tau]=1\) (Proposition 10.9). The stopping rule is a legitimate stopping time, and the identity fails because the time is unbounded.
327: “A stopping time that is finite with probability one always satisfies \(\E[M_\tau]=\E[M_0]\).”
328: Proposition 10.9(a) gives \(\Prob(\taun\}\) the loss is \(2^n-1\) cents, and that loss in absolute value, times its probability \(2^{-n}\), is \(1-2^{-n}\), which does not vanish as \(n\) grows.

365: P1 · The stopped increment
366: Prove the identity \(M_{\tau\wedge(k+1)}-M_{\tau\wedge k}=\indic_{\{\tau>k\}}(M_{k+1}-M_k)\) for every stopping time \(\tau\), including \(\tau=\infty\).
367: Solution

372: P2 · Optional stopping with the bound written out
373: In Theorem 10.3, write out the telescoping sum for \(N=2\), and verify that each summand has expectation zero using the martingale property at each step.
374: Solution
375: For \(N=2\), \(M_{\tau}=M_{\tau\wedge 2}=M_0+(M_{\tau\wedge 1}-M_{\tau\wedge 0})+(M_{\tau\wedge 2}-M_{\tau\wedge 1})\). The first summand is \(\indic_{\{\tau>0\}}(M_1-M_0)\), and \(\{\tau>0\}\in\mathcal F_0\). 382: Bets \(1,\ldots,w-1\) lose, with total stake \(\sum_{j<w}2^{j-1}=2^{w-1}-1\), so the net from them is \(-(2^{w-1}-1)\). Bet \(w\) wins and pays its stake \(2^{w-1}\). The net gain is \(-(2^{w-1}-1)+2^{w-1}=1\). The wealth is \(K+1\), since \(K\) plus the net gain is \(K+1\).

415: R1 · The martingale hypothesis cannot be dropped
416: Let \(X_k=k\) for \(k\ge 0\), a deterministic process that is not a martingale, and let \(\tau\equiv 1\). Show that \(\E[X_\tau]\neq\E[X_0]\), so Theorem 10.3 fails without the martingale hypothesis.
417: Solution
418: Here \(X_\tau=X_1=1\), so \(\E[X_\tau]=1\). Also \(X_0=0\), so \(\E[X_0]=0\). The time \(\tau\equiv 1\) is bounded and a stopping time, so the only hypothesis that fails is the martingale property: \(\E[X_{k+1}\mid\mathcal F_k]=k+1\neq k=X_k\).
422: R2 · The truncated expectation by direct computation
423: Without using Theorem 10.3, show that \(\E[D_{\tau\wedge n}]=0\) for each \(n\) in Example 10.8, using \(\Prob(\tau\le n)=1-2^{-n}\).
424: Solution
425: On \(\{\tau\le n\}\), \(D_{\tau\wedge n}=1\), and \(\Prob(\tau\le n)=1-2^{-n}\). On \(\{\tau>n\}\), \(D_{\tau\wedge n}=D_n=-(2^n-1)\), with probability \(2^{-n}\). So \(\E[D_{\tau\wedge n}]=(1-2^{-n})\cdot 1+2^{-n}\cdot(-(2^n-1))=1-2^{-n}-1+2^{-n}=0\).
429: R3 · Where the limit of the expectations fails
430: Using Proposition 10.9, explain why \(\lim_n\E[D_{\tau\wedge n}]\) and \(\E[\lim_n D_{\tau\wedge n}]\) differ, and identify the part of the sum that carries the difference.
431: Solution
432: The first limit is \(\lim_n 0=0\). For almost every outcome, \(D_{\tau\wedge n}\) equals \(1\) once \(n\ge \tau(\omega)\), so \(\lim_n D_{\tau\wedge n}=1\) almost surely and its expectation is 1. The difference is carried by the all-loss branch \(\{\tau>n\}\): its probability \(2^{-n}\) times the size of the loss, \(2^n-1\), equals \(1-2^{-n}\). That product tends to 1, so the losses on that branch do not vanish in expectation, and for each \(n\) they cancel the win part exactly. The limit of the expectations is therefore 0, and the expectation of the limit is 1.
