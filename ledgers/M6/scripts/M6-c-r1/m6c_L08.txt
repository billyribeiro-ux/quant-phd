52: A filtration is the record of what is known at each time, and a martingale is a process whose best forecast of its next value, given that record, is its current value. It is the formal version of a fair game. This lesson defines both on a finite tree. It proves that the fair \(\pm 1\) walk (defined in §2) is a martingale on its natural filtration (Definition 1.2), and that the biased walk is not, because the next step averages \(2p - 1\) given the record, where \(p\) is the up-step probability (§2, Proposition 2.3). Lab 1 checks both exactly on all 1024 paths of a ten-step tree. L09 (stopping times) and L10 (optional stopping) build on these.
54: 1Information over time
56: Let \(\Omega\) be a finite set of paths, with weights \(w(\omega) > 0\) that sum to 1. A process is a finite sequence \((X_0, X_1, \dots, X_N)\) of random variables on \(\Omega\). For a fixed \(k\), the record of the first \(k\) values \((X_0, \dots, X_k)\) partitions \(\Omega\) into cells, one for each possible record. A cell of this partition is an event that is known once the record is known. The partition is the information at time \(k\).
59: Definition 1.1 (filtration)
60: A filtration on \(\Omega\) is a family \(\mathcal F_0 \subseteq \mathcal F_1 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \mathcal F_N\) of sub-\(\sigma\)-algebras of the power set of \(\Omega\). Here each \(\mathcal F_k\) is generated by a partition of \(\Omega\) into cells, as in L07. The filtration is increasing: information is never lost as time passes. A process \((X_k)\) is adapted to the filtration when each \(X_k\) is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable, which means that \(X_k\) is constant on each cell of the partition at time \(k\), so the value of \(X_k\) is known at time \(k\).
64: Definition 1.2 (natural filtration)
65: For a process \((X_k)\), the natural filtration has \(\mathcal F_k\) generated by the record \((X_0, \dots, X_k)\). Its cells at time \(k\) are the sets of paths that share the same record up to time \(k\).
68: 2The walk, its filtration, and martingales
70: Fix \(N \ge 1\). Let \(\Omega\) be the set of paths \(\omega = (x_1, \dots, x_N)\) with each \(x_i \in \{+1, -1\}\), and let the weight of a path be \(\prod_{i} q(x_i)\), where \(q(+1) = p\) and \(q(-1) = 1 - p\) for a fixed \(p \in (0,1)\). Let \(X_i(\omega) = x_i\) be the step, and \(S_0 = 0\), \(S_k = X_1 + \cdots + X_k\). The fair walk has \(p = 1/2\). The biased walk has \(p \ne 1/2\). The walk and its natural filtration \(\mathcal F_k\) are the objects of this lesson. The level-\(k\) cells are the \(2^k\) sets of paths with a common prefix of length \(k\).
73: Proposition 2.1 (the natural filtration of the walk)
74: Assumptions.
76: \(\Omega\), \(p\), the weights and the steps \(X_i\) are as defined above.
77: \(\mathcal F_k\) is the natural filtration of the walk, generated by the prefix of length \(k\).
79: Statement. (a) \(\mathcal F_0\) is the trivial family \(\{\emptyset, \Omega\}\), and \(\mathcal F_N\) contains every subset of \(\Omega\). (b) \(\mathcal F_k \subseteq \mathcal F_{k+1}\). (c) \(S_k\) is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable, so the walk is adapted.
83: Proof
84: (a) The prefix of length 0 is empty, so there is one cell, \(\Omega\). The prefix of length \(N\) is the whole path, so the cells at time \(N\) are single paths, and every subset is a union of them. (b) Each level-\(k\) cell is the disjoint union of the two level-\((k+1)\) cells with the same prefix followed by \(+1\) and \(-1\). So each generator of \(\mathcal F_k\) is in \(\mathcal F_{k+1}\), and hence \(\mathcal F_k \subseteq \mathcal F_{k+1}\). (c) \(S_k\) depends only on the first \(k\) steps, so it is constant on each level-\(k\) cell. By the characterization of L07 (Proposition 1.1), it is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable.
88: Definition 2.2 (martingale)
89: An adapted process \((M_k)_{k=0}^N\) on a filtration \((\mathcal F_k)\) is a martingale when \(\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k] = M_k\) for every \(k = 0, \dots, N-1\). The conditional expectation is the L2 conditional expectation of L07, which on a partition-generated \(\mathcal F_k\) is the cell average of Theorem 2.2 in L07.
93: Proposition 2.2 (the martingale test on cells)
94: Assumptions.
96: \((M_k)\) is adapted to the natural filtration of the walk, as in Proposition 2.1.
97: Each cell at time \(k\) has positive probability.
99: Statement. \((M_k)\) is a martingale if and only if, for every \(k\) and every level-\(k\) cell \(B\), the average \(\E[M_{k+1}\indic_B]/\Prob(B)\) equals the value of \(M_k\) on \(B\).
103: Proof
104: By Theorem 2.2 in L07, \(\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k]\) is the function whose value on each level-\(k\) cell \(B\) is \(\E[M_{k+1}\indic_B]/\Prob(B)\). Since \(M_k\) is adapted, it has a single value on each such cell. Two functions that are constant on each cell are equal if and only if they agree on each cell. So \(\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k] = M_k\) if and only if the cell test holds for every \(B\) at time \(k\).
108: Proposition 2.3 (the walk's increment on each cell)
109: Assumptions.
111: The walk, its weights and its natural filtration are as defined above, with \(p \in (0,1)\).
112: The weight of a path is the product of its step weights \(q(x_i)\).
114: Statement. For every \(k < N\) and every level-\(k\) cell \(B\) with prefix \(h\), the average of \(S_{k+1}\) over \(B\) is \(S_k(h) + (2p - 1)\), where \(S_k(h)\) is the common value of \(S_k\) on \(B\). Hence the walk is a martingale if and only if \(p = 1/2\).
118: Proof
119: Let \(B\) be the cell with prefix \(h = (h_1, \dots, h_k)\). The weight of a path in \(B\) factors as \(\prod_{i \le k} q(h_i)\) times \(\prod_{i > k} q(x_i)\). The sum of the second factor over the tail \((x_{k+1}, \dots, x_N)\) is 1, because \(\sum_{x} q(x) = p + (1 - p) = 1\) at each step. So \(\Prob(B) = \prod_{i \le k} q(h_i)\), and for \(s \in \{+1,-1\}\), \(\Prob(B \cap \{X_{k+1} = s\}) = \Prob(B)\,q(s)\), because the sum over the later steps is again 1. Therefore the average of \(X_{k+1}\) over \(B\) is \(q(+1)\cdot(+1) + q(-1)\cdot(-1) = p - (1 - p) = 2p - 1\). Since \(S_{k+1} = S_k + X_{k+1}\) and \(S_k\) is constant on \(B\), the average of \(S_{k+1}\) over \(B\) is \(S_k(h) + (2p - 1)\). By Proposition 2.2, the walk is a martingale if and only if this equals \(S_k(h)\) on every cell, which holds if and only if \(2p - 1 = 0\), that is \(p = 1/2\). The cell \(B\) with \(k = 0\) has \(\Prob(B) = 1\), so the failure for \(p \ne 1/2\) occurs on a cell of positive probability.
123: Remark 2.4 (what the fair walk satisfies)
124: For \(p = 1/2\), Proposition 2.3 gives a cell average of \(S_k(h)\) on every cell at every level. This is the statement that \(\E[S_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k] = S_k\), which makes the fair walk a martingale on its natural filtration. Lab 1 checks the cell test on all 1023 cells of the ten-step tree.
128: Lab 1 · The fair and biased walks on a ten-step tree
129: The inputs are a fixed finite tree of \(2^{10} = 1024\) paths, not data, and no random draws are used. The walk has steps \(\pm 1\). Part A checks that the natural filtration is nested and that the fair measure has total mass 1. For each walk, Part B checks the cell test of Proposition 2.2 at every level \(k = 0, \dots, 9\) and every cell, with exact fractions, and prints the increment on the first cell. Before running, the expected result is that the fair walk passes every cell at every level, that the biased walk with \(p = 1/3\) fails every cell at every level, with the first cell's increment equal to \(2p - 1 = -1/3\) at each level, and that the expected final value is \(N(2p - 1)\) in each case.
130: """M6 L08 lab 1: the martingale property of a +1/-1 walk on its natural filtration, checked on all 2^10 paths.
132: Inputs are a fixed finite probability space, not data. The walk is S_0 = 0, S_k = X_1 + ... + X_k, where each step
133: X_i is +1 with probability p and -1 with probability 1 - p, independently. Two walks are tested on the same tree:
134: the fair walk with p = 1/2 and a biased walk with p = 1/3. No random draws are used.
136: The filtration F_k is generated by the first k steps. Its cells at level k are the sets of paths that share a
137: prefix of length k, so there are 2^k cells at level k. For each cell A at level k the lab computes the cell average
138: of S_{k+1}, E[S_{k+1} 1_A] / P(A), and compares it with S_k on the same cell. A walk is a martingale on this tree
139: when the two agree on every cell at every level k = 0, ..., 9.
141: Every probability and value is a Fraction, so every comparison is exact.
142: """
144: from fractions import Fraction
145: from itertools import product
147: N = 10
148: FAIR = Fraction(1, 2)
149: BIASED = Fraction(1, 3)
152: def path_probability(path: tuple[int, ...], p: Fraction) -> Fraction:
153: """The probability of a path of +1/-1 steps when each step is +1 with probability p, independently."""
154: out = Fraction(1)
155: for step in path:
156: out *= p if step == 1 else 1 - p
157: return out
160: def partial_sum(path: tuple[int, ...], k: int) -> int:
161: """S_k along a path: the sum of its first k steps, with S_0 = 0."""
162: return sum(path[:k])
165: def level_check(paths: list[tuple[int, ...]], p: Fraction, k: int) -> tuple[int, int, Fraction]:
166: """At level k, count the cells and the paths where the cell average of S_{k+1} differs from S_k.
168: Returns (cells that agree on every path in them, paths whose cell average differs from S_k, the common
169: increment E[S_{k+1} - S_k | A] of the first cell). The increment is printed only as a check of the formula 2p - 1.
170: """
171: cells: dict[tuple[int, ...], list[tuple[int, ...]]] = {}
172: for path in paths:
173: cells.setdefault(path[:k], []).append(path)
174: agreeing = 0
175: mismatched = 0
176: first_increment = Fraction(0)
177: for index, (prefix, members) in enumerate(cells.items()):
178: mass = sum((path_probability(q, p) for q in members), Fraction(0))
179: cell_average = sum((path_probability(q, p) * partial_sum(q, k + 1) for q in members), Fraction(0)) / mass
180: level_value = partial_sum(prefix, k)
181: differing = sum(1 for q in members if cell_average != level_value)
182: mismatched += differing
183: if differing == 0:
184: agreeing += 1
185: if index == 0:
186: first_increment = cell_average - level_value
187: return agreeing, mismatched, first_increment
190: def nested(paths: list[tuple[int, ...]]) -> bool:
191: """Each cell at level k is the union of exactly two cells at level k + 1, so the filtration increases."""
192: for k in range(N):
193: coarse = {path[:k] for path in paths}
194: fine = {path[: k + 1] for path in paths}
195: for prefix in coarse:
196: children = {(*prefix, 1), (*prefix, -1)}
197: if not children <= fine:
198: return False
199: return True
202: def main() -> None:
203: """Print the filtration check, then the level-by-level martingale check for the fair and the biased walk."""
204: paths = list(product((1, -1), repeat=N))
205: print("Part A: the natural filtration is nested, and the fair measure has total mass 1")
206: print(f"paths {len(paths)} steps {N}")
207: print(f"filtration_nested {nested(paths)}")
208: total_mass = sum((path_probability(q, FAIR) for q in paths), Fraction(0))
209: print(f"total_mass_fair {total_mass}")
211: print("Part B: the cell test of Proposition 2.2 at every level, for the fair and the biased walk")
212: for label, p in (("fair", FAIR), ("biased", BIASED)):
213: print(f"Walk {label} with p = {p}")
214: cells_agreeing = 0
215: cells_total = 0
216: mismatched_total = 0
217: for k in range(N):
218: agreeing, mismatched, increment = level_check(paths, p, k)
219: cells_total += 2**k
220: cells_agreeing += agreeing
221: mismatched_total += mismatched
222: print(f"level {k} cells {2**k} agreeing {agreeing} mismatched_paths {mismatched} increment {increment}")
223: print(f"cells_agreeing {cells_agreeing} of {cells_total}")
224: print(f"mismatched_paths_all_levels {mismatched_total}")
225: expected_mean = sum((path_probability(q, p) * partial_sum(q, N) for q in paths), Fraction(0))
226: print(f"expected_S_N {expected_mean} equals 2p-1 times N: {expected_mean == N * (2 * p - 1)}")

267: 3Where the canon breaks
269: Zero mean without the history. A process can have zero expected increment and still fail to be a martingale, because the martingale property is a condition on the conditional mean given the record (Misconception 1). The unconditional mean is a consequence of the martingale property, not its definition.
270: A filtration can hide the future. The natural filtration at time \(k\) reveals only the first \(k\) steps. The cell test is computed from the cells of that filtration, and for the biased walk it fails on every one of the 1023 cells at levels 0 to 9 of the ten-step tree (Lab 1).
271: Bias. The biased walk has increments of conditional mean \(2p - 1\). Proposition 2.3 shows that the average increment on every cell is exactly this constant \(2p - 1\), and it is zero only for \(p = 1/2\).
272: Infinite horizons. The tree here has \(N = 10\) steps. The lesson does not treat martingales with infinitely many steps, where the cell test and the finite sums are no longer the whole argument.
277: 4Misconceptions
280: “A process with zero expected increments is a martingale.”
281: Let \(M_0 = 0\), \(M_1 = X_1\) with \(X_1\) fair, and \(M_2 = 2X_1\), so the second increment \(X_1\) repeats the first. Then \(\E[M_2 - M_1] = \E[X_1] = 0\), but \(\E[M_2 \mid \mathcal F_1] = 2X_1 \ne X_1 = M_1\). The martingale property is a condition on the conditional mean given the record, and Proposition 2.2 tests it cell by cell.
284: “The natural filtration reveals the whole path from time 0.”
285: At time \(k\) the natural filtration reveals only the first \(k\) steps. At time 0 it is the trivial family \(\{\emptyset, \Omega\}\) (Proposition 2.1(a)), so nothing is known about the walk before the first step.
288: “Subtracting the mean path \(k(2p-1)\) from a biased walk gives a martingale only when \(p = 1/2\).”
289: The compensated walk \(M_k = S_k - k(2p - 1)\) is a martingale for every \(p\). On each cell, the average of \(M_{k+1}\) is \(S_k(h) + (2p-1) - (k+1)(2p-1) = S_k(h) - k(2p-1) = M_k(h)\), as Problem P2 shows. The lab does not print this check; the claim rests on Proposition 2.3 and Problem P2.

299: Mechanics
301: M1 · The fair walk after two steps
302: For the fair walk with \(N = 2\), compute \(\E[S_2 \mid \mathcal F_1]\) on the cell where \(X_1 = +1\), and on the cell where \(X_1 = -1\).
303: Solution
304: On the cell \(X_1 = +1\), \(S_1 = 1\), and \(S_2 = 1 + X_2\). With \(p = 1/2\), the cell average of \(X_2\) is \(\tfrac12(+1) + \tfrac12(-1) = 0\). So \(\E[S_2 \mid \mathcal F_1] = 1 = S_1\) there. On the cell \(X_1 = -1\), \(S_1 = -1\) and the same argument gives \(\E[S_2 \mid \mathcal F_1] = -1 = S_1\). Both cells pass the test.
308: M2 · The biased walk's expected final value
309: For the biased walk with \(p = 1/3\) and \(N = 2\), compute \(\E[S_2]\) from the four path weights.
310: Solution
311: The paths \((+,+)\), \((+,-)\), \((-,+)\), \((-,-)\) have weights \(1/9\), \(2/9\), \(2/9\), \(4/9\), and final values \(2\), \(0\), \(0\), \(-2\). So \(\E[S_2] = 2/9 + 0 + 0 - 8/9 = -6/9 = -2/3\). This is \(N(2p - 1) = 2 \cdot (-1/3)\), as Proposition 2.3 predicts through the increments.
315: M3 · The biased walk's cell average
316: For the biased walk with \(p = 1/3\) and \(N = 2\), compute \(\E[S_2 \mid \mathcal F_1]\) on the cell \(X_1 = +1\).
317: Solution
318: On this cell \(S_1 = 1\). Given \(X_1 = +1\), \(X_2 = +1\) has conditional probability \(p = 1/3\), giving \(S_2 = 2\), and \(X_2 = -1\) has probability \(2/3\), giving \(S_2 = 0\). The average is \(\tfrac13 \cdot 2 + \tfrac23 \cdot 0 = 2/3\). This equals \(S_1 + (2p - 1) = 1 - 1/3 = 2/3\), as Proposition 2.3 states, and it is not \(S_1 = 1\).
324: Proof
326: P1 · The cell average of the increment for general p
327: Prove the claim of Proposition 2.3 that the average of \(X_{k+1}\) over a level-\(k\) cell is \(2p - 1\), using only the factorization of path weights.
328: Solution
329: A path in the cell \(B\) with prefix \(h\) and step \(x_{k+1} = s\) has weight \(\prod_{i \le k} q(h_i) \cdot q(s) \cdot \prod_{i \ge k+2} q(x_i)\). Summing over the free steps \(x_{k+2}, \dots, x_N\) gives 1, since \(q(+1) + q(-1) = 1\) at each step. So the total weight of paths in \(B\) with \(X_{k+1} = s\) is \(\Prob(B)\,q(s)\). The average of \(X_{k+1}\) over \(B\) is therefore \(\sum_s s\,q(s) = p - (1-p) = 2p - 1\).
333: P2 · The compensated walk
334: Show that \(M_k = S_k - k(2p-1)\) is a martingale on the natural filtration for every \(p \in (0,1)\).
335: Solution
336: \(M_k\) is adapted, since \(S_k\) is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable and \(k(2p-1)\) is a constant. On a level-\(k\) cell \(B\) with prefix \(h\), the average of \(M_{k+1} = S_{k+1} - (k+1)(2p-1)\) is, by Proposition 2.3, \(S_k(h) + (2p-1) - (k+1)(2p-1) = S_k(h) - k(2p-1) = M_k(h)\). By Proposition 2.2, \(M\) is a martingale.
340: P3 · Constant expectation of a martingale
341: Let \((M_k)\) be a martingale on the natural filtration of the walk. Show that \(\E[M_k] = \E[M_0]\) for every \(k\).
342: Solution
343: The filtration \(\mathcal F_0\) is trivial (Proposition 2.1(a)), so \(\E[M_0 \mid \mathcal F_0] = \E[M_0]\). For each \(k\), \(\E[M_{k+1}] = \E\big[\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k]\big]\) by Proposition 2.4 of L07 with the trivial partition. Since \(M\) is a martingale, \(\E[M_{k+1} \mid \mathcal F_k] = M_k\), so \(\E[M_{k+1}] = \E[M_k]\). Induction on \(k\) gives \(\E[M_k] = \E[M_0]\).

365: E3 · Why 1024 mismatches at each level
366: The biased walk reports 1024 mismatched paths at every level. Explain why the count is 1024 at each level rather than the number of cells.
367: Solution
368: The lab counts mismatched paths, not cells. Each path is in one cell at each level. At each level, every cell fails, and every path in a failing cell is a mismatched path. Since all \(2^{10} = 1024\) paths lie in some cell at each level, each level has 1024 mismatched paths. The total over the ten levels 0 to 9 is 10240.
374: Research
376: R1 · The quadratic martingale
377: For the fair walk, show that \(S_k^2 - k\) is a martingale on the natural filtration.
378: Solution
379: On a level-\(k\) cell with prefix \(h\), \(S_{k+1}^2 = (S_k + X_{k+1})^2 = S_k^2 + 2S_kX_{k+1} + 1\), because \(X_{k+1}^2 = 1\). The cell average of \(X_{k+1}\) is 0 when \(p = 1/2\) (Proposition 2.3), and \(S_k\) is constant on the cell. So the average of \(S_{k+1}^2\) is \(S_k(h)^2 + 1\). Then the average of \(S_{k+1}^2 - (k+1)\) is \(S_k(h)^2 - k\), which is the value of \(S_k^2 - k\) on the cell. Proposition 2.2 gives the martingale property.
383: R2 · The second moment of the fair walk
384: Using R1 and Problem P3, compute \(\E[S_N^2]\) for the fair walk.
385: Solution
386: By R1, \(M_k = S_k^2 - k\) is a martingale. By P3, \(\E[M_N] = \E[M_0]\). Here \(M_0 = S_0^2 - 0 = 0\), so \(\E[S_N^2] - N = 0\), and \(\E[S_N^2] = N\).
390: R3 · The natural filtration of a constant process
391: Let \(C_k = 5\) for all \(k\). Find the natural filtration of \((C_k)\) and show that \((C_k)\) is a martingale on it.
392: Solution
393: The record of a constant process reveals nothing, so every \(\mathcal F_k\) is the trivial family \(\{\emptyset, \Omega\}\). Each \(C_k\) is constant, hence adapted. The conditional expectation of \(C_{k+1} = 5\) on the trivial family is the constant 5, which is \(C_k\). So the constant process is a martingale on its natural filtration.
