A martingale can keep moving forever, yet many martingales settle down. This lesson asks when a martingale has a limit along its paths. The answer proved here, under the orthogonality hypothesis of §1 (Assumption OA, defined there), uses a bound on second moments, which is a stronger hypothesis than the first-moment bound of the general statement (Result 12.6, not proved here). The proof combines the orthogonality of increments with Doob's maximal inequality from L11. The lesson also shows that a limit along paths does not carry the expectation over, so the limit can have a different mean from the start.
1Orthogonality of increments
Let \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\) be a probability space with a filtration \((\mathcal F_n)\). A martingale \(M=(M_n)_{n\ge 0}\) is an adapted process with the identity of L08, Definition 2.2, required for every \(n\ge 0\), not only up to a finite horizon. It is square-integrable when \(\E[M_n^2]<\infty\) for every \(n\). For a square-integrable martingale and \(m\le n\), the martingale property gives \(\E[M_n\mid\mathcal F_m]=M_m\). On a finite probability space, L07 shows that conditional expectation is an orthogonal projection, so the residual \(M_n-M_m\) is orthogonal to every \(\mathcal F_m\)-measurable random variable \(Y\) with \(\E[Y^2]<\infty\), in the sense that \(\E[Y(M_n-M_m)]=0\). This page does not prove that orthogonality on a general probability space. It states it as Assumption OA and marks every result that uses it.
Assumption OA (orthogonality of increments on a general space; not proved here)
Let \(M\) be a square-integrable martingale on a filtration \((\mathcal F_n)\) of a probability space \((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\). For \(0\le m\le n\) and every \(\mathcal F_m\)-measurable \(Y\) with \(\E[Y^2]<\infty\), \(\E[Y(M_n-M_m)]=0\). This is stated, not proved here, and it carries no grade. L07 proves the case of a finite \(\Omega\) only.
Lemma 12.1 (orthogonality of increments)
\((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\) is a probability space, and \((\mathcal F_n)\) is a filtration on it.
\(M\) is a square-integrable martingale on \((\mathcal F_n)\).
\(0\le m\le n\) are whole numbers.
Assumption OA holds (stated, not proved).
Assuming Assumption OA, \(\E[M_m(M_n-M_m)]=0\), \(\E[(M_n-M_m)^2]=\E[M_n^2]-\E[M_m^2]\), and in particular \(\E[M_m^2]\le\E[M_n^2]\).
Proof
The variable \(M_m\) is \(\mathcal F_m\)-measurable and square-integrable, so by Assumption OA with \(Y=M_m\), \(\E[M_m(M_n-M_m)]=0\). Expanding the square of \(M_n=M_m+(M_n-M_m)\),
\[
\E[M_n^2]=\E[M_m^2]+2\,\E[M_m(M_n-M_m)]+\E[(M_n-M_m)^2]=\E[M_m^2]+\E[(M_n-M_m)^2].
\]
Rearranging gives both identities, and the inequality follows because a square is nonnegative.
2A square-integrable martingale converges along almost every path
The main result of this section is the martingale convergence theorem for square-integrable martingales, Theorem 12.4 below. The theorem is conditional on Assumption OA.
Lemma 12.3 (the square of the increments from a fixed time)
\(M\) is a square-integrable martingale on the filtration \((\mathcal F_n)\).
\(m\ge 0\) is a fixed whole number.
Assumption OA holds (stated, not proved).
Assuming Assumption OA, the process \(W_k=(M_k-M_m)^2\), for \(k\ge m\), is a nonnegative submartingale in the sense of Definition 11.1 of L11, with the index running from \(m\).
Proof
Nonnegativity and integrability are clear, since each \(M_k\) is square-integrable. Fix \(k\ge m\) and \(A\in\mathcal F_k\). Put \(R=M_{k+1}-M_k\). Then \(W_{k+1}-W_k=(M_{k+1}-M_m)^2-(M_k-M_m)^2=R^2+2(M_k-M_m)R\). The variable \((M_k-M_m)\indic_A\) is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable and square-integrable, so Assumption OA, applied to the residual \(R\) over one step, gives \(\E[(M_k-M_m)\indic_A\,R]=0\). Hence
\[
\E[W_{k+1}\indic_A]-\E[W_k\indic_A]=\E[R^2\indic_A]\ge 0.
\]
This is the submartingale inequality of Definition 11.1.
Theorem 12.4 (the square-integrable case of martingale convergence)
\((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\) is a probability space, and \((\mathcal F_n)_{n\ge 0}\) is a filtration on it.
\(M=(M_n)_{n\ge 0}\) is a martingale on \((\mathcal F_n)\), and each \(M_n\) is square-integrable.
There is a real number \(L\) with \(\E[M_n^2]\le L\) for every \(n\ge 0\).
Assumption OA holds (stated, not proved).
Assuming Assumption OA, there is an event \(\Omega_0\) with \(\Prob(\Omega_0)=1\) such that the real sequence \(M_n(\omega)\) converges to a finite limit for every \(\omega\in\Omega_0\).
Proof
Let \(v_m=\E[M_m^2]\), and let \(L^\ast=\sup_n v_n\). By Lemma 12.1, \(v_m\) is nondecreasing in \(m\), and \(v_m\le L\) by hypothesis 3, so \(L^\ast\le L\) is finite. A bounded nondecreasing sequence converges to its supremum, so \(v_m\to L^\ast\) as \(m\to\infty\).
Step 1. Fix \(m\) and \(\varepsilon>0\), and let \(N\ge m\). Apply Doob's maximal inequality (Theorem 11.3 of L11) to the nonnegative submartingale \(W_k=(M_k-M_m)^2\), \(m\le k\le N\), after relabelling the times \(k-m\) from 0 to \(N-m\), with \(\lambda=\varepsilon^2\). Since \(\max_{m\le k\le N}W_k\ge\varepsilon^2\) exactly when \(\max_{m\le k\le N}|M_k-M_m|\ge\varepsilon\), and \(\E[W_N]=\E[(M_N-M_m)^2]=v_N-v_m\le L^\ast-v_m\) by Lemma 12.1,
\[
\Prob\Bigl(\max_{m\le k\le N}|M_k-M_m|\ge\varepsilon\Bigr)\le\frac{L^\ast-v_m}{\varepsilon^2}.
\]
Step 2. Let \(D_m(\varepsilon)=\{\exists k\ge m:\ |M_k-M_m|\ge\varepsilon\}\). The events in Step 1 increase with \(N\) and have union \(D_m(\varepsilon)\). By continuity of probability from below (L02), \(\Prob(D_m(\varepsilon))\le(L^\ast-v_m)/\varepsilon^2\).
Step 3. Let \(G\) be the set of outcomes where \((M_n)\) is not a Cauchy sequence. If \(\omega\in G\), there is \(\varepsilon>0\) such that for every \(m\) there are \(k,l\ge m\) with \(|M_k-M_l|\ge\varepsilon\). Then \(|M_k-M_m|+|M_l-M_m|\ge\varepsilon\), so one of the two terms is at least \(\varepsilon/2\), and \(\omega\in D_m(\varepsilon/2)\) for every \(m\). Taking \(\varepsilon=1/j\), \(G\subseteq\bigcup_{j\ge 1}\bigcap_{m}D_m\bigl(\tfrac1{2j}\bigr)\). For each \(j\), \(\Prob\bigl(\bigcap_m D_m(\tfrac1{2j})\bigr)\le\inf_m\,4j^2(L^\ast-v_m)=0\), because \(v_m\to L^\ast\). A countable union of null sets is null, so \(\Prob(G)=0\).
Outside \(G\), the real sequence is Cauchy, and every real Cauchy sequence converges, by the completeness of the reals from M5. Take \(\Omega_0\) to be the complement of \(G\).
Remark 12.5 (what the theorem does not say)
Under Assumption OA, Theorem 12.4 gives the existence of the limit almost surely. The proof does not identify the limit. It also does not say that \(\E[M_\infty]=\E[M_0]\), and the example in §4 shows that this can fail.
Result 12.6 (the general statement, stated and not proved here)
\((\Omega,\mathcal F,\Prob)\) is a probability space, and \((\mathcal F_n)\) is a filtration on it. Not proved here.
\(M=(M_n)_{n\ge 0}\) is a martingale on \((\mathcal F_n)\). Not proved here.
\(\sup_n\E|M_n|<\infty\). Not proved here.
Then \(M_n\) converges almost surely to a finite limit. This result is not proved on this page, and it carries no grade here. Theorem 12.4 covers the case \(\sup_n\E[M_n^2]<\infty\) (assuming Assumption OA), which implies hypothesis 3 by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, \(\E|M_n|\le(\E[M_n^2])^{1/2}\).
3A dyadic martingale
Example 12.7 (the dyadic martingale for Y(x) = x)
On \([0,1]\), take the probability measure that gives an interval its length, and let \(Y(x)=x\). For \(n\ge 0\), the level-\(n\) cells are the intervals \([j/2^n,(j+1)/2^n)\), with the last cell closed at 1. Let \(\mathcal F_n\) be the collection of unions of level-\(n\) cells. Define \(M_n=\E[Y\mid\mathcal F_n]\). On a cell with endpoints \(a<b\) and midpoint \(c=(a+b)/2\), the average of \(Y\) is \(c\), so \(M_n(x)=c\) for \(x\) in the cell. The martingale property holds: the cell splits into two halves with midpoints \(c-h/4\) and \(c+h/4\), where \(h=b-a\). Their average is \(c\), so \(\E[M_{n+1}\indic_{\text{cell}}]=\E[M_n\indic_{\text{cell}}]\) for each cell.
Proposition 12.8 (error and convergence of the dyadic martingale)
\([0,1]\) carries the length measure, \(Y(x)=x\), and \((\mathcal F_n)\) is the dyadic filtration of Example 12.7 (unions of level-\(n\) cells), with \(M_n=\E[Y\mid\mathcal F_n]\).
\(n\ge 0\) is a whole number.
For the martingale of Example 12.7 and each \(n\ge 0\): (a) \(\E[(M_n-Y)^2]=1/(12\cdot 4^n)\); (b) \(\E[M_n^2]=1/3-1/(12\cdot 4^n)\); (c) \(|M_n(x)-x|\le 2^{-(n+1)}\) for every \(x\in[0,1]\), so \(M_n(x)\to x\) for every \(x\).
Proof
(a) On a cell \([a,b]\) of width \(h\) with midpoint \(c\), the exact integral is \(\int_a^b (x-c)^2\,dx=\bigl((b-c)^3-(a-c)^3\bigr)/3\). Since \(b-c=h/2\) and \(a-c=-h/2\), this equals \(\bigl(h^3/8+h^3/8\bigr)/3=h^3/12\). There are \(2^n\) cells with \(h=2^{-n}\), so \(\E[(M_n-Y)^2]=2^n\cdot 2^{-3n}/12=1/(12\cdot 4^n)\).
(b) On each level-\(n\) cell with midpoint \(c\), \(M_n=c\), so \(\E[M_n(Y-M_n)]=\sum_{\text{cells}}c\int_{\text{cell}}(x-c)\,dx\). For a cell \([a,b]\) with midpoint \(c\), \(b-c=h/2\) and \(a-c=-h/2\), so \(\int_a^b(x-c)\,dx=\bigl((b-c)^2-(a-c)^2\bigr)/2=0\). Hence \(\E[M_n(Y-M_n)]=0\). Expanding \(Y^2=\bigl(M_n+(Y-M_n)\bigr)^2\) gives \(\E[Y^2]=\E[M_n^2]+2\,\E[M_n(Y-M_n)]+\E[(Y-M_n)^2]=\E[M_n^2]+\E[(Y-M_n)^2]\). Also \(\E[Y^2]=\int_0^1x^2\,dx=1/3\), and part (a) gives \(\E[(Y-M_n)^2]=1/(12\cdot 4^n)\), so \(\E[M_n^2]=1/3-1/(12\cdot 4^n)\).
(c) If \(x\) lies in a level-\(n\) cell with midpoint \(c\), then \(|x-c|\le h/2=2^{-(n+1)}\). The value \(M_n(x)\) is that midpoint, so \(|M_n(x)-x|\le 2^{-(n+1)}\). This bound tends to 0 as \(n\to\infty\), so \(M_n(x)\to x\) for every \(x\).
By (b), \(\E[M_n^2]\le 1/3\) for every \(n\), so Theorem 12.4 applies, assuming Assumption OA, and the limit exists almost surely. Part (c) identifies the limit as \(Y\) at every point, without Assumption OA. Part (a) is the mean-square error, and it is a statement about an average. The pointwise bound in (c) is a separate fact, and it is what gives convergence on every path.
4A nonnegative martingale without convergence in mean
Example 12.9 (the doubling martingale on all heads)
Let \(H_1,H_2,\ldots\) be independent fair coin flips, with \(\mathcal F_n=\sigma(H_1,\ldots,H_n)\). Let \(M_n=2^n\) if \(H_1=\cdots=H_n=1\) (all heads), and \(M_n=0\) otherwise. Then \(M_n\ge 0\), and \(\E[M_n]=2^n\cdot 2^{-n}=1\) for every \(n\). It is a martingale: on the event that the first \(n\) flips are heads, \(M_{n+1}\) is \(2^{n+1}\) or 0 with probability \(1/2\) each, with mean \(2^n=M_n\). On every other event of \(\mathcal F_n\), \(M_n=M_{n+1}=0\).
Proposition 12.10 (the limit of the doubling martingale)
\(H_1,H_2,\ldots\) are independent fair coin flips, \(\mathcal F_n=\sigma(H_1,\ldots,H_n)\), and \(M_n\) is the martingale of Example 12.9.
For Example 12.9, \(M_n\to 0\) almost surely, \(\E[\lim_n M_n]=0\), and \(\E|M_n-\lim_n M_n|=1\) for every \(n\), so \(M_n\) does not converge to its limit in mean.
Proof
Let \(A_\infty\) be the event that all flips are heads. For each \(n\), \(A_\infty\subseteq\{H_1=\cdots=H_n=1\}\), which has probability \(2^{-n}\). So \(\Prob(A_\infty)\le 2^{-n}\) for every \(n\), and \(\Prob(A_\infty)=0\). Off \(A_\infty\), some flip is tails, say the first at index \(j\), and then \(M_n=0\) for all \(n\ge j\). So \(M_n\to 0\) almost surely, and the limit is 0 and has mean 0. For the last claim, \(\E|M_n-0|=\E[M_n]=1\) for every \(n\), since \(M_n\ge 0\).

It predicts \(1/(12\cdot 4^n)\), the second moment \(1/3-1/(12\cdot 4^n)\), and the largest error \(|M_n(x)-x|\) over the grid \(j/4096\) to be \(2^{-(n+1)}\). Part B enumerates the coin sequences of length \(n\le 10\) for the martingale of Example 12.9, checks the one-step martingale identity on every atom of the first-\(n\)-flips partition (an atom is the set of paths that share their first \(n\) flips) for \(n\) from 0 to 9, and prints \(\E[M_n]\) and the probability that \(M_n\) is not zero. It predicts \(\E[M_n]=1\) and \(2^{-n}\) for that probability.
"""M6 L12 lab 1: dyadic martingales and a nonnegative martingale without L1 convergence.
Part A, the dyadic martingale. On [0, 1] with the length measure, let Y(x) = x. For n >= 0 the level-n cells are the
half-open intervals [j/2**n, (j+1)/2**n), with the last cell closed at 1. M_n is Y averaged over the level-n cell
that contains x, so M_n(x) is the midpoint c of that cell. The lab computes every integral exactly with
fractions.Fraction. The exact integral of (x - c)**2 over a cell [a, b] is ((b - c)**3 - (a - c)**3) / 3. It checks:
E[(M_n - Y)**2] = 1/(12 * 4**n) for n = 0, ..., 10; E[M_n**2] = 1/3 - 1/(12 * 4**n); E[Y**2] = 1/3; and the largest
value of |M_n(x) - x| over the grid j/4096, j = 0, ..., 4096, which is 1/2**(n+1) for each n.
Part B, a nonnegative martingale. A fair coin gives flips 1 (heads) or 0 (tails). M_n = 2**n when the first n flips
are all heads and M_n = 0 otherwise. The lab enumerates all coin sequences of length n <= 10 and checks the one-step
martingale identity on every atom of the first-n-flips partition, prints E[M_n] = 1 and P(M_n not equal to 0) = 2**(-n).
Every quantity is an exact Fraction. The lab uses no random draws, so every run prints the same lines.
"""
from __future__ import annotations
import itertools
from fractions import Fraction
MAX_LEVEL = 10
GRID_SIZE = 4096
def cell_index(x: Fraction, level: int) -> int:
"""The index j of the level-n cell [j/2**n, (j+1)/2**n) that contains x, with the last cell closed at 1."""
cells: int = 2**level
index: int = int(x * cells)
return min(index, cells - 1)
def cell_midpoint(index: int, level: int) -> Fraction:
"""The midpoint c = (j + 1/2) / 2**n of the level-n cell with index j."""
return Fraction(2 * index + 1, 2 ** (level + 1))
def part_a() -> None:
"""Exact moments and errors of the dyadic martingale M_n = E[Y | F_n] for Y(x) = x, for n from 0 to 10."""
print("Part A: dyadic martingale M_n = E[Y | F_n] for Y(x) = x on [0, 1]")
expected_y = Fraction(0)
expected_y2 = Fraction(0)
for j in range(2**MAX_LEVEL):
a, b = Fraction(j, 2**MAX_LEVEL), Fraction(j + 1, 2**MAX_LEVEL)
expected_y += (b * b - a * a) / 2
expected_y2 += (b**3 - a**3) / 3
print(f"E_Y {expected_y} E_Y_squared {expected_y2} E_Y_squared_is_one_third {expected_y2 == Fraction(1, 3)}")
grid = [Fraction(j, GRID_SIZE) for j in range(GRID_SIZE + 1)]
for level in range(MAX_LEVEL + 1):
cells = 2**level
error = Fraction(0)
second_moment = Fraction(0)
for j in range(cells):
a, b = Fraction(j, cells), Fraction(j + 1, cells)
c = cell_midpoint(j, level)
error += ((b - c) ** 3 - (a - c) ** 3) / 3
second_moment += c * c * (b - a)
worst = max(abs(cell_midpoint(cell_index(x, level), level) - x) for x in grid)
print(
f"n {level} E_error_squared {error} formula_matches {error == Fraction(1, 12 * 4**level)} "
f"E_M_squared_matches_one_third_minus {second_moment == Fraction(1, 3) - Fraction(1, 12 * 4**level)} "
f"largest_grid_error {worst} equals_half_over_2_to_n {worst == Fraction(1, 2 ** (level + 1))}"
)
def coin_value(flips: tuple[int, ...], level: int) -> int:
"""M_level for one coin sequence: 2**level if the first level flips are all heads, otherwise 0."""
return 2**level if all(flips[:level]) else 0
def part_b() -> None:
"""A nonnegative martingale: E[M_n] = 1 for every n, yet M_n is 0 except on the all-heads path."""
print("Part B: nonnegative martingale M_n = 2**n on all heads, 0 otherwise")
every_step_holds = True
for level in range(MAX_LEVEL):
for prefix in itertools.product((0, 1), repeat=level):
extensions = [(*prefix, flip) for flip in (0, 1)]
average_next = Fraction(sum(coin_value(e, level + 1) for e in extensions), 2)
every_step_holds = every_step_holds and average_next == coin_value(prefix, level)
print(f"one_step_martingale_on_every_atom {every_step_holds}")
for level in range(MAX_LEVEL + 1):
sample = list(itertools.product((0, 1), repeat=level))
expected = sum((Fraction(coin_value(s, level), 2**level) for s in sample), Fraction(0))
nonzero = sum(1 for s in sample if coin_value(s, level) != 0)
print(
f"n {level} E_M_n {expected} P_M_n_nonzero {Fraction(nonzero, 2**level)} "
f"is_two_to_minus_n {Fraction(nonzero, 2**level) == Fraction(1, 2**level)}"
)
def main() -> None:
"""Print Part A and Part B."""
part_a()
part_b()
main()

5Where the canon breaks
The second-moment bound. Theorem 12.4 needs \(\sup_n\E[M_n^2]<\infty\) and Assumption OA. Result 12.6 gives a.s. convergence under the weaker first-moment bound, but that statement is not proved on this page, so the proof here does not cover it.
Almost sure is not in mean. Example 12.9 has \(\E|M_n|=1\) for every \(n\) and \(M_n\to 0\) almost surely. The convergence is not in mean, so the expectation of the limit is 0 while \(\E[M_n]=1\).
Every path versus almost every path. Proposition 12.8(c) gives convergence at every \(x\). Theorem 12.4 gives convergence only off a null set. The proof identifies the exceptional set \(G\) only as the set where the sequence is not Cauchy, and does not describe it further.
Finite checks. Lab 1 checks levels 0 to 10 and a grid of 4097 points. The statements for all levels and all points are proved above, not checked by the lab.
6Misconceptions
“A martingale that converges along almost every path has the same expectation in the limit as at the start.”
Example 12.9 has \(\E[M_0]=1\), since \(M_0=1\), and \(M_n\to 0\) almost surely, so \(\E[\lim_n M_n]=0\). Proposition 12.10 proves both facts. The expectation of the limit is not the limit of the expectations.
“If the mean-square error of the dyadic martingale tends to zero, then every path converges to \(Y\).”
The mean-square error is an average over \(x\). It does not, by itself, control each path. Proposition 12.8(c) gives the separate pointwise bound \(|M_n(x)-x|\le 2^{-(n+1)}\), and that bound is what gives convergence at each \(x\). Part A of Lab 1 prints the largest grid error \(2^{-(n+1)}\) at each level.
“The martingale convergence theorem says that the sequence converges to its expectation.”
The limit is a random variable, not a number. In Example 12.7 the limit is \(Y(x)=x\), which is not the constant \(\E[Y]=1/2\). The theorem gives the existence of a limit, not its value.

Mechanics
M1 · The dyadic error at level 1
For the dyadic martingale at level \(n=1\), find \(M_1\) on each half of \([0,1]\), and compute \(\E[(M_1-Y)^2]\) by hand.
Solution
On \([0,\tfrac12)\) the midpoint is \(\tfrac14\), and on \([\tfrac12,1]\) it is \(\tfrac34\). On the first half, \(\int_0^{1/2}(x-\tfrac14)^2\,dx=\bigl((\tfrac14)^3-(-\tfrac14)^3\bigr)/3=\tfrac{1}{96}\). The second half gives the same value by symmetry. The total is \(2\cdot\tfrac1{96}=\tfrac1{48}\), which is \(1/(12\cdot 4)\), as Proposition 12.8(a) states and as Part A of Lab 1 prints for \(n=1\).
M2 · The second moment at level 2
For \(n=2\), the cell midpoints are \(\tfrac18,\tfrac38,\tfrac58,\tfrac78\), each cell of length \(\tfrac14\). Compute \(\E[M_2^2]\) and compare it with \(1/3-1/(12\cdot 16)\).
Solution
The sum of the squared midpoints is \((1+9+25+49)/64=84/64\). Each cell has probability \(1/4\), so \(\E[M_2^2]=\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac{84}{64}=\tfrac{84}{256}=\tfrac{21}{64}\). The formula gives \(1/3-1/192=64/192-1/192=63/192=21/64\), which agrees.
M3 · The all-heads martingale at level 3
For Example 12.9, find \(\Prob(M_3\ne 0)\) and \(\E[M_3]\), and check that the contribution of the all-heads event to \(\E[M_3]\) is 1.
Solution
\(M_3\ne 0\) exactly when the first three flips are heads, which has probability \(1/8\). On that event \(M_3=8\), so the contribution to the mean is \(8\cdot\tfrac18=1\). Off that event \(M_3=0\), so \(\E[M_3]=1\). Part B of Lab 1 prints \(\E[M_3]=1\) and the non-zero probability \(1/8\) at \(n=3\).
Proof
P1 · The square of a martingale is a submartingale
Assuming Assumption OA, prove Lemma 12.3 in full, showing where the orthogonality of the residual is used and why the cross term vanishes.
Solution
Let \(R=M_{k+1}-M_k\) and \(Z=(M_k-M_m)\indic_A\). Then \(Z\) is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable because \(M_k-M_m\) is, and \(A\in\mathcal F_k\). Also \(\E[Z^2]<\infty\) by square-integrability. The residual \(R\) satisfies \(\E[ZR]=0\) by Assumption OA applied to \(Z\) (which is \(\mathcal F_k\)-measurable and square-integrable) and \(R\), which is the one-step orthogonality. The algebraic identity \(W_{k+1}-W_k=R^2+2(M_k-M_m)R\) then gives \(\E[(W_{k+1}-W_k)\indic_A]=\E[R^2\indic_A]+2\E[ZR]=\E[R^2\indic_A]\ge 0\).
P2 · The dyadic cell martingale identity
Show that for each level-\(n\) cell with midpoint \(c\) and length \(h\), the two children have midpoints \(c\pm h/4\), and deduce \(\E[M_{n+1}\indic_{\text{cell}}]=\E[M_n\indic_{\text{cell}}]\).
Solution
The cell \([a,b]\) with \(b-a=h\) splits at \(c=(a+b)/2\) into \([a,c)\) and \([c,b]\). Their midpoints are \((a+c)/2=c-h/4\) and \((c+b)/2=c+h/4\), and each child has length \(h/2\). On the cell, \(M_{n+1}\) takes these two values, each on half the cell, so \(\E[M_{n+1}\indic_{\text{cell}}]=(c-h/4)(h/2)+(c+h/4)(h/2)=ch\). Also \(\E[M_n\indic_{\text{cell}}]=c\cdot h\). The two are equal.
P3 · The countable union in Step 3
Show that if \(\omega\) is not in \(\bigcup_j\bigcap_m D_m(1/(2j))\), then \((M_n(\omega))\) is Cauchy, and explain why this proves \(\Prob(G)=0\).
Solution
Suppose \(\omega\) is not in the union. Then for each \(j\) there is some \(m_j\) with \(\omega\notin D_{m_j}(1/(2j))\), so \(|M_k-M_{m_j}|<1/(2j)\) for all \(k\ge m_j\). For \(k,l\ge m_j\), \(|M_k-M_l|<1/j\), so the sequence is Cauchy at scale \(1/j\) beyond \(m_j\), for every \(j\). This is the Cauchy property, and \(\omega\notin G\). The union of the sets \(\bigcap_m D_m(1/(2j))\) contains \(G\), and each set in the union has probability zero, so \(\Prob(G)\le\sum_j 0=0\).

E2 · The largest grid error at level 10
Read the largest grid error at \(n=10\) in Part A. Compare it with \(2^{-(n+1)}\), and say why the grid attains it.
Solution
The printed value is \(1/2048\), which is \(2^{-11}\). The grid \(j/4096\) contains the cell endpoints \(j/2^{10}\), since \(4096/1024=4\). At an endpoint \(a\) of a level-10 cell, the midpoint is \(a+2^{-11}\), so the error is \(2^{-11}\). So the grid attains the bound from Proposition 12.8(c).
E3 · The mean of the all-heads martingale
Read Part B of Lab 1 at \(n=3\). The printed probability that \(M_3\ne 0\) is \(1/8\), and the mean is 1. Show how these two printed values fit together.
Solution
On the event \(\{M_3\ne 0\}\) the value is \(2^3=8\), so the contribution to the mean is \(8\times\tfrac18=1\). Off the event, \(M_3=0\). So the printed mean 1 equals the value 8 times the printed probability 1/8, which is consistent with the lab's output.
Research
R1 · The pointwise bound has no slack
Show that for each \(n\), the bound \(|M_n(x)-x|\le 2^{-(n+1)}\) is attained at a cell endpoint, and deduce that no smaller constant works for every \(x\).
Solution
Take the left endpoint \(x=a=j/2^n\) of a level-\(n\) cell. Its midpoint is \(c=a+2^{-(n+1)}\), so \(|M_n(a)-a|=2^{-(n+1)}\). A constant \(c_0<2^{-(n+1)}\) would fail at this point. So the bound is attained, and no smaller uniform constant works for every \(x\).
R2 · A biased coin version
Let the heads probability be \(p\in(0,1)\), and \(M_n=p^{-n}\indic\{H_1=\cdots=H_n=1\}\). Show that \(M\) is a nonnegative martingale with \(\E[M_n]=1\), and that \(M_n\to 0\) almost surely.
Solution
On the event that the first \(n\) flips are heads, \(M_{n+1}\) equals \(p^{-(n+1)}\) with probability \(p\) and 0 otherwise, so its conditional mean is \(p^{-(n+1)}\cdot p=p^{-n}=M_n\). Off that event both are zero. Also \(\E[M_n]=p^{-n}\cdot p^n=1\). The all-heads event has probability at most \(p^n\) for every \(n\), and \(p^n\to 0\) because \(p<1\), so it has probability zero, and \(M_n\to 0\) almost surely as in Proposition 12.10.
R3 · Where the second moment enters
Identify the step of Theorem 12.4 that uses the second moment rather than the first, and say what breaks if only \(\sup_n\E|M_n|<\infty\) is assumed.
Solution
Assuming Assumption OA, the second moment enters Lemma 12.1, which gives \(\E[(M_n-M_m)^2]=\E[M_n^2]-\E[M_m^2]\) through the orthogonality of increments. That identity is what makes \(v_m\) converge and makes the Doob bound in Step 1 small. With only a first-moment bound, the increments need not be orthogonal in this sense, and Lemma 12.3 does not apply. The general first-moment statement in Result 12.6 is stated on this page without proof.
